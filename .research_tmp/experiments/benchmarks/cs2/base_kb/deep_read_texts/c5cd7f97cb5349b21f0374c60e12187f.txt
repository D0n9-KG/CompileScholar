# AutoProc: An automatic proactive replication scheme for P2P storage

YANG Zhi & DAI YaFei*

Computer Science Department, Peking University, Beijing 100871, China

Received September 8, 2010; accepted March 8, 2011

Abstract Due to the prevalence of peer departures, P2P storage systems need to constantly generate replicas for high durability. However, few existing systems are able to select the right replication rate that balances replication cost and durability, e.g., minimizing replication cost without threatening the durability target. In this paper, we propose a method called AutoProc to automate the replication process in P2P storage systems. AutoProc employs two key techniques to enable cost-effective replication: correctly adapting replication rate with a reliability model, and replicating objects at the right order with a reliability indicator. Extensive simulations based on the real system failure trace show that AutoProc achieves the near-optimal performance.

Keywords P2P storage, churn, durability, fault tolerance, proactive replication, resource usage

Citation Yang Z, Dai Y F. AutoProc: An automatic proactive replication scheme for P2P storage. Sci China Inf Sci, 2011, 54: 1151-1160, doi: 10.1007/s11432-011-4260-5

# 1 Introduction

Peer-to-peer (P2P) storage networks aim at aggregating the storage in today's resource-abundant computers to form a large, shared storage space at a much lower price [1, 2]. Due to the prevalence of peer dynamics (i.e., churn), a fundamental issue in P2P storage systems is how to tolerate failures and departures of peers [3, 4]. To meet the service level agreements (SLA), a system must provide a certain level of durability for the data stored in it (notice that recent studies [2, 5] recognize that durability is a more practical goal than availability in highly dynamic environments).

To do so, a system must constantly regenerate new replicas to compensate for lost ones due to peer failures [5-7]. However, replication also incurs a heavy cost on storage systems since replica generation consumes a large amount of bandwidth [3, 4]. Clearly, high bandwidth cost can congest the network and significantly reduce system throughput, while reducing the number of replicas generated might result in an unacceptable level of durability.

Therefore, the challenge in building today's storage systems is to carefully navigate this durability-cost tradeoff, by reducing replication cost as much as possible while maintaining the desired level of durability.

Most existing systems [1, 5, 6, 8, 9] attempt to navigate durability-cost tradeoff with reactive replication method, e.g., the system monitors the failures of replicas and replaces lost ones on other peers in reaction to failures. However, this method is complicated by the fact that peer failure can be either transient or permanent. A peer suffering from a transient failure can eventually rejoin the network, bringing back its

Therefore, it is desirable that an efficient reactive system would reliably distinguish between peers that are temporarily down and those that have permanently failed, and only pay the costs of replication generation following permanent failures. Unfortunately, distinguishing permanent and transient failures turns out to be a daunting task, because peers are unresponsive in both cases [4, 5, 7, 10].

Proactive replication [11] can avoid the difficult problem of distinguishing failures by replicating data at a constant rate. But choosing the replication rate walks a fine line between aggressive replication that incurs unnecessary replica generation, and lazy replication that reduces data durability. Thus, how to select replication to navigate cost-durability tradeoffs remains unanswered. In addition, objects should be replicated in a suitable order.

It makes sense to give priority to files with fewer remaining replicas, since they are more likely to be destroyed by unpredictable future failures. However, this problem is complicated by the fact that we have no reliable way to know whether a peer departure at a given timestamp is transient or permanent. Incorrect replication order can increase data loss since many objects cannot be properly replicated.

# 2 Related work

The effectiveness of providing durable storage in real, highly dynamic P2P systems has seldom been studied, probably for lack of practical analysis environments. The traces used in previous studies [3, 6] only last a few days. In contrast, Maze [12] has been monitored for long periods of time, and thus provides an opportunity to explore the highly dynamic P2P storage space.

Many storage systems replicate data for durability [2, 7, 8]. A common way, known as reactive replication, is to detect node failures and make response by regenerating new replicas. In [5], Chun et al. analyzed the effectiveness of preserving durability under the context of reactive recovery. However, this method has two main weaknesses: First, it can create dramatic spikes in network use after a failure, which could seriously annoy users. Moreover, it is difficult to distinguish between permanent failures and transient failures, which significantly increases the complexity and limits the efficiency of reactive method [10, 13].

An alternative scheme of preserving durability is proactive recovery [11]. This approach constantly generates replicas with a constant replication rate, hence smoothing bandwidth usage and avoiding the complexity of distinguishing failures. But an important issue needed to be addressed is how to determine

The models shown in [14, 15] cannot address this tradeoff because the relationship between the average number of replicas and durability is unclear. In contrast, AutoProc can navigate this tradeoff based on a new durability model and controls the replication rate adaptively.

Another design issue in proactive method is how to decide which object should be repaired with high priority, since it may take a long time to produce a new replica with a fixed low replication rate. Intuitively, it makes sense to give priority to files with fewer remaining replicas (i.e., replicas residing on online peers or peers experiencing transient failures), because they are more likely to be destroyed by future failures. However, evaluating the exact rarity of objects turns out to be a major challenge, since failures can be either transient or permanent.

Tempo [11] uses the number of online replicas to decide the replication priority. However, this method overlooks the fact that many departed peers can rejoin the network and bring back their stored replicas, thus only providing coarse-grained ordering of the replicas. In this paper, we present a carefully tuned priority assignment policy. Notice that this method can be used in both P2P storage and globally distributed storage systems where transient failures are also frequent [16].

# 3 AutoProc design

In this section, we introduce the framework of AutoProc and show its two fine-grained components: a rate cost controller and a priority assigner, which dynamically decide the replication rate and priority. We also describe how AutoProc obtains required parameters from the historical failure trace of the system.

# 3.1 AutoProc overview

AutoProc implements a proactive recovery algorithm that constantly generates replication with a given replication rate. It has several fine-grained components, as shown in Figure 1.

AutoProc works as follows: First, the monitor periodically collects peer failure information, based on which the estimator obtains the parameters needed by controller and assigner, including peer permanent failure rate, historical failure distribution and current downtime. Next, the controller takes as inputs the durability requirements specified by users and the permanent failure rate, and outputs the replication rate $\gamma$ (details will be given later in subsection 3.2).

On the other hand, the assigner takes as input peer historical failure distribution and current downtime and outputs the replication priority for each data object (details will be given later in subsection 3.3). With the output of AutoProc, the replication process can decide the rate and the order of replication, making a good balance between cost and durability.

In this paper, we focus on the replication algorithms. In actual implementations, AutoProc can be implemented either at a master node in a centrally controlled storage system such as the Google file

# 3.2 Self-tuned rate control

The design of rate control requires a model to make balance between cost and durability. To make our analysis both practical and tractable, we assume that permanent failures occur randomly with rate $\lambda$ and the system constantly recreates a replica with a fixed rate $\gamma$ (we will discuss more practical environment in the later part of this subsection). Rate $\lambda$ is a property of how often users permanently leave the system; rate $\gamma$ depends on the amount of data stored (system workload), as well as the available repair bandwidth (peer cost).

Making the above assumption allows us to model the number of remaining replicas of an object as a birth-death process $n(t)$ using a continuous time Markov chain. Each state corresponds to the number of remaining replicas of the object. From a given state $i$ ( $i > 0$ ), there is a transition to state $i + 1$ with a constant rate $\gamma$ , and a transition to next lower state $i - 1$ with a rate $i\lambda$ . State 0 corresponds to the loss of durability. Based on this system model, we derive closed-form approximation for the durability:

Proposition 1. The probability $p_L$ of losing a data object over a retention period $t_f$ can be approximated by

$$
p _ {L} \approx \frac {\gamma t _ {f}}{\exp \left(\frac {\gamma}{\lambda}\right) - 1}. \tag {1}
$$

Proof. Clearly, the lifetime $T$ of an object can be formalized as the first hitting time of process $n(t)$ on level 0. According to Aldous et al. [18], the lifetime $T$ of the object asymptotically behaves as an exponential random variable with mean $E[T]$ . To estimate $E[T]$ , we make the following two simplifications:

First, we allow for transitions out of state 0. This change turns the model into an $M / M / \infty$ queue. As shown in Figure 2, the "arrival rate" is the repair rate and the "service rate" is the peer failure rate. Hence, the probability of finding $n(t)$ in state $i$ at any random time $t \gg 0$ follows a Poisson distribution with mean $E[n]$ [19]. Using little's law [18] we can derive $E[n] = \frac{\gamma}{\lambda}$ . As a result, we get the probability of finding all replicas are lost at any random time $t \gg 0$ :

$$
P (n (t) = 0) = \exp \left(- \frac {\gamma}{\lambda}\right). \tag {2}
$$

Second, we view $n(t)$ as an alternating on/off process, where each on period corresponds to $n(t) > 0$ and each off period corresponds to $n(t) = 0$ . Let $T_{j}$ be the time that $n(t)$ stays in its $j$ th on state and $U_{j}$ represents the time in the $j$ th off state, as shown in Figure 3. We approximate process $n(t)$ as regenerative, and assume that when $n(t)$ makes a transition from 0 to 1, points $j$ are regenerative points. Then, applying the theorem of regenerative process [20] to $n(t)$ , the probability of finding $n(t)$ in the dead state at any random time $t \gg 0$ is given by

$$
P (n (t) = 0) = \frac {E [ U _ {j} ]}{E [ T _ {j} ] + E [ U _ {j} ]}, \tag {3}
$$

where $E[U_j] = \frac{1}{\gamma}$ . Combining the last two equations and solving $E[T_j]$ , we get

$$
\ln \left(1 - p _ {L}\right) = \frac {- \gamma t _ {f}}{\exp \left(\frac {\gamma}{\lambda}\right) - 1}. \tag {4}
$$

Since users usually require very small $p_L$ in practice, we can write $\ln (1 - p_L) \approx p_L$ , which leads to (1).

It is certain that the actual system can be much more complicated. Objects can have a large number of initial replicas, and peers may have sufficiently heavy-tailed lifetime distributions (e.g., Pareto, lognormal, Weibull, Cauchy). However, deriving a more accurate approximation would probably be too complicated to be of practical value. Instead, model (1) allows at least a conservative estimate of durability, which ensures the service guarantee and still turns the system toward better performance.

Based on model (1), AutoProc selects the replication rate threshold $\gamma_{th}$ that attains the durability $p_f$ (given the retention period $t_f$ ) specified by users. From eq. (1), we can easily derive that $\gamma_{th}$ should satisfy the following non-linear equation:

$$
\exp \left(\frac {\gamma_ {t h}}{\lambda}\right) - \frac {t _ {f} \gamma_ {t h}}{1 - p _ {f}} - 1 = 0. \tag {5}
$$

Eq. (5) has exactly one positive root. The system can easily derive an approximate solution for $\gamma_{th}$ using existing methods (such as the bisection method). This solution allows the system to deliver user-specified durability while maximizing efficiency.

# 3.3 Sophisticated priority assignment

The design goal of priority assigner is to put low durable objects at high repair priority. To do this, AutoProc evaluates the reliability of objects with an replication-level indicator, because objects with fewer remaining replicas are more easily to be destroyed by future failures.

In general, we can think of a priority assignment policy as a monotonically decreasing function $P(r)$ , where $r$ is an indicator of the remaining replica number. $r$ should be larger for those objects that tend to have more remaining replicas, but notably, it does not need to be equal to the exact value of the replica number. In fact, unreliable failure distinction complicates the attempt to get the actual value of replica number [11].

To create a reliable indicator, we take into account both prior and current failure information. More formally, suppose that we know the cumulative distribution $P(l \leqslant x)$ of the duration $l$ of transient failures. Then, for a peer that has already been offline for $d$ units of time, we use the likelihood ratio $\Lambda(d) = \frac{L_1(d)}{L_2(d)}$ to measure the likelihood that this is a transient failure. $L_1(d)$ and $L_2(d)$ indicate the probability that the failure duration is larger than $d$ , in the case of transient failure and permanent failure, respectively.

Clearly, a larger likelihood ratio $\Lambda$ implies a higher chance that a departed peer can rejoin, so the replica stored on it is more reliable. It is easy to get $L_1(d) = P(l > d)$ and $L_2(d) = 1$ since the duration of a permanent failure is infinite. Thus, we can reformulate $\Lambda$ as

$$
\Lambda (d) = 1 - P (l \leqslant d). \tag {6}
$$

Specially, $\Lambda(0) = 1$ since $P(l \leqslant 0) = 0$ . This means that if a peer is online, it cannot be a permanent failure. We see that likelihood ratio $\Lambda(d)$ provides us a way to exploit the subtle difference among failures.

Now, suppose that there are $n$ peers hosting replicas of an object. Let the $n$ peers be numbered from 1, 2, ... to peer $n$ . Let $d_{i}$ be the downtime of peer $i$ , and $d_{i} = 0$ if peer $i$ is online at that time. Then we aggregate the likelihood ratios of these peers as a measure of this object's remaining replica number, and obtain the indicator:

$$
r = \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n} \Lambda \left(d _ {i}\right). \tag {7}
$$

The intuition behind the use of such an indicator can be justified as follows. Consider any two objects $O_{1}$ and $O_{2}$ , whose replicas are hosted by $n_{1}$ and $n_{2}$ peers respectively ( $n_{1} \leqslant n_{2}$ ). We can rewrite their indicator values as

$$
r _ {1} = n _ {2} \left[ \frac {1}{n _ {2}} \left(\sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n _ {1}} \Lambda \left(d _ {i}\right) + \sum_ {i = n _ {1} + 1} ^ {n _ {2}} 0\right) \right], r _ {2} = n _ {2} \left[ \frac {1}{n _ {2}} \sum_ {i = 1} ^ {n _ {2}} \Lambda \left(d _ {i}\right) \right]. \tag {8}
$$

We see that $r$ essentially reflects the average likelihood ratio by assuming that the replicas of objects are hosted by the same number of peers, so a larger $r$ indicates that an object tends to have more remaining replicas and thus higher durability.

While the basic method considers that all peers have homogeneous failure behaviors, it can easily incorporate heterogeneous behaviors. To do so, the system can monitor the distribution of failure duration $l_{i}$ for an individual peer $i$ , i.e., $P(l_{i} \leqslant x)$ , and get the peer-specific $\Lambda_{i}$ :

$$
\Lambda_ {i} (d) = 1 - P \left(l _ {i} \leqslant d\right).
$$

However, this may incur a large boost delay for recognizing online history of individual peers. Hence, we still use the basic method below to show its conservative performance.

# 3.4 Parameter estimator

To apply AutoProc to a particular system, we need an estimator to determine system failure parameters based on system statistics. The primary parameter is the permanent failure rate $\lambda$ , which is the ratio of peers to fail per unit time. Notice that peer lifetime is modeled as an exponential distribution, so the probability for any peer to fail by $t$ time units is $P(t) = 1 - \exp (t)$ . Thus we have

$$
\lambda = \frac {- \ln (1 - P (t))}{t}. \tag {10}
$$

Suppose that the system observes $K$ permanent failures with $M$ known peers during $t$ time units. Then, the above probability $P(t)$ can be estimated as $\frac{K}{M}$ . When $P(t)$ is sufficiently small, $\lambda$ can also be estimated as $\frac{K}{Mt}$ . The parameters needed by priority assigner, such as peer downtime and failure distribution, can be directly collected by the monitor.

# 4 Evaluation

In this section, we use the real-life trace of Maze to evaluate the effectiveness of AutoProc. We begin by describing the evaluation setting, including trace characteristics, file workload and system setup. Then, we present simulation results.

# 4.1 Evaluation setting

In this section, we use actual system traces collected from a peer-to-peer file-sharing system to evaluate the performance of the AutoProc. The peer-to-peer system that we use is Maze [12], which we now briefly explain. With an average of $20\mathrm{K}$ simultaneously online nodes, Maze is one of the largest distributed systems on CERNET (China Education and Research Network). Maze can be a representative of highly dynamic distributed system since its dynamic level is close to Overnet. We choose it as our experiment environment mainly because it is a large, distributed collection of hosts that has been monitored for long periods of time. We construct the environment with the system log from 25/12/2008 to 27/3/2009, in which period we see fewer crashes of the log server.

To exclude some extremely unstable peers $^{1)}$ , our system only uses peers that have appeared at least two times. The rates of transient and permanent failures are shown in Figure 4. The average permanent failure rate is 0.016 peer failures per day, corresponding to a 62-day peer lifetime. In addition, the average peer availability in Maze is only about 0.3. Figure 5 further shows the distribution of peer lifetime in the Maze system, which indicates that an exponential distribution is a reasonable fit for peer lifetime.

We employ the real file workload collected from Maze, which contains both small and large files (Figure 6). Since each user contributes a small amount of storage, large files should be divided into small chunks so that they can be inserted into the system. For our workload, files whose sizes are larger than $64\mathrm{MB}$ will be divided into chunks. Moreover, we also aggregate many small files into larger units ( $\lesssim 32\mathrm{MB}$ ), resulting in a $35\%$ decrease in the number of files.

# 4.2 Delivered durability

It is critical that AutoProc is able to deliver the level of durability requested. To make verification, we compute the required replication rate from the durability specified above. Using the method described in section 3.2, we derive a threshold of at least $\gamma_{th} = 0.08$ replica/object/day to meet target durability. corresponding to an average cost of $0.7\mathrm{KB / s}$ bandwidth and $1.4\mathrm{GB}$ storage at each peer under the given workload (200 GB unique data scattered across 1000 peers). Here, the average bandwidth cost $B$ and storage cost $S$ per peer can be derived as follows:

$$
B = \frac {D \gamma_ {t h}}{\alpha N}, S = \frac {\alpha B}{\lambda}, \tag {11}
$$

where $D$ is the amount of unique data and $N$ is the number of peers with an average availability of $\alpha$ .

To see how well our prediction holds, we vary the repair bandwidth and storage consumed at each peer across several different simulations. To focus on the effectiveness of our prediction alone, we pay little attention to peer failures, so the priority policy reduces to fewest replicas first (FRF) instead of fewest remaining replicas first, meaning that the object with the fewest replicas (both live and dead) is selected for replication.

The durability of data can be thought of as the percentage of store units that are not lost after 70 days. Both figures show that the durability achieved with our prediction ( $B = 0.7\mathrm{KB / s}$ , $S = 1.4\mathrm{GB}$ ) only slightly surpasses the target. This demonstrates

# 4.3 Durability improvement

In this subsection, we study the benefit of our indicator-based priority assignment method (described in subsection 3.3) in improving the overall data durability. We run the simulator again using $0.7\mathrm{KB / s}$ repair bandwidth and 1.4 GB storage at each peer, but replace FRF method with our indicator method. Figure 9 shows the changes in the histogram of the number of live replicas per store unit observed after 70 days.

We find that there is a slight decrease in the mean number of replicas (from 5.79 to 5.74), while the decrease in the variance is relatively large (from 2.51 to 0.98). This is because our method can exploit the subtle difference among failures to equalize the number of replicas per object. As a result, we can witness the following benefits of our method:

First, the durability of objects has been improved. Figure 9 shows that our indicator method achieves about one nine durability over FRF method (from 0.991 to 0.999). This also explains the slight decrease in the mean number of replicas: Because fewer large objects are lost after employing our indicator method. To put this benefit another way, Figure 7 shows that the system, still using FRF method, needs to double the bandwidth (from 0.7 to $1.4\mathrm{KB / s}$ ) in order to achieve a durability of 0.999.

Second, the fraction of under-replicated objects (e.g., those with replica number no more than three) can be greatly reduced. This enables the system to provide a more failure-resistant storage service to its users, especially in the case of severe machine failure correlations (e.g., disaster events). To confirm this benefit, we perform a further study to examine the impact of correlated failures on the durability gain. We remove a certain fraction of live peers at the middle of the trace to simulate a large correlated failure event. This burst of permanent failures may destroy many objects that only have a few replicas existing.

Figure 10 shows the durability at the end of trace as a function of the fraction of the peers removed. We observe that our indicator method produces a slower decrease in durability as the number of simultaneous failures increases. This clearly suggests that it makes the system more resilient to large correlated failure events, which is a very useful property for data protection.

# 5 Conclusions

This paper describes AutoProc, a method for automating replication in a highly dynamic P2P network. It uses a synthesis of two key methods to achieve high efficiency, including adaptive tuning of replication rate and accurate priority assignment. We evaluate its performance based on the real trace of a popular P2P file sharing system. This paper shows that i) AutoProc is self-tuned in changing peer behavior, guaranteeing the system to take the roadwith the lowest replication cost; ii) by putting low durable objects at high priority, AutoProc allows the system to provide more failure-resistant storage service, especially in the case of disaster events.
