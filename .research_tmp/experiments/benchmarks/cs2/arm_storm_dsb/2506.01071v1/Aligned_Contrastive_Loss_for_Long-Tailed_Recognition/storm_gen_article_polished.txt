# summary

**Aligned Contrastive Loss for Long-Tailed Recognition** (often referred to as Aligned Contrastive Learning or ACL) is a machine learning methodology designed to address the challenges of class imbalance in deep learning-based classification models. In real-world datasets, such as ImageNet and iNaturalist, the distribution of samples per class is often highly imbalanced, with a few "head" classes possessing abundant data while "tail" classes are severely underrepresented.[1] This imbalance typically results in biased feature spaces where majority classes dominate the training process, causing models to predict samples into head classes and deteriorating performance on minority classes.[2] The ACL framework aims to mitigate these issues by improving the uniformity of feature distributions on the hypersphere and ensuring that feature representations are consistent with semantic labels, thereby enhancing generalization across imbalanced classes.[2][3]
The core of the ACL methodology involves two primary components: the ACL-Embed objective and the ACL-Grad mechanism. The ACL-Embed objective treats label embeddings as additional augmented samples with distinct labels, employing contrastive learning to align these embeddings with the representations of their corresponding samples.[3] This alignment helps structure the feature space to be consistent with semantic labels, directly addressing the bias towards high-frequency classes often observed in standard supervised contrastive losses.[4][3] To facilitate the joint optimization of this contrastive objective with the standard cross-entropy (CE) loss, ACL incorporates the ACL-Grad mechanism, which dynamically discards the contrastive term when the gradients of the two objectives conflict, ensuring stable training without destabilization from conflicting optimization signals.[3]
Experimental evaluations indicate that incorporating such contrastive learning and feature balancing strategies significantly improves performance over traditional supervised baselines in long-tailed recognition tasks. Methods utilizing parametric class-wise learnable centers, such as PaCo, have achieved state-of-the-art results on benchmarks including CIFAR, ImageNet, and iNaturalist 2018 by rebalancing the optimization process and enhancing hard example learning.[5] Furthermore, theoretical analyses reveal that standard supervised contrastive learning often fails to form a regular simplex in long-tailed settings, leading to biased feature spaces; ACL addresses this by eliminating imbalanced attraction and repulsion gradients, promoting a more balanced and informative feature space where minority class samples are not collapsed into the same region as majority classes.[6][7]
The development of ACL is part of a broader trend in addressing long-tailed distributions through decoupling representation learning from classification and leveraging self-supervised or contrastive techniques. Recent research suggests that data imbalance may not significantly hinder the learning of high-quality representations if appropriate balancing strategies, such as instance-balanced sampling, are applied, allowing strong long-tailed recognition to be achieved by adjusting the classifier alone.[8] Additionally, related approaches like Generalized Parametric Contrastive Learning (GPaCo) and Rebalanced Siamese Contrastive Mining (ResCom) have introduced class-balanced losses and adaptive weights to further mitigate the dual class-imbalance problem at both batch and Siamese batch levels.[4][9] These advancements underscore the importance of aligning feature spaces and managing optimization conflicts to achieve robust recognition capabilities in imbalanced scenarios.[10][6]

# Background

The long-tailed distribution of real-world data poses significant challenges for deep learning-based classification models, where the number of samples per class is highly imbalanced.[1] This class imbalance often results in biased feature spaces that deteriorate model performance, as majority classes can dominate the training process and alter the decision boundaries of minority classes.[2] Consequently, models trained on such data tend to predict samples into majority classes, a phenomenon that can be theoretically explained through the lens of energy-based models, where free energies of categories align with the label distribution.[11]
To address these issues, various strategies have been proposed, including data augmentation, re-balancing techniques, and transfer learning from head to tail classes.[12][8] However, many existing methods focus on jointly learning representations and classifiers, which can be suboptimal.[8] Recent research has explored decoupling the learning procedure into representation learning and classification, revealing that data imbalance might not be a primary issue in learning high-quality representations if appropriate balancing strategies are applied.[8] Furthermore, supervised contrastive learning has emerged as a powerful tool for long-tailed recognition, offering strong performance gains by improving the uniformity of feature distributions on the hypersphere.[2]
Despite these advancements, challenges remain in how contrastive learning handles imbalanced data. Standard supervised contrastive loss tends to bias high-frequency classes, increasing the difficulty of imbalanced learning.[4] Additionally, supervised contrastive learning can suffer from a dual class-imbalance problem at both the original batch and Siamese batch levels, which is more severe than in standard long-tailed classification.[9] To mitigate these effects, methods such as Rebalanced Siamese Contrastive Mining (ResCom) have introduced class-balanced losses and queues to assign adaptive weights and maintain balanced key counts across classes.[9] Other approaches, like Generalized Parametric Contrastive Learning (GPaCo), introduce parametric class-wise learnable centers to rebalance the optimization process, enhancing the intensity of pulling samples of the same class closer together.[4]
Beyond simple balancing, recent works have investigated the role of latent features and invariances in representation learning. For instance, the Latent Categories based long-tail Recognition (LCReg) method hypothesizes that common latent features shared among head and tail classes can improve feature representation by implicitly enriching training sample diversity.[12] Similarly, research into contrastive self-supervised learning has highlighted the importance of invariances established during pre-training, suggesting that task-specific invariance requirements can be better addressed through amortized invariance learning.[13] These developments underscore the complexity of aligning feature spaces in imbalanced scenarios, where cross-sensor or cross-class mismatches must be resolved to achieve robust recognition capabilities.[10]

# Methodology

The methodology for Aligned Contrastive Learning (ACL) is designed to address the specific challenges of long-tailed recognition, where class imbalance leads to poor generalization and suboptimal feature representations. The approach is grounded in the observation that standard supervised contrastive learning (SCL) often suffers from gradient conflicts and imbalanced attraction and repulsion forces between positive and negative pairs, particularly as the number of views increases.[6] To resolve these issues, ACL introduces mechanisms to align label embeddings with sample representations and to manage the optimization process more effectively.
A core component of the ACL framework is the ACL-Embed objective, which treats label embeddings as additional augmented samples with distinct labels.[3] By employing contrastive learning to align these label embeddings with the representations of their corresponding samples, the method ensures that the feature space is structured in a way that is consistent with the semantic labels, thereby enhancing the model's ability to generalize across imbalanced classes.[3] This alignment helps mitigate the bias towards high-frequency classes that is often observed in supervised contrastive losses, which can otherwise increase the difficulty of learning from imbalanced data.[4]
To facilitate the joint optimization of the ACL-Embed objective and the standard cross-entropy (CE) loss, the ACL framework incorporates a mechanism known as ACL-Grad.[3] Pilot experiments have shown that the CE loss and the contrastive learning objective can frequently conflict with each other in supervised settings, hindering performance.[3] ACL-Grad addresses this by dynamically discarding the ACL-Embed term when the gradients of the two objectives are in conflict, thus ensuring stable and effective training.[3] This adaptive approach allows the model to benefit from the structural advantages of contrastive learning without being destabilized by conflicting optimization signals.
Furthermore, the methodology addresses the issue of poor uniformity in the feature distribution, which is a common problem in long-tailed scenarios where minority class samples exhibit poor separability.[2] By improving the uniformity of the feature distribution on the hypersphere, ACL ensures that samples from minority classes are not crowded or collapsed into the same region as majority classes.[2] This is critical because class collapse, where all points from the same class share the same embedding, minimizes the contrastive loss but discards critical information such as breeds, poses, or backgrounds, leading to suboptimal generalization.[14] The ACL algorithm is specifically designed to eliminate the imbalanced attraction and repulsion gradients identified in theoretical analyses of SCL, thereby promoting a more balanced and informative feature space.[6]
In addition to the primary ACL framework, related methodologies in the field of long-tailed recognition employ parametric class-wise learnable centers to rebalance the optimization process.[4] These centers help adaptively enhance the intensity of pulling samples of the same class closer together, which benefits hard example learning.[4] Other approaches focus on decoupling representation learning from classification, finding that data imbalance may not significantly affect the quality of representations if simple instance-balanced sampling is used, and that strong long-tailed recognition can be achieved by adjusting the classifier rather than the representation.[8] Additionally, some methods propose explicit alignment strategies to correct the misalignment between feature and classifier weight spaces, which is a critical issue in long-tailed regimes that prevents the emergence of Neural Collapse phenomena.[15] These complementary strategies highlight the broader methodological landscape in which ACL operates, emphasizing the importance of balancing, alignment, and uniformity in achieving state-of-the-art performance on long-tailed benchmarks.[6]

# Experimental Results

Experimental evaluations of methods addressing long-tailed recognition demonstrate that incorporating contrastive learning and feature balancing strategies significantly improves performance over traditional supervised baselines. Parametric Contrastive Learning (PaCo) has been shown to achieve state-of-the-art results on long-tailed benchmarks, including CIFAR, ImageNet, Places, and iNaturalist 2018, by using parametric class-wise learnable centers to rebalance optimization and enhance hard example learning[5]. On the full ImageNet dataset, models trained with the PaCo loss surpass standard supervised contrastive learning across various ResNet architectures[5].
Further studies indicate that addressing the geometric configuration of the feature space is critical for long-tailed data. Theoretical analyses reveal that standard supervised contrastive learning fails to form a regular simplex in long-tailed settings, leading to biased feature spaces[7]. To mitigate this, methods such as Supervised contrastive learning on Mixed Classes (SMC) utilize MixUp-based data augmentation to create blended images and weight positives based on class mixture ratios, thereby exploring a more diverse data space and enhancing generalization capabilities[1]. Similarly, feature augmentation techniques that perturb features of different classes with varying Gaussian amplitudes have been shown to balance the embedding distribution, preventing the compression of tail class embeddings that typically occurs when training directly on imbalanced data[16].
Dual-branch architectures have also proven effective in experimental settings. The Dual-Branch Long-Tailed Recognition (DB-LTR) model, which combines an imbalanced learning branch with a Contrastive Learning Branch (CoLB), improves the model's adaptability to tail classes by learning prototypes for each tail class and calculating inter- and intra-branch contrastive losses[17]. Additionally, approaches that leverage latent features shared between head and tail classes, such as the Latent Categories based long-tail Recognition (LCReg) method, implicitly enrich training sample diversity through semantic data augmentation, resulting in better feature representations for tail classes[12].
In the domain of object detection, the dual-balanced network for fine-grained re-balancing has addressed the limitations of coarse-grained re-weighting strategies. By employing a Balance Region Proposal Network (BRPN) and a Balance Classification Network (BCN), this approach equalizes the number of proposals and classification logic, effectively handling the challenges of long-tail distributions in detection tasks[18]. While these results are specific to long-tailed recognition, related work in self-supervised learning has also shown that adaptive bootstrapping of neighbors based on latent space quality can lead to consistent improvements over naive bootstrapping approaches, although naive incorporation of nearest neighbor bootstrapping in self-distillation schemes can sometimes lead to performance drops or collapse[19].

# Discussion

The challenge of long-tailed recognition (LTR) in deep learning stems from the inherent class imbalance in real-world visual data, where a few "head" classes possess abundant samples while "tail" classes are severely underrepresented. Traditional approaches to this problem often rely on class-balancing strategies, such as loss re-weighting, data re-sampling, or transfer learning from head to tail classes, typically within a joint learning framework for representations and classifiers. However, recent research suggests that decoupling the learning procedure into distinct stages of representation learning and classification yields surprising and more robust results. Specifically, it has been found that data imbalance may not significantly hinder the learning of high-quality representations when using simple instance-balanced (natural) sampling; instead, strong long-tailed recognition capabilities can be achieved by adjusting the classification head alone, provided the underlying representations are robust[8].
A critical aspect of improving these representations involves the design of loss functions and sampling schemes. Negative sampling, which allows for efficient training by approximating computationally expensive loss functions, has a profound impact on how models handle label imbalance. Different negative sampling schemes implicitly create a trade-off between performance on dominant versus rare labels. To address this, unified methods have been proposed that explicitly tackle both sampling bias (arising from using a subset of labels) and labeling bias (inherent to the imbalanced data), thereby improving performance on long-tail classification and retrieval benchmarks[20]. Furthermore, low-level data transformations play an essential role in enhancing metric learning. The Independent Domain Embedding Augmentation Learning (IDEAL) method, for instance, learns multiple independent embedding spaces for domains generated by predefined data transformations. This approach is orthogonal to existing deep metric learning techniques and has been shown to significantly improve performance in visual retrieval tasks, such as increasing Recall@1 on the Cars-196 dataset from 84.5% to 87.1%[21].
The integration of self-supervised learning frameworks offers another promising avenue for addressing long-tail distributions, particularly when combined with noisy labels, which are common in large-scale datasets. Self-supervised pre-training, followed by task-specific fine-tuning, has proven effective in simultaneously mitigating the effects of class imbalance and label noise. Notably, self-supervised approaches are effectively ablative to these challenges, meaning they can robustly learn representations even when data assumptions of balance and cleanliness are violated[22]. This aligns with broader trends in interpretable self-supervised learning, such as the Run Away From your Teacher (RAFT) framework. RAFT optimizes two objectives: aligning two views of the same data to similar representations and "running away" from the model's Mean Teacher to explicitly prevent representation collapse. By avoiding the collapse that can occur in frameworks like BYOL, RAFT provides a more conceptually reliable foundation for learning robust features that can be subsequently adapted for long-tailed recognition tasks[23].
In the context of object detection, which also suffers from long-tail distributions, coarse-grained global thresholds used in standard re-balancing strategies often overlook locally influential categories. To address this, dual-balanced networks have been proposed that employ fine-grained re-balancing in both proposal and classification logic. These networks utilize sub-networks like the Balance Region Proposal Network (BRPN) and the Balance Classification Network (BCN) to equalize the number of proposals and adjust classification scores, respectively, thereby improving detection accuracy for rare classes[18]. Additionally, the pursuit of more efficient and interpretable models, as seen in works aligning deep learning with human reasoning and biological vision, emphasizes the need for explainable-by-design methods. While these efforts often focus on medical imaging and causality, the underlying principles of robust feature extraction and explainability are applicable to enhancing the reliability of long-tailed recognition systems in diverse domains[24].
