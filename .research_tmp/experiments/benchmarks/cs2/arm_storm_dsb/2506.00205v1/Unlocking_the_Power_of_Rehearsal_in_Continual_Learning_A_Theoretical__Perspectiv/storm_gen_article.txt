# Theoretical Foundations

The theoretical underpinnings of continual learning (CL) have evolved from heuristic approaches to rigorous mathematical frameworks that address the stability-plasticity dilemma. A prominent theoretical contribution formulates the CL problem via dynamic programming, modeling the trade-off between catastrophic forgetting and generalization as a two-player sequential game. In this formulation, one player maximizes the cost associated with a lack of generalization, while the other minimizes the cost due to catastrophic forgetting. Theoretical analysis demonstrates that a stable balance point exists for each task, leading to the development of Balanced Continual Learning (BCL), which aims to attain this equilibrium between retaining prior knowledge and adapting to new data.[1]
Complementary to game-theoretic approaches, recent work has cast learning rules as policies for navigating partially observable loss landscapes. This optimal control framework identifies optimal learning rules as solutions to associated control problems, providing a unified theoretical basis for various algorithms. For instance, gradient descent emerges from short-horizon optimization, momentum from longer-horizon planning, and adaptive optimizers like Adam from online Bayesian inference of the loss landscape shape. This perspective also extends to continual learning strategies, offering insights into how models should update parameters to balance exploration and exploitation in dynamic environments.[2]
The dynamics of neural network training are further analyzed through the lens of dynamical systems. The DOODL3 toolkit formulates the training of neural networks as a dynamical system, providing an interpretable global view of trajectories in the loss landscape. This approach allows for the empirical quantification of differences in training trajectories and the identification of problematic regions, addressing the question of *how* models learn rather than just *what* they learn.[3] In the context of non-convex optimization, which is prevalent in deep learning, theoretical frameworks leveraging hidden convexity and overparameterization have been developed to explain the convergence of simple gradient methods in zero-sum games. These results provide sufficient conditions—spanning initialization, training dynamics, and network width—that guarantee global convergence to a Nash equilibrium in broad classes of non-convex min-max games, offering a rigorous basis for understanding training stability in complex neural architectures.[4]
Specific to rehearsal-based methods, which exploit memory to revisit past data, theoretical analyses have focused on the memory quantity/quality trade-off. While traditional CL research often prioritized minimizing exemplar memory, recent theoretical and empirical studies challenge this paradigm, suggesting that in regimes where memory is abundant but retraining is expensive, the core challenge shifts from stability to plasticity. Theoretical insights indicate that improved stability allows simple replay baselines to outperform state-of-the-art methods, highlighting the importance of weight space consolidation and rank-based parameter resets to restore plasticity without sacrificing prior knowledge.[5]
Furthermore, biological plausibility has informed theoretical models of plasticity and homeostasis. Research into recurrent neural networks has introduced local learning rules that provably increase robustness to noise by maintaining a multiplicity of conserved quantities, such as the network’s temporal map of input-to-output trajectories. This synaptic balancing is consistent with observed heterosynaptic plasticity and suggests that homeostatic mechanisms must detect rate changes on timescales of seconds to minutes to maintain stable activity levels, a finding confirmed in mean-field formulations of network activity.[6][7] These biological insights provide a theoretical foundation for designing continual learning systems that mimic the hippocampal circuit’s role in memory replay, enabling the selective recall of limited resources to reconstruct learned knowledge in dynamic scenarios.[8]

# Biological Inspiration and Neuroscientific Mapping

The development of artificial continual learning systems is heavily influenced by the mechanisms observed in biological neural networks, which have evolved to learn new tasks and skills in succession without suffering from catastrophic forgetting.[9] While artificial systems often struggle to achieve parity with their biological analogues, mammalian brains employ numerous neural operations, particularly during sleep, that support this capability.[9] A central concept in this biological inspiration is the role of offline memory reprocessing, where converging evidence from molecular to phenomenological levels suggests that reprocessing during sleep is an essential component of how memories are formed and shaped.[10]

## Sleep-Dependent Memory Consolidation

Sleep is widely believed to play an essential role in synaptic plasticity, although the precise mechanisms governing this function involve complex interactions between state-dependent processes (such as wake versus sleep) and state-independent circadian oscillations.[11] Research into the synaptic homeostasis theory of sleep proposes that low neurotransmitter activity during sleep is optimal for memory consolidation.[12] Experimental studies in mice have demonstrated that increasing acetylcholine levels during early sleep, which disrupts sleep structure and reduces slow-wave power, impairs motor skill acquisition, thereby supporting the idea that specific sleep states are critical for consolidating new skills.[12]
To model these biological processes in artificial neural networks, researchers have investigated the integration of three distinct components of mammalian sleep: veridical memory replay observed during non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, generative memory replay linked to rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, and synaptic downscaling.[9] Synaptic downscaling is proposed to tune signal-to-noise ratios and support neural upkeep.[9] Studies indicate that the inclusion of all three sleep components yields benefits for continual learning in artificial systems, suggesting that a holistic approach to modeling sleep is necessary to replicate biological robustness.[9]

## Mechanisms of Replay and Prioritization

In biological organisms, memory replay is not a random process but is prioritized based on specific criteria. Recent neuroscience suggests that replay is prioritized not only by "gain" (the expected benefit of replaying an experience) but also by "need," which measures the expected relevance of an experience to the current situation.[13] This dual prioritization mechanism has inspired new algorithms in reinforcement learning, such as those that consider both gain and need to improve performance in complex environments.[13]
Similarly, the concept of experience replay in artificial agents, where previous interactions with the environment are stored and reused, mirrors biological memory consolidation.[14] Advanced methods like double-prioritized state-recycled (DPSR) experience replay prioritize experiences in both training and storage stages, aiming to maximize the utility of stored data.[14] This aligns with the biological imperative to efficiently manage limited memory resources by focusing on the most informative samples, a challenge also addressed in continual learning frameworks that seek to determine the optimal number of samples to store to prevent forgetting.[15]

## Synaptic Plasticity and Homeostasis

The stability of neural networks during learning is maintained through homeostatic control mechanisms that counteract the positive feedback loops inherent in Hebbian plasticity.[7] Hebbian changes in excitatory synapses are driven by correlations between pre- and postsynaptic activities, which can lead to instability if not regulated.[7] Numerical simulations suggest that homeostasis must detect rate changes on timescales of seconds to minutes to keep network activity at healthy, low levels.[7] This rapid homeostatic regulation is crucial for maintaining the stability required for continual learning, ensuring that the network does not become saturated or unstable as it acquires new information.
Furthermore, the brain's ability to maintain distinct memories without interference is explained by the Complementary Learning Systems (CLS) theory, which posits that the brain utilizes distinct systems for pattern separation (encoding distinct memories) and pattern completion.[16] This biological framework provides a theoretical basis for understanding how humans can learn new information without forgetting prior knowledge, a capability that artificial neural network models often lack due to catastrophic forgetting.[16] By mapping these biological principles—such as sleep-dependent replay, homeostatic plasticity, and complementary learning systems—onto artificial architectures, researchers aim to develop more robust and efficient continual learning algorithms.[9]

# Practical Constraints and Trade-offs

The implementation of rehearsal-based continual learning (CL) is subject to several practical constraints that influence the choice of memory management strategies and the overall efficacy of the model. A primary challenge lies in the allocation of computational resources, where traditional paradigms often misalign with modern hardware bottlenecks. While early research focused on minimizing exemplar memory due to storage limitations, contemporary systems frequently face constraints where GPU time, rather than storage, is the primary bottleneck[17]. In this "middle ground" regime, where memory is sufficient to mitigate forgetting but full retraining is prohibitively expensive, the core challenge shifts from stability to plasticity, as models may become biased toward prior tasks and struggle to learn new ones[17]. Consequently, improved stability allows simple replay baselines to outperform state-of-the-art methods at a fraction of the GPU cost, highlighting the trade-off between computational efficiency and model adaptability[17].
In high-speed data stream environments, model throughput—defined as the maximum number of training samples a model can process within a unit of time—presents an additional critical constraint[18]. Real-world data streams do not pause to accommodate slow models, creating a dilemma where methods focused solely on mitigating catastrophic forgetting may incur a heavier training workload that limits data utilization[18]. This necessitates a balance between the accuracy gains from extensive rehearsal and the speed required to process continuous data streams[18].
Memory budget limitations are further exacerbated in specific application domains, such as Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR), where storing data can be costly, infeasible with pre-trained models, or restricted by privacy regulations[19]. Running rehearsal-based methods with smaller memory sizes to alleviate these issues typically leads to degraded performance, prompting the development of parameter-efficient methods that remain effective even with minimal memory[19]. Similarly, in semi-supervised continual learning (SSCL) settings, where labeled data is scarce but non-i.i.d. unlabeled data is plentiful, the memory budget for rehearsal must be significantly reduced while leveraging environmental data to maintain performance[20].
The selection of samples for the rehearsal coreset introduces another layer of complexity, as not all data points possess equal potential for informativeness[21]. The quality of stored samples directly affects the model's effectiveness and efficiency, a factor that becomes critical under realistic settings such as imbalanced continual learning or noisy data scenarios[21]. Strategies such as Online Coreset Selection (OCS) aim to select the most representative and informative samples at each iteration to optimize this trade-off[21]. Furthermore, the choice between reservoir sampling and alternative population strategies, as well as the determination of the optimal number of stored samples, remains an area of active investigation to maximize the utility of limited memory[15].
Finally, the pursuit of accuracy in class-incremental learning must be balanced against fairness considerations, particularly for Trustworthy AI applications[22]. Naively using all samples of the current task for training can result in unfair catastrophic forgetting for certain sensitive groups, including specific classes[22]. Theoretical analyses indicate that forgetting occurs when the average gradient vector of the current task data is in an "opposite direction" compared to that of a sensitive group[22]. Addressing this requires adjusting training weights to modify the direction of the average gradient vector, thereby reducing the forgetting of underperforming groups and achieving a balance between overall accuracy and group fairness[22].

# Buffer Management and Sample Selection

In rehearsal-based continual learning, the management of the episodic memory buffer is critical for mitigating catastrophic forgetting. This process involves two primary challenges: determining the optimal number of samples to store and selecting the most informative data points from the stream of experiences. While storing a small subset of previous examples allows models to retain knowledge of past tasks, the quality and quantity of these samples directly impact the model's effectiveness and efficiency.[15]

## Sample Selection Strategies

The selection of samples for the rehearsal buffer, often referred to as coreset selection, is a significant area of research. Not all data points within a dataset possess equal potential; some are more representative or informative than others, and this unequal importance can have a substantial impact on performance in rehearsal-based continual learning.[21]

### Gradient Matching Methods

A prominent approach to coreset selection is based on the principle of gradient matching. This method posits that the gradients induced by the selected coreset should match, as closely as possible, those induced by the original training dataset.[23][24] By leveraging neural tangent kernel theory, researchers have developed methods to perform this gradient matching across the model's initialization distribution, allowing for the extraction of a coreset without the need to train the model first.[24] These gradient-matching strategies have been shown to outperform traditional competitors, such as reservoir sampling, across a range of memory sizes.[23]

### Online and Representative Selection

To address the dynamic nature of continual learning, Online Coreset Selection (OCS) has been proposed as a simple yet effective method that selects the most representative and informative coreset at each iteration.[21] This approach is particularly important under realistic settings, such as imbalanced continual learning or scenarios with noisy data, where the quality of stored samples is paramount.[21]

## Buffer Size and Memory Constraints

The determination of the optimal buffer size has traditionally focused on minimizing exemplar memory, a constraint often misaligned with modern computational systems where GPU time, rather than storage, is the primary bottleneck.[17] Recent studies challenge this paradigm by investigating a "middle ground" where memory is abundant enough to mitigate forgetting, but full retraining from scratch remains prohibitively expensive.[17]
In this regime, the core challenge shifts from stability to plasticity, as models may become biased toward prior tasks and struggle to learn new ones.[17] Conversely, improved stability allows simple replay baselines to outperform state-of-the-art methods at a fraction of the GPU cost.[17] Research has also empirically analyzed the effectiveness of very small episodic memories, finding that even simple baselines that jointly train on current and replayed examples can be surprisingly effective across various supervised learning benchmarks.[25]

## Theoretical Perspectives on Forgetting and Balance

Theoretical frameworks have also been developed to understand the trade-offs inherent in buffer management. One approach formulates the continual learning problem via dynamic programming, modeling the trade-off between catastrophic forgetting and generalization as a two-player sequential game.[1] In this model, one player maximizes the cost due to lack of generalization, while the other minimizes the cost due to catastrophic forgetting.[1] Theoretical analysis suggests that a stable balance point exists for each task, leading to the development of Balanced Continual Learning (BCL), which aims to attain this equilibrium between generalization and forgetting.[1]
Furthermore, mechanistic analyses of catastrophic forgetting in large language models have identified primary drivers such as gradient interference in attention weights, representational drift in intermediate layers, and loss landscape flattening.[26] Understanding these mechanisms is essential for designing buffer management strategies that effectively counteract the interference of newly acquired knowledge with previously learned capabilities.[26]

# Challenges and Open Problems

The field of continual learning (CL) faces several significant theoretical and practical challenges, primarily centered around the stability-plasticity dilemma, the limitations of current evaluation metrics, and the computational and memory constraints of rehearsal-based methods.

## The Stability-Plasticity Dilemma and Catastrophic Forgetting

A core challenge in connectionist systems is addressing the stability-plasticity dilemma, where models must adapt to new data without forgetting previously acquired knowledge.[5] This issue, known as catastrophic forgetting, remains a dominant concern as deep learning methods are typically evaluated on dedicated test sets under a "closed world assumption," where test data is guaranteed to originate from the same distribution as the training data.[27][28] This assumption poses a massive challenge because neural networks are known to provide overconfident false predictions on unknown or corrupted instances, yet current comparisons of individual methods are often performed in isolation from real-world conditions by monitoring accumulated benchmark performance.[27]

## Limitations of Rehearsal-Based Methods

Rehearsal-based methods, which exploit memory to revisit past data, have proven effective in achieving state-of-the-art performance by mitigating catastrophic forgetting.[5][29] However, these approaches face critical limitations regarding memory budgets and data availability. Storing data for rehearsal is often costly, infeasible with pre-trained models, or restricted by privacy regulations.[19] Consequently, running existing rehearsal-based methods with smaller memory sizes to alleviate these issues usually leads to degraded performance.[19]
Recent theoretical analyses have further complicated the understanding of rehearsal strategies. While concurrent rehearsal (training on past and new data together) is widely used, it remains unclear if this approach is always optimal compared to sequential rehearsal, where new data is trained first followed by revisiting past data.[29] Furthermore, the theoretical understanding of how rehearsal scale influences learning dynamics is limited; counterintuitive findings suggest that rehearsal can impair a model's adaptability, and increasing the rehearsal scale does not necessarily improve memorability or generalization.[30]

## Memory Efficiency and Data Quality Trade-offs

To address the high memory costs of rehearsal, researchers are investigating the memory quantity/quality trade-off by adopting various data reduction approaches to increase the number of instances storable in memory.[5] This includes exploring complex instance compression techniques.[5] Additionally, semi-supervised continual learning (SSCL) settings have been formalized to leverage plentiful non-i.i.d. unlabeled data from an agent's environment when labeled data is scarce.[20] Strategies involving pseudo-labeling, consistency regularization, Out-of-Distribution (OoD) detection, and knowledge distillation have been shown to reduce forgetting in these realistic settings.[20] Other approaches propose parameter-efficient methods, such as applying Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) to changes in linear layers to retrain only gating vectors, allowing for effective continual learning even with minimal memory.[19]

## Theoretical Gaps in Learning Dynamics and Optimization

Beyond specific CL techniques, broader theoretical gaps exist in understanding the optimization dynamics of neural networks. While learning rules are typically assumed rather than derived, recent frameworks have begun to cast learning rules as policies for navigating loss landscapes, identifying optimal rules as solutions to optimal control problems.[2] This perspective helps explain why certain rules, such as gradient descent, momentum, or adaptive optimizers like Adam, emerge under different assumptions regarding horizon optimization and parameter space geometry.[2]
Furthermore, while empirical evidence shows that simple gradient methods frequently converge in non-convex min-max games (such as those in adversarial training), theoretical guarantees for global convergence to von Neumann-Nash equilibria in broad classes of non-convex games involving two-layer neural networks have only recently been established.[4] In the context of deep learning theory, precise finite-width estimates for the convergence of shallow neural networks to their associated Gaussian processes in the infinite-width limit remain limited, particularly during training, though recent work has provided explicit upper bounds on the quadratic Wasserstein distance demonstrating polynomial decay with network width.[31]

## Biological Plausibility and Homeostasis

From a biological perspective, a major challenge is developing learning algorithms that are both efficient and biologically compatible. Recent approximations to backpropagation have mitigated some computational inefficiencies but often decrease accuracy, suggesting that entirely different approaches may be more fruitful.[32] Algorithms like multilayer SoftHebb aim to train deep networks without feedback, target, or error signals, avoiding issues like weight transport and non-local plasticity.[32] However, maintaining stability in such systems requires homeostatic control mechanisms. In recurrent networks with spike-timing-dependent plasticity, homeostasis must detect rate changes on timescales of seconds to minutes to keep activity stable, suggesting the existence of specific regulatory mechanisms that react to firing rate changes over these intervals.[7] Developing local learning rules that provably increase the robustness of neural dynamics to noise in nonlinear recurrent networks remains an active area of research.[6]
