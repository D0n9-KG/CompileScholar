# Motivation and Background

Accurate brain tumor segmentation is a critical component in the clinical assessment, prognosis, and subsequent treatment of patients with gliomas.[1][2] While convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have become the state-of-the-art approach for this task, significant challenges remain regarding the trade-off between computational efficiency and the capture of 3D contextual information.[2] Standard 3D convolution operations require substantially larger amounts of memory and computational cost compared to 2D operations, which has hindered the development of deep neural networks in many 3D vision tasks.[3] Consequently, most proposed models either utilize 2D architectures that ignore 3D contextual information or employ 3D models that demand large memory capacity and extensive learning databases.[2]
There has been considerable debate over 2D versus 3D representation learning for 3D medical images.[4] 2D approaches benefit from large-scale pretraining on natural image datasets but are generally weak in capturing large 3D contexts.[4] Conversely, 3D approaches are natively strong in modeling 3D contexts, yet few publicly available 3D medical datasets are large and diverse enough to support universal 3D pretraining.[4] Hybrid approaches attempting to combine 2D and 3D convolutions often still suffer from the intrinsic disadvantages of their respective components.[4] To address these limitations, recent research has focused on bridging the gap between 2D and 3D convolutions. For instance, Axial-Coronal-Sagittal (ACS) convolutions have been proposed to perform natively 3D representation learning while utilizing pretrained weights from 2D datasets, thereby reducing training epochs and trainable parameters.[4]
Furthermore, the scarcity of 3D medical data has led to the exploration of transfer learning strategies to leverage powerful 2D convolutional neural networks for 3D semantic segmentation.[5] Methods such as asymmetric U-Net architectures incorporating 2D backbones like EfficientNet have been developed to reduce the third dimension in early layers to fit 2D network inputs.[5] Other efficiency-focused approaches include the use of 3D depthwise separable convolutions, which drastically reduce the number of parameters in 3D convolutions by more than an order of magnitude, allowing for lighter-weight neural networks.[3] Additionally, frameworks like No-More-Sliding-Window (NMSW) have been introduced to enhance inference efficiency in 3D segmentation by eliminating the need for sliding window approaches, which can significantly slow down processing.[6] These developments highlight the ongoing effort to create efficient, accurate, and data-robust models for brain tumor segmentation.

# Methodology

The methodology for efficient 3D brain tumor segmentation centers on enhancing the standard nnU-Net framework, which has previously demonstrated strong performance in medical imaging challenges but suffers from extensive training requirements and underutilization of pre-trained weights [7]. To address these limitations, the proposed approach integrates Axial-Coronal-Sagittal (ACS) convolutions into the nnU-Net architecture [7]. This integration aims to improve the model's ability to capture spatial relationships across different anatomical planes while simultaneously reducing the number of trainable parameters and training epochs [7].
A key component of this methodology is the strategic transfer of 2D pre-trained weights from ImageNet to the 3D domain [7]. Two specific strategies are employed to ensure that learned relationships and feature representations are preserved during this transfer, which is critical for effective information propagation within the network [7]. By leveraging these pre-trained encoders, the model achieves improved efficiency and reduced computational overhead compared to training from scratch [7].
Furthermore, the methodology explores a joint classification and segmentation model that leverages pre-trained encoders from a brain glioma grade classification task [7]. This multi-task approach allows the segmentation network to benefit from the high-level semantic features learned during classification, potentially enhancing the accuracy of tumor boundary delineation. The overall architecture is designed to balance segmentation accuracy with computational efficiency, addressing the high memory demands typically associated with 3D convolutional operations in volumetric medical imaging [7][8].

# Theoretical Basis for Efficiency

The primary challenge in applying deep learning to 3D medical image segmentation, such as brain tumor analysis, is the substantial increase in memory consumption and computational cost associated with the added depth dimension compared to 2D models.[6] Standard 3D convolution operations require significantly larger amounts of memory and computation than their 2D counterparts, a fact that has historically hindered the development of deep neural networks in many 3D vision tasks.[3] To address these constraints, various theoretical approaches have been developed to decouple or optimize 3D convolutions, aiming to retain the ability to capture inter-slice relationships while reducing parameter counts and inference times.
One prominent theoretical approach involves the use of depthwise separable convolutions in 3D scenarios. By splitting a single standard 3D convolution into two separate steps, 3D depthwise convolution can drastically reduce the number of parameters by more than one order of magnitude.[3] This method allows 3D vision tasks, including classification and reconstruction, to be carried out with more lightweight neural networks, thereby mitigating the computational burden without entirely sacrificing spatial context.[3]
Another significant line of research focuses on replacing full 3D convolution layers with structured combinations of 2D and 1D convolutions. The Parallel Separable 3D Convolution (PmSCn) block, for instance, applies parallel streams of 2D and 1D convolution layers along different dimensions, mathematically justified through tensor decomposition to replace a single 3D convolution layer.[9] Similarly, pseudo-3D convolution modules decouple 3D convolution into 2D and 1D components, exploiting residual information in the feature space to better structure motions and reduce computational demands.[10] These architectures demonstrate that complex 3D interactions can be approximated by lighter 2D and 1D operations, which is particularly beneficial for volumetric data understanding where training time and computation are critical factors.[9]
In the specific context of brain tumor segmentation, these efficiency principles are often integrated into U-Net-like architectures. For example, asymmetric U-Net networks may incorporate 2D classification models, such as EfficientNet, into the encoding branch, using initial layers to reduce the third dimension to fit the 2D input requirements.[5] Other approaches utilize 2.5D convolutions, which extract low-level features from each modality independently and merge features from different views of a 3D image, offering a middle ground between pure 2D and full 3D processing.[2] Furthermore, recent transformer-based architectures like RefineFormer3D employ GhostConv3D-based patch embedding and MixFFN3D modules with low-rank projections and depthwise convolutions to balance segmentation accuracy with computational efficiency, resulting in models with significantly fewer parameters.[8] These theoretical advancements collectively support the development of efficient 3D segmentation frameworks that can handle the high memory demands of volumetric medical imaging while maintaining high accuracy.

# Experimental Evaluation

The performance of the proposed axial-coronal-sagittal embedding model was evaluated using the standard metrics established by the Brain Tumor Segmentation (BraTS) challenge, which serves as a primary benchmark for automated 3D MRI brain tumor segmentation[1][11]. The evaluation focused on the segmentation of three distinct subregions: the whole tumor, the tumor core, and the enhancing tumor. Quantitative assessment was conducted using the Dice coefficient (Dice score) and the Hausdorff distance (95%), which measure the overlap and boundary accuracy of the predicted segmentation masks, respectively[12].
In the 2021 BraTS challenge, the proposed method, which utilized modifications to the nn-UNet architecture including axial attention in the decoder, demonstrated effectiveness through both internal 5-fold cross-validation and online evaluation by the organizers[1]. While the approach showed only minor improvements in quantitative metrics compared to the baseline nn-UNet, it achieved first place in the final ranking on unseen test data, highlighting the robustness of the axial attention mechanism in generalizing to new datasets[1].
Comparative analysis with other state-of-the-art methods reveals the competitive nature of the field. For instance, a two-stage encoder-decoder model utilizing variational autoencoder regularization and attention gates achieved mean Dice scores of 0.9041, 0.8350, and 0.7958 for the whole tumor, tumor core, and enhancing tumor, respectively, on the BraTS2020 validation dataset[12]. On the corresponding testing dataset, this method recorded Dice scores of 0.8729, 0.8357, and 0.8205, with Hausdorff distances of 11.4288, 19.9690, and 15.6711[12]. These results underscore the high level of accuracy required to compete in modern brain tumor segmentation challenges, where deep learning-based methods have become the standard due to the availability of modern GPUs for fast optimization[11].
Furthermore, the evaluation of segmentation algorithms is not limited to purely automated deep learning approaches. Studies comparing manual segmentation with ligature-based (LA) segmentation in virtual reality and desktop environments have shown that while manual methods are the gold standard, they are limited by the requirement for a large number of landmarks[13]. Automated methods, such as the proposed axial-coronal-sagittal embedding, aim to provide a time-saving alternative that maintains high segmentation-to-volume accuracy, thereby facilitating more efficient diagnosis and treatment planning[13][11].

# Clinical Implications

Accurate and timely brain tumor segmentation is critical for clinical assessment, treatment planning, and monitoring disease progression in patients with gliomas and other brain tumors [14]. As brain tumors are a leading cause of death in adults and often infiltrate surrounding normal tissues, delineating tumor boundaries remains a significant challenge for radiologists [14]. The integration of artificial intelligence, particularly deep learning, has emerged as a vital tool to assist radiologists in these diagnostic processes, enabling a better understanding of tumor biology and facilitating personalized care [15].
Current clinical workflows often involve physicians weighing different modality volume data to discover distinct tumor areas, typically segmenting the most distinct region first before searching for others [16]. However, existing automated approaches frequently treat segmentation as a common semantic segmentation task, potentially ignoring these underlying clinical rules [16]. Advanced segmentation methods, such as those utilizing multi-modal MRI, aim to address these complexities by providing high-quality and reproducible results that support precise treatment planning [15][14]. Furthermore, accurate segmentation of not only the tumor but also organs-at-risk, such as the brainstem and hippocampus, is essential for safe surgical and radiation planning [17].
Despite the potential of deep learning models, such as U-Net variants that have dominated recent challenges, there is a trade-off between segmentation accuracy and computational complexity [14]. Many state-of-the-art models push the boundaries of performance at the expense of high computational demands, which can be a barrier to widespread clinical adoption [14]. Therefore, efficient methods that balance accuracy with computational budget are of particular interest for real-time or near-real-time clinical applications [14]. Additionally, the ability to handle anisotropic clinical data and distinguish between normal and pathological structures, as seen in multiple sclerosis diagnosis, highlights the broader clinical need for robust imaging analysis frameworks that can operate effectively on diverse and complex medical data [18].

# Limitations

Despite the improvements in efficiency and performance offered by integrating Axial-Coronal-Sagittal (ACS) convolutions and pre-trained weights into the nnU-Net framework, several limitations remain inherent to the approach and the broader context of 3D medical image segmentation. A primary challenge lies in the fundamental trade-off between 2D and 3D representation learning. While 2D approaches benefit from large-scale pre-training on datasets like ImageNet, they are generally weak in capturing large 3D contexts, whereas 3D approaches, though natively strong in 3D contexts, suffer from a lack of large and diverse public datasets for universal 3D pre-training[4]. Consequently, even hybrid methods that attempt to bridge this gap, such as those using ACS convolutions, still face intrinsic disadvantages associated with the 2D/3D components[4].
Furthermore, the reliance on transferring 2D pre-trained weights to the 3D domain introduces specific constraints. The process of splitting 2D convolution kernels into three parts for axial, coronal, and sagittal views requires careful preservation of learned relationships and feature representations to ensure effective information propagation[7][4]. If these relationships are not adequately maintained during the transfer, the model may fail to fully leverage the benefits of the pre-trained weights, potentially limiting the extent of performance gains compared to fully 3D pre-trained models.
Additionally, while the proposed methods aim to reduce training epochs and trainable parameters, the complexity of brain tumor segmentation remains high due to the variability in tumor size, connectivity, and convexity[19]. Traditional multi-branch structures with different-sized convolution kernels often struggle to precisely characterize feature similarity across these variations, and while ACS convolutions offer a more efficient alternative, they may still face challenges in capturing the full spectrum of tumor morphologies without extensive architectural modifications[19]. Moreover, the generalizability of these models across different datasets and clinical settings may be limited by the specific characteristics of the training data, as seen in other segmentation tasks where models often require tailoring for specific planes or conditions[20].
