# Background

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a neurodegenerative disease characterized by the formation of focal lesions in both white and gray matter, with pathological processes involving inflammation, demyelination, and neuro-axonal loss.[1][2] While histopathological evaluation has long served as the gold standard for defining these underlying processes, it suffers from intrinsic biases due to the limited availability of tissue samples, which often represent late-stage or atypical cases, and the impossibility of longitudinal follow-up.[1][2] Consequently, advanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques have become essential for in vivo assessment, offering sensitivity to various pathological substrates and enabling the monitoring of dynamic changes over time.[1]
Among these advanced imaging modalities, Quantitative Susceptibility Mapping (QSM) has emerged as a critical tool for identifying specific lesion subtypes, particularly paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs), also known as rim+ lesions.[3][4][5] These lesions are characterized by a paramagnetic rim at the edge of non-gadolinium-enhancing lesions, which indicates perilesional chronic inflammation and the presence of iron-laden active microglia and macrophages.[6][7][5] PRLs are considered an emerging biomarker in MS, as patients with a higher burden of these lesions tend to exhibit more aggressive disease courses, increased disability, and greater neurodegeneration.[3][6][5] The detection of PRLs is further supported by other susceptibility-sensitive sequences, such as susceptibility-weighted angiography (SWAN), which can reveal compartmentalized inflammation and central vein signs associated with higher levels of MS pathology.[7]
Despite the clinical significance of PRLs, their detection and quantification present significant challenges. PRLs are relatively rare compared to non-rim lesions, creating a substantial class imbalance that complicates the development of robust detection algorithms.[4] Existing literature has historically lacked comprehensive quantitative analysis of these lesions, with many studies relying on manual review or semi-automated methods that may not fully capture the complexity of rim morphology, such as partial rim lesions.[3][7] To address these limitations, recent approaches have introduced quantitative identification frameworks, such as RimSet, which combines unsupervised segmentation methods with radiomic measurements to improve the characterization of rim+ lesions.[3] Furthermore, the scarcity of annotated data for PRLs has led to the exploration of synthetic data generation techniques; producing synthetic quantitative susceptibility maps of paramagnetic rim lesions has been shown to improve classifier performance by mitigating the effects of class imbalance.[4] These developments highlight the growing importance of advanced computational methods in accurately detecting and monitoring PRLs to better understand their impact on disease progression and to support clinical decision-making in MS management.[3]

# Synthetic Data Generation

The generation of synthetic data is a critical component in advancing medical imaging research, particularly in addressing challenges related to data scarcity, class imbalance, and the fidelity of physical acquisition processes. In the context of computed tomography (CT), traditional image-domain methods have been limited by their inability to simulate the physical acquisition process or utilize the complete statistical information present in projection data. To overcome these limitations, PRO, a projection domain synthesis foundation model, was developed as the first study to perform CT synthesis in the projection domain. By learning rich structural representations from projection data and leveraging anatomical text prompts, PRO enables controllable synthesis that simulates physical scanning processes, including material attenuation characteristics and beam hardening effects[8].
In magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), generative models are increasingly used to synthesize data for various modalities, addressing the variability in modality availability across datasets. A physics-informed generative model utilizing latent diffusion models has been proposed to synthesize a variable number of brain MRI modalities, including those not present in the original dataset. This approach employs a two-step generative process: first, unobserved physical tissue property maps are synthesized using a latent diffusion model, and then these maps are combined with a physical signal model to generate the final MRI scan. This method has demonstrated efficacy in generating unseen MR contrasts while preserving physical plausibility and validating the distributions of generated tissue properties[9].
For specific clinical applications such as breast cancer classification in mammography, where malignancy rates are low and lesions are small, generative adversarial networks (GANs) are employed for data augmentation. A novel U-net-based GAN architecture, incorporating self-attention and semi-supervised learning components, has been developed to realistically synthesize and remove lesions on mammograms. This model generates high-resolution outputs to address the overfitting on cancer cases and under-utilization of non-cancer data inherent in screening populations[10].
Beyond medical imaging, synthetic data generation techniques are also being applied to other domains to address privacy and data availability issues. In the field of specialized machine learning on smart devices, the SpinML system generates customized synthetic image data to privately train models tailored to user requests. This system allows for fine-grained, object-level control over reference images, enabling a trade-off between privacy and utility[11]. Similarly, in the domain of large language models (LLMs), the LENS framework synthesizes preference data directly in the latent embedding space. By employing a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) to learn structured latent representations and performing controlled perturbations, LENS efficiently generates diverse, semantically consistent synthetic preference pairs, bypassing the high costs associated with textual data synthesis and annotation[12]. Additionally, in text-to-image synthesis, the Hierarchical Self-Supervised LVLM (Hi-SSLVLM) utilizes a two-stage self-supervised learning strategy to generate hierarchical captions and refine image generation, reducing reliance on extensive human annotation for paired image-text datasets[13].

# Denoising and Image Restoration

Denoising and image restoration are critical components in the analysis of multiple sclerosis (MS) MRI data, as magnetic resonance imaging is susceptible to noise and artifacts that can limit diagnostic accuracy[14]. While a myriad of denoising algorithms have been developed for medical imaging, many traditional models have become increasingly complex, often requiring high computational resources or extensive pretraining[14]. To address these limitations, lightweight algorithms have been proposed that utilize the image’s inherent noise via dictionary learning to improve image quality without high computational complexity[14]. One such approach employs orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP) to enhance image quality, offering a viable alternative to more complex traditional denoising methods for clinical settings[14].
Beyond simple noise reduction, variational models have been applied to the image restoration inverse problem to handle more complex degradation scenarios[15]. These models often assume that additive noise and image gradient magnitudes follow a generalized normal (GN) distribution, characterized by parameters that determine the shape and scale of the probability density function[15]. Within a hierarchical Bayesian framework, the unknown image and distribution parameters are jointly estimated using a Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) approach[15]. The resulting hypermodels are minimized through an alternating scheme that benefits from robust initialization based on the noise whiteness property, allowing for the automatic estimation of both the image and the noise parameters[15].
In the context of advanced generative and latent space techniques, denoising principles are also being leveraged to improve the internal representations of multimodal models[16]. Inspired by latent denoising for visual tokenizers, recent frameworks corrupt projected visual tokens using a saliency-aware mixture of masking and Gaussian noising[16]. The model is then trained to denoise these corrupted tokens by recovering clean teacher patch features, which serves as an effective form of visual supervision to improve feature alignment and multimodal understanding[16]. This probabilistic approach to handling noise in latent spaces parallels the use of stochastic sampling in other architectures, such as Variational Sparse Autoencoders (vSAE), where stochastic sampling from learned Gaussian posteriors helps organize features more coherently in the latent space[17].
Furthermore, the challenge of noise and artifact mitigation is integral to the synthesis of synthetic data for MS research. For instance, when generating synthetic MS lesions on MRI images to augment training data for supervised machine learning algorithms, the quality of the underlying images is paramount[18]. Similarly, in the synthesis of brain MRI modalities using physics-informed generative models, the preservation of physical plausibility and the accurate representation of tissue properties are essential to ensure that the generated data is free from unrealistic artifacts[9]. These advancements in restoration and synthesis collectively aim to overcome the lack of available ground truth and improve the performance of diagnostic algorithms in the presence of imaging noise[18][14].

# Experimental Methodology

The experimental methodologies for the synthetic generation and analysis of rim lesions in multiple sclerosis (MS) and related neurological conditions rely on a diverse array of deep learning architectures, physics-informed models, and statistical analysis techniques. These approaches are designed to address specific challenges such as data scarcity, the need for physical plausibility, and the accurate quantification of lesion characteristics.

## Generative Models for Lesion Synthesis

To overcome the limitations of small annotated datasets, several studies have employed generative frameworks to synthesize both lesions and healthy brain images. A progressive brain lesion synthesis framework, known as PAVAE, utilizes two sequential networks: a mask synthesis network and a mask-guided lesion synthesis network to expand the quantity and diversity of training data for laser interstitial thermal therapy (LITT) applications[19]. Similarly, a foreground-based generative method has been developed to model local lesion characteristics, enabling the generation of synthetic lesions on healthy images and the synthesis of subject-specific pseudo-healthy images from pathological ones[20]. This method serves as a data augmentation module, improving the performance of brain image segmentation networks in multiple sclerosis studies[20].
Another approach involves a two-input, two-output fully convolutional neural network (FCNN) for MS lesion synthesis in MRI images[18]. In this model, lesion information is encoded as discrete binary intensity level masks, which are stacked with input images and processed end-to-end without the need for manual annotation of lesions in the training set[18]. The generated synthetic lesions are then used for data augmentation to enhance supervised MS lesion detection algorithms[18].

## Physics-Informed and Projection Domain Synthesis

Recent advances have integrated physical constraints into generative models to improve the fidelity of synthetic data. A physics-informed generative model utilizes latent diffusion models in a two-step process: first synthesizing unobserved physical tissue property maps, and then combining these maps with a physical signal model to generate final MRI scans[9]. This approach allows for the synthesis of a variable number of brain MRI modalities, including those not present in the original dataset, while preserving physical plausibility[9].
In the context of computed tomography (CT), a projection domain synthesis foundation model named PRO has been introduced to generate synthetic CT projection data[8]. Unlike image-domain methods, PRO learns rich structural representations directly from projection data and leverages anatomical text prompts for controllable synthesis[8]. This method simulates physical scanning processes, including material attenuation characteristics and beam hardening, utilizing complete measurement signals[8].

## Registration and Denoising Techniques

Accurate registration and image quality enhancement are critical for analyzing lesion data. To improve atlas-to-image registration accuracy in the presence of large pathologies, a deep variational convolutional encoder-decoder network is used to learn a mapping from pathological images to quasi-normal images[21]. This method estimates local mapping uncertainty through network inference statistics and down-weights the image registration similarity measure in areas of high uncertainty[21].
For MRI data, which is susceptible to noise and artifacts, a lightweight denoising algorithm utilizing orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP) and dictionary learning has been developed[14]. This algorithm leverages the image’s inherent noise to improve quality without high computational complexity or pretraining, and has been evaluated on real noise encountered in clinical settings for MS patients[14].

## Statistical and Diagnostic Analysis

The diagnostic utility of specific lesion types, such as paramagnetic rim lesions (PRLs), is assessed through retrospective, cross-sectional analyses of longitudinal cohorts[22]. Automated MRI systems are employed to classify PRLs into distinct groups based on post-processing protocols, such as Susceptibility Weighted Angiography, to determine their specificity and sensitivity for discriminating MS from diagnostic mimics[22]. Additionally, conventional MRI characteristics, including the presence of lesions and neurologic activity, are analyzed to identify imaging biomarkers that correlate with disease burden and outcome[23].

# Results and Performance

The performance of methods for synthetic generation, latent projection, and denoising of rim lesions in multiple sclerosis (MS) has been evaluated across various datasets and metrics, demonstrating significant improvements in diagnostic accuracy, segmentation quality, and computational efficiency.
**Quantitative Identification and Characterization**
In the context of quantitative analysis, the RimSet framework, which combines unsupervised segmentation (RimSeg) with radiomic measurements, demonstrated robust performance in identifying rim+ lesions on Quantitative Susceptibility Mapping (QSM) images. Validation on an in vivo dataset of 172 MS subjects, comprising 177 rim+ and 3,986 rim- lesions, showed that RimSeg achieved a Dice score of 78.7% against ground truth, although challenges remained in segmenting partial rim lesions. The overall RimSet pipeline detected rim+ lesions with a partial ROC AUC of 0.808 and a PR AUC of 0.737, surpassing existing methods. Additionally, the QSMRim-Net component exhibited the lowest mean square error (0.85) and high correlation with ground truth data, indicating its effectiveness in quantitative characterization[3].
**Synthetic Lesion Generation and Data Augmentation**
Synthetic generation techniques have proven effective in enhancing supervised learning algorithms by addressing the scarcity of annotated ground truth data. A two-input two-output fully convolutional neural network model for MS lesion synthesis, which encodes lesion information as discrete binary intensity level masks, was trained end-to-end without manual annotation. When used for data augmentation on healthy images via registration, this pipeline improved the performance of supervised MS lesion detection algorithms on in-house clinical data[18]. Similarly, a foreground-based generative method capable of synthesizing subject-specific pseudo-healthy images from pathological ones demonstrated the ability to generate highly realistic pseudo-healthy and pseudo-pathological brain images. Experiments on MS brain MRI data confirmed that data augmentation using these synthetic images improved brain image segmentation performance compared to training on real data alone[20].
**Denoising and Image Restoration**
In terms of image quality enhancement, a lightweight denoising algorithm utilizing orthogonal matching pursuit (OMP) and dictionary learning was developed to improve MRI image quality without high computational complexity or pretraining. This method leverages the image’s inherent noise to enhance quality and was evaluated against traditional denoising algorithms on real noise encountered in clinical settings, specifically on 1.5T spine MRI scans from 50 MS patients[14]. Furthermore, variational models for image restoration based on a generalized normal (GN) distribution for noise and gradient magnitudes were optimized within a Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) framework. These models jointly estimate unknown images and parameters, utilizing an alternating scheme with robust initialization based on noise whiteness to minimize hypermodels, thereby improving restoration accuracy in the presence of additive noise[15].
**Registration and Latent Space Synthesis**
For atlas-to-image registration in the presence of large pathologies, a deep variational convolutional encoder-decoder network was employed to learn a mapping from pathological images to quasi-normal images. This approach, which estimates local mapping uncertainty to down-weight similarity measures in high-uncertainty areas, improved registration accuracy when tested on synthetic brain tumor images and data from the BRATS 2015 challenge[21]. In the domain of latent space synthesis, the LENS framework, which uses a Variational Autoencoder (VAE) to learn structured latent representations of response embeddings, provided theoretical guarantees that synthesized pairs approximately preserve original preference ordering. Empirically, this latent-space synthesis method significantly outperformed existing textual data synthesis methods in terms of efficiency and generalization for reward modeling, bypassing costly text generation and annotation processes[12].

# Clinical Implications

The integration of advanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques and synthetic data generation methods has significant implications for the clinical management, diagnosis, and prognosis of multiple sclerosis (MS). While pathological evaluation remains the gold standard for identifying disease processes, it is limited by the small number of available donors and the inability to perform longitudinal follow-up [2]. Consequently, MRI has become pivotal in aiding clinicians with diagnosis and monitoring, extending beyond simple clinical utility to facilitate the in vivo detection of various components of MS pathogenesis [24].
One of the primary clinical challenges addressed by these advancements is the differentiation between MS and other conditions that may present with similar imaging features. Inappropriate image interpretation and the application of MRI diagnostic criteria can contribute to misdiagnosis, particularly in cases involving neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders or Susac syndrome, which may satisfy MS MRI criteria [25]. Furthermore, tumefactive demyelinating lesions (TDLs) pose diagnostic dilemmas, as they can mimic brain tumors, leading to delayed diagnosis or unnecessary intervention if not correctly identified [26]. The ability to accurately distinguish between "rim" lesions, characterized by deposited paramagnetic iron, and "non-rim" lesion types using quantitative susceptibility maps provides adequate contrast to improve diagnostic accuracy [4].
The identification of specific biomarkers, such as the central vein sign (CVS) and the paramagnetic rim sign (PRS), has emerged as a promising tool for both diagnosis and prognosis [27]. These markers have demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity in distinguishing MS from radiologically isolated syndrome (RIS), a condition where imaging reveals lesions suggestive of demyelinating disease without consistent clinical episodes [27]. Given the uncertain prognosis for RIS patients, with some remaining asymptomatic while others progress to MS, the use of these biomarkers helps in stratifying risk, particularly in patients with risk factors such as male sex, younger age at diagnosis, and spinal cord lesions [27].
In the context of treatment and clinical trials, MRI biomarkers are increasingly utilized to assess the response of patients with relapsing–remitting MS to available treatments [24]. Additionally, MRI indicators of neurodegeneration show potential as primary and secondary endpoints in clinical trials targeting progressive phenotypes [24]. The development of synthetic data generation methods, such as generative adversarial networks (GANs), further enhances clinical utility by addressing the class imbalance between rare rim lesions and common non-rim lesions [4]. By generating synthetic quantitative susceptibility maps and pseudo-healthy images, these methods improve the performance of automated segmentation and classification networks, which are essential for monitoring longitudinal changes and detecting anomalies in serial imaging [20][28][29]. This automated change detection is critical for clinical decision-making, as lesion changes on serial imaging can significantly impact management strategies [29].

# Limitations

The application of advanced generative models and diagnostic criteria in the context of multiple sclerosis (MS) lesion analysis faces several inherent limitations. While the 2017 McDonald criteria provide updated guidance for clinicians and researchers, they continue to apply primarily to patients experiencing a typical clinically isolated syndrome and require the demonstration of dissemination in time and space, which may not capture all atypical presentations[30]. Furthermore, the reliance on specific markers, such as CSF-specific oligoclonal bands, to fulfill diagnostic requirements introduces constraints in cases where these markers are absent or inconclusive[30].
In the domain of synthetic generation and image processing, traditional methods for text-to-image synthesis often struggle with the high cost of meticulously curated paired image-text datasets and face difficulties in achieving precise control over fine-grained visual attributes and intricate spatial relationships[13]. Although novel approaches like Hierarchical Self-Supervised LVLM (Hi-SSLVLM) aim to mitigate these issues through self-supervised learning, the challenge of aligning hierarchical captions without extensive human annotation remains a significant hurdle for cultivating deep internal semantic understanding[13].
Additionally, recent diffusion-based models, while capable of achieving photorealism in image inpainting, are limited by their requirement for many sampling steps, which restricts their practical utility in time-sensitive clinical applications[31]. Few-step text-to-image models offer faster generation but often result in poor harmonization and artifacts between the background and the inpainted region when naively applied to inpainting tasks[31]. This issue is largely attributed to random Gaussian noise initialization, which causes semantic misalignment and reduced fidelity under low function evaluations[31]. Consequently, even with proposed solutions like InverFill that inject semantic information into initial noise, the balance between generation speed and high-fidelity reconstruction remains a critical limitation in the synthetic generation of complex medical images such as rim lesions[31].
