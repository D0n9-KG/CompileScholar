# Overview

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a significant paradigm in the training and inference of large language models (LLMs), offering a departure from the traditional next-token prediction (NTP) approach. While NTP is the dominant method for LLM training, it is inherently sequential, which constrains both contextual coverage and inference efficiency [1]. MTP addresses these limitations by enabling models to predict multiple future tokens simultaneously, thereby enhancing the model's ability to capture long-range dependencies and accelerating the decoding process [1].
The integration of MTP into language model architectures has been driven by the need to improve sample efficiency and address the bottlenecks associated with autoregressive decoding. For instance, in the context of vision-language models (VLMs), the autoregressive nature of decoding creates a significant speed bottleneck for tasks such as document parsing. Methods like Parallel-Token Prediction (PTP) have been proposed to allow VLMs to generate multiple future tokens in parallel, improving sample efficiency without requiring complex architectural changes [2]. Similarly, in the domain of pure text LLMs, research has shown that models exhibit latent MTP capabilities even when trained solely for next-token generation. Training-free approaches have been developed to probe these latent capabilities, constructing speculative token trees that allow for parallel verification of candidate predictions, resulting in lossless generation with substantially reduced model calls [3].
However, the implementation of MTP as an auxiliary training objective has presented challenges. Early studies indicated that MTP showed inconsistent improvements and often underperformed in standard NLP benchmarks when used as an auxiliary loss alongside NTP [4]. This was attributed to the difficulty of exact future token prediction as a training signal. To address this, alternative objectives such as Token Order Prediction (TOP) were proposed, which train models to order upcoming tokens by their proximity using a learning-to-rank loss. TOP has been shown to outperform standard NTP, MTP, and other variants like DeepSeek MTP across various benchmarks, even at scale [4].
Recent advancements have further refined MTP through innovative mechanisms such as Leap Multi-Token Prediction (L-MTP). Unlike conventional MTP, which generates tokens at adjacent positions, L-MTP introduces a leap-based mechanism that strategically skips over intermediate tokens to predict non-sequential ones in a single forward pass. This structured approach not only enhances the capture of long-range dependencies but also enables decoding strategies specifically optimized for non-sequential token generation, effectively accelerating inference [1].
The efficiency gains from MTP are also evident in specialized model architectures designed for end-side devices. For example, MiniCPM4 utilizes systematic innovations in model architecture, including trainable sparse attention mechanisms, to accelerate both prefilling and decoding phases for long-context processing [5]. These developments highlight the broader trend of integrating MTP and related parallel prediction techniques to overcome the sequential constraints of traditional LLM training and inference, aiming for higher throughput and better performance in diverse applications ranging from general language modeling to specialized document parsing and medical text analysis [5][2][6].

# Methodology

The methodology for pre-training curricula in multi-token prediction (MTP) centers on addressing the inherent difficulties smaller language models (SLMs) face when adopting MTP objectives, which often struggle to leverage the benefits of predicting multiple future tokens simultaneously. To mitigate these challenges, researchers have proposed curriculum learning strategies that systematically adjust the complexity of the pre-training objective. A primary approach involves a **forward curriculum**, which gradually increases the complexity of the training task from standard next-token prediction (NTP) to full MTP. This progressive exposure allows SLMs to better internalize the MTP objective during pre-training, leading to improved downstream NTP performance and generative quality compared to models trained with MTP from the outset [7]. Conversely, a **reverse curriculum** has been explored, which starts with the more complex MTP objective and gradually simplifies it to NTP, though the forward approach has shown particular efficacy in enabling SLMs to utilize MTP effectively [7].
Beyond simple curriculum scheduling, methodological innovations have focused on reformulating the prediction objective to better align with model capabilities and computational constraints. One such method is **Token Order Prediction (TOP)**, which replaces the exact future token prediction of standard MTP with a learning-to-rank loss that trains models to order upcoming tokens by their proximity. This approach is more computationally efficient, requiring only a single additional unembedding layer rather than the multiple transformer layers typically associated with MTP heads. Experiments across model scales (340M, 1.8B, and 7B parameters) indicate that TOP outperforms standard NTP, MTP, and DeepSeek MTP (DS-MTP) on standard NLP benchmarks, even at scale [4].
Another significant methodological development is **Leap Multi-Token Prediction (L-MTP)**, which extends conventional MTP by introducing a leap-based mechanism. Unlike standard MTP, which generates tokens at adjacent positions, L-MTP strategically skips intermediate tokens to predict non-sequential ones in a single forward pass. This structured leap enhances the model's ability to capture long-range dependencies and enables a decoding strategy optimized for non-sequential generation, thereby accelerating inference [1]. Similarly, **Future Summary Prediction (FSP)** addresses the limitations of MTP in capturing long-horizon reasoning by training an auxiliary head to predict a compact representation of the long-term future. FSP explores two variants: handcrafted summaries (such as bag-of-words) and learned summaries generated by a reverse language model trained in right-to-left order. Large-scale pre-training experiments with 3B and 8B-parameter models demonstrate that FSP preserves information relevant for long-form generations, offering improvements over standard MTP which primarily captures short-range dependencies [8].
In the context of vision-language models (VLMs), **Parallel-Token Prediction (PTP)** has been introduced as a plugable, model-agnostic method to enable parallel decoding for tasks like document parsing. PTP inserts learnable tokens into the input sequence and designs corresponding training objectives to equip the model with parallel decoding capabilities, supported by a comprehensive data generation pipeline for high-quality training data [2]. Furthermore, to address the instability often observed during the training of these complex objectives, methodological frameworks have been developed to prevent loss spikes. These include analyzing the spectral norms of Jacobian matrices to ensure small sub-layers and large shortcuts, thereby stabilizing the pre-training process [9]. Additionally, understanding the mechanism of Adam optimizer spikes, where adaptive preconditioners can trigger instability when squared gradients are significantly smaller than second-order moment estimates, has informed strategies to maintain training stability during the adoption of these advanced prediction objectives [10].

# Theoretical Foundations

The theoretical underpinnings of Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) in language models are rooted in the analysis of gradient inductive biases and their impact on internal representation learning. While conventional Next-Token Prediction (NTP) focuses on one-step-ahead supervision, MTP has been shown to promote the convergence toward internal belief states by inducing representational contractivity via gradient coupling.[11] This mechanism suggests that MTP encourages the development of more structured representations compared to standard autoregressive approaches. However, theoretical and empirical analyses reveal that standard MTP implementations often suffer from structural hallucinations, where discrete token supervision encourages illegal shortcuts in latent space that violate environmental constraints.[11] To address these theoretical limitations, methods such as Latent Semantic Enhancement MTP (LSE-MTP) have been proposed to anchor predictions to ground-truth hidden state trajectories, thereby mitigating the risks associated with unstructured latent space exploration.[11]
Beyond representation learning, the efficiency of pre-training dynamics is governed by the relationship between batch size, training steps, and data consumption. The concept of Critical Batch Size, originally pioneered by OpenAI, served as a foundational principle for large-scale pre-training; however, this framework fails to align with the dynamics introduced by the Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD) learning rate scheduler.[12] Recent theoretical work has derived a revised E(S) relationship tailored for the WSD scheduler, characterizing the trade-off between training data consumption (E) and steps (S).[12] This analysis identifies two fundamental properties of WSD-based pre-training: $B_{min}$, the minimum batch size threshold required to achieve a target loss, and $B_{opt}$, the optimal batch size that maximizes data efficiency by minimizing total tokens.[12] These properties provide a theoretical basis for dynamic batch size strategies that optimize the pre-training curriculum for MTP architectures.
Furthermore, the acquisition of natural language capabilities through pre-training is influenced by the inductive biases imparted by formal languages. Theoretical insights from linguistics and complexity theory suggest that effective transfer occurs when a formal language captures the dependency structures present in natural language while remaining within the computational limitations of the model architecture.[13] Experiments on transformers indicate that pre-pretraining on formal languages that capture hierarchical dependencies enables models to achieve lower loss on natural language and better linguistic generalization compared to other formal languages.[13] This supports the hypothesis that the structural complexity of the pre-training data must align with the model's capacity to effectively learn the underlying dependencies required for multi-token prediction tasks.
The internal mechanisms of decoder-only language models, particularly their capacity for planning and organizing intermediate computations, also form a critical part of the theoretical landscape. Planning involves structuring computations over long horizons and considering multiple possible continuations, a capability that is not fully understood in transformer-based models without external scaffolds.[14] By analyzing hidden states, which act as carriers of information, researchers have developed pipelines using vector-quantized variational autoencoders to compress these redundant representations into compact summary codes.[14] This compression aids in understanding how models organize intermediate results to support coherent long-range generation, a process essential for the success of multi-token prediction strategies that rely on internal state consistency. Additionally, the identification of "concept neurons" through concept vectors has provided a method to locate significant neurons responsible for representing specific concepts, reducing the computational complexity of forward passes from $O(n \cdot m)$ to $O(n)$, where $n$ is the number of neurons and $m$ is the number of examples.[15] This optimization in interpretability and computation further supports the theoretical framework for efficient and structured pre-training curricula.

# Experimental Results

Experimental evaluations of multi-token prediction (MTP) and related parallel decoding strategies demonstrate significant improvements in both training efficiency and inference speed across various model architectures and tasks. In the domain of chemical reaction prediction, a self-supervised pretraining strategy utilizing MTP principles allowed transformer models to absorb chemical information from approximately 1 billion molecules. When fine-tuned on small-scale reaction prediction tasks, this approach yielded average accuracy increases of 5.7% for Baeyer-Villiger, 10.8% for Heck, and 4.8% for Sharpless asymmetric epoxidation reactions, marking a substantial advancement in low-resource reaction prediction[16].
In the context of large language model (LLM) training, MTP has been shown to enhance reasoning capabilities compared to standard next-token prediction (NTP). Empirical studies indicate that MTP consistently outperforms NTP on synthetic graph path-finding tasks and realistic reasoning benchmarks, such as Countdown and boolean satisfiability problems[17]. Theoretically, this improvement is attributed to a gradient decoupling property that induces a two-stage reverse reasoning process, allowing the model to attend to end nodes and reconstruct paths by tracing intermediate nodes backward[17]. However, the effectiveness of MTP as an auxiliary objective can be inconsistent; some studies found that exact future token prediction in MTP is too difficult as an auxiliary loss, leading to underperformance in standard NLP benchmarks compared to alternative objectives like token order prediction (TOP)[4]. TOP, which uses a learning-to-rank loss to order upcoming tokens, was shown to outperform NTP, MTP, and DeepSeek MTP across nine standard NLP benchmarks for models ranging from 340M to 7B parameters[4].
Regarding inference acceleration, several methods leveraging MTP have demonstrated notable speedups. FastMTP, which aligns MTP training with inference patterns to improve multi-step draft quality, significantly enhances speculative decoding performance by maintaining high acceptance rates across multiple recursive draft steps[18]. Similarly, P(arallel)-EAGLE transforms EAGLE from autoregressive to parallel multi-token prediction via a learnable shared hidden state. Implemented in vLLM, P-EAGLE achieved speedups of 1.10–1.36x over autoregressive EAGLE-3 across GPT-OSS 120B, 20B, and Qwen3-Coder 30B models[19]. In the field of document parsing, Parallel-Token Prediction (PTP) enables vision-language models to generate multiple future tokens in parallel, effectively mitigating the bottleneck of autoregressive decoding and improving parsing speed on benchmarks such as OmniDocBench and olmOCR-bench[2].
Furthermore, training-free approaches have also shown promise. A probing-based MTP method that uses on-the-fly mask tokens to predict future tokens in parallel without modifying model weights consistently outperformed existing training-free baselines, increasing acceptance length and improving token throughput across various benchmarks[3]. Additionally, Leap Multi-Token Prediction (L-MTP), which predicts non-sequential tokens by skipping intermediate positions, has been proposed to enhance the capture of long-range dependencies and accelerate inference through optimized decoding strategies[1]. These results collectively highlight the versatility of MTP in addressing both the computational costs of training and the latency constraints of inference in modern machine learning applications.

# Comparison with Related Work

Multi-token prediction (MTP) has emerged as a significant paradigm in language model research, addressing the inherent limitations of standard autoregressive next-token prediction (NTP) in both training efficiency and inference speed. While NTP is the dominant training objective for causal decoder-only transformers, it restricts contextual coverage and results in slow, sequential generation processes [20][1][21]. Recent advancements in MTP can be categorized into training objectives, inference acceleration techniques, and architectural modifications, each offering distinct advantages and trade-offs compared to the proposed pre-training curriculum.
**Training Objectives and Auxiliary Losses**
Several studies have explored MTP as an auxiliary objective to enhance the quality of next-token prediction. However, direct MTP objectives have shown inconsistent improvements, often underperforming in standard NLP benchmarks due to the difficulty of exact future token prediction [4]. To mitigate this, Token Order Prediction (TOP) was proposed, which trains models to order upcoming tokens by proximity using a learning-to-rank loss rather than exact prediction, requiring fewer additional parameters than standard MTP [4]. Similarly, Future Token Prediction (FTP) investigates generating embedding vectors that capture the meaning of longer sequences of future text, aligning with cognitive theories that humans predict multiple upcoming words rather than just one [22]. In contrast, the proposed curriculum focuses on leveraging the MTP paradigm to specifically enhance the learning of transitive relations, a cornerstone for complex planning that standard LLMs often struggle to master [23].
**Inference Acceleration and Decoding Strategies**
A primary motivation for MTP is to accelerate inference by breaking the token-by-token generation bottleneck. Parallel Token Prediction (PTP) proposes a framework where multiple tokens are predicted in a single model call by moving the source of randomness to input variables, achieving significant speedups without requiring auxiliary speculator models [24]. Similarly, online distillation methods have been developed to convert pretrained autoregressive models into standalone multi-token prediction models, retaining the original implementation while decoding more than 3x faster with minimal accuracy loss [20]. Other approaches, such as Leap Multi-Token Prediction (L-MTP), introduce a leap-based mechanism to predict non-sequential tokens, enhancing the capture of long-range dependencies and optimizing decoding for non-sequential generation [1]. Furthermore, training-free methods have been proposed that probe existing LLMs using mask tokens to construct speculative token trees, enabling parallel prediction and lossless generation without modifying model weights [3]. While these methods primarily target inference efficiency, the proposed work emphasizes the theoretical benefits of MTP in learning unobserved transitive reachability relations during the pre-training phase [23].
**Architectural and Joint Modeling Approaches**
Beyond pure text generation, MTP concepts have been integrated into broader architectural frameworks. In speech language models, the choice of decoding paradigm, such as interleaved versus parallel generation, significantly impacts alignment quality and efficiency, with recent work proposing early-stop interleaved patterns to balance speed and performance [25]. Additionally, novel frameworks have incorporated multitoken prediction interaction modules alongside learnable dense residual connections and TransformerX modules to address issues like gradient vanishing and complex feature interactions [26]. In the domain of reinforcement learning, Multi-token Policy Gradient Optimization (MPO) treats sequences of consecutive tokens as unified semantic actions, bridging the gap between token-level optimization and the block-level nature of complex reasoning tasks [27]. These diverse applications highlight the versatility of multi-token concepts, yet the proposed pre-training curriculum specifically targets the theoretical and practical enhancement of transitive relation learning through a structured MTP approach [23].

# Limitations and Challenges

The integration of multi-token prediction (MTP) into pre-training curricula for large language models (LLMs) faces several significant technical and practical challenges, ranging from computational complexity to data availability and architectural constraints.
**Computational and Training Complexity**
A primary limitation of parallel drafting and multi-token prediction is the quadratic scaling of training complexity with respect to the product of sequence length and parallel positions. This scaling behavior renders long-context training impractical for many standard architectures, as the computational cost grows rapidly with the number of tokens predicted simultaneously [19]. To mitigate this, specialized frameworks such as P-EAGLE have been developed, which utilize attention mask pre-computation and sequence partitioning techniques to enable gradient accumulation within individual sequences [19]. However, these optimizations require significant engineering effort and specific implementation details, such as those found in vLLM, to achieve speedups over autoregressive baselines [19]. Furthermore, the shift from autoregressive to parallel multi-token prediction often necessitates architectural modifications, such as learnable shared hidden states, to transform the model's prediction mechanism effectively [19].
**Data Scarcity and Curriculum Design**
Effective curriculum learning for MTP relies on the availability of diverse and structured data, which poses challenges in low-resource settings. Existing curriculum approaches often require sampling sufficient amounts of "easy" samples from training data at early stages, a condition that is not always achievable for low-resource languages or specialized domains where data volume is limited [28]. To address this, token-wise curriculum learning has been proposed, where models learn to predict short sub-sequences that gradually expand, leveraging the cumulative effect of translation errors to define difficulty [28]. Additionally, the diversity and structure of data, rather than volume alone, have become limiting factors in sustaining performance gains, particularly when using reinforcement learning paradigms to refine MTP capabilities [29]. Synthetic data generation pipelines, such as those using multi-turn teacher-student interactions, are increasingly necessary to produce structured difficulty progressions and "stepping stones" that support curriculum-based training without relying solely on natural data distributions [29].
**Architectural and Theoretical Misalignments**
The theoretical foundations of pre-training, such as the Critical Batch Size concept, may not align with the dynamics introduced by new scheduling methods like Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD) when applied to MTP or complex pre-training regimes [12]. The original theoretical frameworks often fail to capture the trade-offs between training data consumption and steps in these new contexts, necessitating revised relationships to characterize optimal batch sizes and data efficiency [12]. Moreover, standard transformer architectures face inherent challenges such as low computational efficiency and gradient vanishing, which can hinder the capture of complex feature interactions required for robust multi-token prediction [26]. Novel frameworks incorporating learnable dense residual skip connections and modules like TransformerX, which integrate multiscale convolution and adaptive activation functions, have been proposed to enhance information flow and feature capture across layers, addressing these architectural limitations [26].
**Inference Efficiency and Model Behavior**
While MTP aims to improve efficiency, it can introduce challenges in inference behavior and computational resource management. Reasoning LLMs, which often benefit from MTP, produce longer outputs that require drafters trained on extended sequences, potentially increasing memory and latency demands [19]. Conversely, some approaches attempt to reduce excess computation by teaching models to truncate forward passes early using reinforcement learning, but this requires careful calibration to maintain task performance while incentivizing early exits [30]. Balancing the need for deep computation for non-myopic planning with the efficiency of early exits remains an open challenge, particularly when scaling these techniques to larger models [30]. Additionally, the stability of training dynamics can be affected by the ordering of data; while curricula can change the time spent in latent training phases, they may also introduce higher gradient noise scales or output-head saturation if not carefully designed, as observed in studies comparing random ordering against linguistically motivated curricula [31].
