# summary

**Large Language Model-Enhanced Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation** (LLM-CDSR) is an emerging subfield of recommender systems that integrates Large Language Models (LLMs) to improve the prediction of user interactions across multiple domains. Traditional Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) aims to mitigate data sparsity and the cold-start problem by transferring knowledge between domains, but it often suffers from the "overlap dilemma," where models rely heavily on users with interactions in all domains, and the "transition complexity" of learning mixed behavior sequences [1][2]. By leveraging the powerful representation, reasoning, and world knowledge capabilities of LLMs, recent frameworks seek to bridge semantic gaps between heterogeneous domains, thereby capturing comprehensive user preferences that are often obscured within single-domain interactions [2][3].
The integration of LLMs addresses several critical limitations of conventional CDSR methods, including the "seesaw effect," where performance in one domain improves at the expense of another due to gradient conflicts, and the "imbalance issue," where dominant domains obscure specific features of others [1][3]. LLMs function as both generators and encoders to provide robust semantic views of user behavior, alleviating reliance on overlapping users and improving cross-domain alignment [2][3]. For instance, models such as **LLM4CDSR** utilize LLMs to obtain semantic item representations to address transition complexity, while **LLM-EMF** and **IFCDSR** enhance textual and visual data using frozen CLIP models to improve multimodal item representations [4][2][5]. In zero-shot scenarios, LLMs facilitate knowledge transfer to unseen domains through rich, pretrained representations, although challenges such as domain semantic bias due to vocabulary differences remain a persistent issue [6].
Methodologically, LLM-enhanced CDSR frameworks employ diverse strategies to optimize knowledge transfer, including semantic tag generation, multimodal fusion, and model merging. **TEMA-LLM** uses LLMs to generate domain-aware tags from item descriptions, fusing these with visual and identifier features to capture cross-domain patterns [7]. **X-Cross** integrates domain-specific language models fine-tuned with low-rank adapters (LoRA) to dynamically refine representations while preserving domain-specific nuances [8]. Additionally, **MergeRec** addresses privacy constraints in data-isolated settings by using model merging techniques to transfer knowledge without sharing raw user interaction data [9]. Other approaches, such as **SpecTran**, propose spectral transformations to effectively inject high-dimensional LLM embeddings into sequential models, avoiding the dimension collapse seen in traditional adapter-based methods [10].
Experimental evaluations indicate that LLM-based recommenders can outperform conventional systems, with studies reporting up to a 5% improvement in Area Under the Curve (AUC) metrics for click-through rate prediction across diverse datasets [11]. However, the effectiveness of these models depends on carefully designed workflows that incorporate reasoning, retrieval-augmented generation, and specific alignment mechanisms to handle domain heterogeneity [12]. Challenges remain regarding computational overhead for on-device deployment, the risk of LLM hallucinations leading to performance deterioration, and the need for robust denoising mechanisms to handle noisy user interactions [13][14][15]. Despite these hurdles, the trend toward combining LLM-derived semantic information with behavioral data continues to drive the development of more robust and adaptable cross-domain recommendation systems [16].

# Background

Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) is a specialized area of recommender systems designed to predict future user interactions by leveraging historical behavior across multiple domains. The primary objective of CDSR is to enhance recommendation quality by transferring knowledge between domains, thereby offering effective solutions to common challenges such as data sparsity and the cold-start problem [1][17]. By modeling cross-domain preferences and capturing both intra-sequence and inter-sequence item relationships, CDSR aims to extract comprehensive user preferences that are often obscured within single-domain interactions [4][2].
Despite its potential, CDSR faces several significant limitations that hinder its practical application and performance. A major challenge is the "overlap dilemma," where existing methods heavily rely on users who have interactions in all domains to learn cross-domain item relationships, which compromises the system's practicability in real-world scenarios [2]. Additionally, the "transition complexity" or "transition issue" refers to the difficulty in learning complex transition patterns from mixed behavior sequences, often leading to poor next-item prediction performance for specific domains [2][3]. Other critical issues include the "imbalance issue," where interactions in one domain may dominate the entire behavior, making it difficult to capture domain-specific features in others [3]. Furthermore, existing methods often overlook varying contexts in user interaction sequences, resulting in spurious correlations that obscure true causal relationships, and they may suffer from gradient conflicts between domains, leading to a "seesaw effect" where performance in one domain improves at the expense of another [1].
To address these challenges, recent research has increasingly turned to Large Language Models (LLMs) to enhance CDSR. LLMs possess powerful representation and reasoning abilities, as well as rich world knowledge, which allow them to bridge items and capture user preferences from a semantic perspective [2][3]. By integrating LLMs, researchers aim to alleviate the overlap and transition issues by providing a more robust semantic view of user behavior [2]. For instance, LLMs can act as both generators and encoders to improve cross-domain alignment and knowledge transfer [3].
Several novel frameworks have been proposed to leverage LLMs in this context. LLM4CDSR utilizes LLMs to obtain semantic item representations, addressing the overlap dilemma and transition complexity [2]. LLM-EMF enhances textual information with LLM knowledge and fuses it with visual data from frozen CLIP models to improve multimodal item representations [4]. Similarly, IFCDSR incorporates item image information via a frozen CLIP model to enrich original item embeddings, using multiple attention layers to capture cross-domain interests [5]. In the realm of zero-shot cross-domain sequential recommendation (ZCDSR), which enables predictions in unseen domains without additional training, LLMs facilitate cross-domain knowledge transfer through rich, pretrained representations [6]. However, challenges such as domain semantic bias, arising from differences in vocabulary and content focus, remain a persistent issue, leading to misaligned item embeddings [6].
Beyond direct LLM integration, other approaches focus on optimizing the transfer of knowledge. The X-Cross model integrates several domain-specific language models fine-tuned with low-rank adapters (LoRA) to dynamically refine representations and preserve domain-specific nuances while enabling adaptability [8]. CoDiS proposes a context-aware disentanglement framework grounded in a causal view to accurately disentangle domain-shared and domain-specific preferences, addressing gradient conflicts and the reliance on user overlap [1]. Additionally, methods like LEKA focus on active knowledge transfer by autonomously retrieving suitable knowledge sources to enrich the target domain, moving beyond passive knowledge acquisition [18]. These developments highlight a growing trend toward combining semantic information from LLMs with behavioral data to create more robust and adaptable cross-domain recommendation systems [16].

# Methodology

The methodology for Large Language Model (LLM)-enhanced cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) focuses on bridging semantic gaps between domains, mitigating data sparsity, and aligning heterogeneous user behaviors. Recent frameworks employ diverse strategies, including semantic tag generation, multimodal fusion, model merging, and spectral embedding transformations, to improve generalization and capture complex cross-domain preferences.

## Semantic Enrichment and Tag Generation

A primary methodological approach involves leveraging the semantic capabilities of LLMs to enrich item representations, particularly in scenarios where collaborative signals are scarce. **TEMA-LLM** (Tag-Enriched Multi-Attention with Large Language Models) utilizes LLMs to assign domain-aware prompts and generate descriptive tags from item titles and descriptions. These tag embeddings are subsequently fused with item identifiers, textual, and visual features to construct enhanced item representations that capture both domain-specific and cross-domain behavioral patterns [7]. Similarly, **LLMSeR** addresses the long-tail user dilemma in sequential recommender systems by using LLMs to generate pseudo-prior items. This data augmentation strategy helps improve recommendation efficacy for users with limited interaction records by leveraging the LLM's ability to discern semantic relationships among items [19].
In the context of zero-shot cross-domain sequential recommendation (ZCDSR), where predictions must be made in unseen domains without additional training, methodologies focus on addressing **domain semantic bias**. This bias arises from differences in vocabulary and content focus between domains, leading to misaligned item embeddings. To counter this, semantic bias-aware frameworks introduce generalization losses that align item embeddings across domains at both the item and sequential levels, thereby enhancing cross-domain alignment and improving generalization [6].

## Multimodal Fusion and Embedding Transformation

Integrating multimodal data with LLM-derived semantic information is another critical methodological trend. **LLM-EMF** (LLM-Enhanced Multimodal Fusion) enhances textual information with LLM knowledge and fuses it with visual data to improve recommendation performance. By using a frozen CLIP model to generate image and text embeddings, this approach enriches item representations with multimodal data. A multiple attention mechanism is then employed to jointly learn single-domain and cross-domain preferences, effectively capturing complex user interests across diverse domains [4]. **IFCDSR** (Image Fusion for Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation) similarly incorporates item image information to capture visual preferences. It integrates a frozen CLIP model to generate image embeddings, which are fused with original item embeddings from both intra-sequence and inter-sequence interactions. A multiple attention layer is used to capture cross-domain interests, enabling the joint learning of user preferences [5].
To address the limitations of traditional embedding transformation strategies, such as dimension collapse in adapter-based methods and rigidity in SVD-based methods, **SpecTran** proposes a spectral transformation approach. This method aims to inject high-dimensional semantic embeddings from LLMs into sequential recommendation models more effectively, preserving rich information across the spectrum rather than discarding it [10]. Additionally, **TRAWL** (External Knowledge-Enhanced Recommendation with LLM Assistance) utilizes LLMs to extract relevant recommendation knowledge from raw external data. It employs a contrastive learning strategy for adapter training to denoise raw external knowledge and adapt semantic representations, thereby enriching behavioral-based recommender systems [16].

## Model Merging and Cross-Attention Mechanisms

Methodologies that operate at the model level often focus on integrating knowledge from multiple domain-specific models. **X-Cross** is a novel cross-domain sequential-recommendation model that integrates several domain-specific language models, each fine-tuned with low-rank adapters (LoRA). Operating layer by layer, X-Cross dynamically refines the representation of each source language model by integrating knowledge from all other models. These refined representations are propagated through layers, leveraging activations from each domain adapter to preserve domain-specific nuances while enabling adaptability across new domains [8]. **MergeRec** proposes a framework based on model merging under a "data-isolated" problem setting, where raw user interaction data cannot be shared across domains due to privacy constraints. This approach allows for the generalization of recommender systems across multiple domains without relying on overlapping users or items [9].
The internal mechanisms of cross-attention, widely used to enhance alignment in CDSR, have also been re-examined. While traditionally interpreted as **residual alignment**—where output is generated by removing redundant information from the query input by referencing another domain's data—research has identified a phenomenon termed **Orthogonal Alignment**. In this mechanism, cross-attention discovers novel information not present in the query input. Studies indicate that these two contrasting alignment mechanisms can co-exist, and model performance improves when the query input and output of cross-attention are orthogonal [20].

## Agent-Based Evaluation and World Modeling

Evaluating the effectiveness of these recommendation systems remains challenging due to the gap between offline metrics and real user behavior. **AlignUSER** introduces a framework that learns world-model-driven agents from human interactions to serve as synthetic users for evaluation. This approach formalizes world modeling as a next-state prediction task, helping agents internalize the environment. To align actions with human personas, the framework generates counterfactual trajectories around demonstrations and prompts LLMs to compare their decisions with human choices, identify suboptimal actions, and extract lessons. This method provides a more faithful reproduction of user actions compared to traditional few-shot prompting approaches [21].

# Experimental Evaluation

Experimental evaluations of Large Language Model (LLM)-enhanced cross-domain sequential recommendation frameworks have focused on validating the efficacy of these models in addressing data sparsity, improving generalization, and overcoming domain-specific biases. These studies typically employ benchmark datasets from diverse domains, including fashion, news, video, books, and music, to assess performance metrics such as click-through rate (CTR) prediction and sequential recommendation accuracy.[11]
**Performance in Cross- and Zero-Shot Settings**
Recent experiments demonstrate that LLM-based recommenders can outperform conventional systems, with some studies reporting up to a 5% improvement in AUC when utilizing comprehensive item representation forms such as semantic embeddings and identifiers.[11] In the context of zero-shot cross-domain sequential recommendation (ZCDSR), where models must predict in unseen domains without fine-tuning, LLMs have shown significant potential by leveraging rich, pretrained representations to facilitate knowledge transfer.[6] However, evaluations have also highlighted persistent challenges, such as domain semantic bias, which arises from differences in vocabulary and content focus between domains, leading to misaligned item embeddings.[6] To mitigate this, frameworks like the semantic bias-aware approach have been tested, showing improved cross-domain alignment at both item and sequential levels.[6]
**Impact of Model Merging and Data Isolation**
A critical area of experimental focus is the application of model merging techniques under realistic constraints, such as data-isolated cross-domain settings where raw user interaction data cannot be shared.[9] The MergeRec framework, for instance, was evaluated using components like merging initialization and pseudo-user data construction, demonstrating the viability of transferring knowledge without explicit overlaps in users or items.[9] Conversely, empirical studies have revealed that naively training an LLM on combined domains or simply merging domain-specific LLMs can degrade performance relative to models trained solely on the target domain, indicating that sophisticated merging strategies are necessary to avoid suboptimal outcomes.[22] Similarly, the X-Cross model, which integrates domain-specific language models fine-tuned with low-rank adapters (LoRA), achieved performance comparable to fully retrained models on Amazon datasets, validating the effectiveness of dynamic representation refinement across layers.[8]
**Denoising and Data Augmentation**
Experiments have also investigated the role of LLMs in enhancing data quality and quantity. In sequential recommendation, noisy interactions often impair performance; LLMs have been employed to identify and denoise these interactions, although challenges remain regarding the reliability of LLM outputs and the generation of nonsensical responses without proper fine-tuning.[13] Furthermore, to address the long-tail user dilemma, frameworks like LLMSeR have been evaluated for their ability to generate pseudo-prior items, thereby augmenting sparse interaction records and improving recommendation efficacy for users with limited history.[19] These studies collectively suggest that while LLMs offer powerful tools for cross-domain recommendation, their success depends on carefully designed workflows that incorporate reasoning, retrieval-augmented generation, and specific alignment mechanisms to handle domain heterogeneity.[12]

# Results and Analysis

Recent empirical studies indicate that Large Language Models (LLMs) significantly enhance the performance of cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) systems, often outperforming conventional recommender architectures. In comprehensive benchmarks such as RecBench, which evaluates various item representation forms across five diverse datasets including fashion, news, video, books, and music, LLM-based recommenders have demonstrated superior capabilities compared to traditional models, achieving up to a 5% improvement in Area Under the Curve (AUC) metrics for click-through rate prediction and sequential recommendation tasks [11]. This performance gain is attributed to the LLMs' ability to leverage rich, pretrained representations to facilitate cross-domain knowledge transfer, particularly in zero-shot scenarios where models must predict in unseen domains without additional fine-tuning [6].
The integration of LLMs addresses specific limitations inherent in traditional CDSR methods, such as data sparsity and the "seesaw effect," where improving performance in one domain often degrades it in another due to gradient conflicts [1]. By acting as both generators and encoders, LLMs help alleviate imbalance issues where interactions in one domain might dominate user behavior, thereby improving the capture of domain-specific features [3]. For instance, frameworks like TEMA-LLM utilize LLMs to generate domain-aware semantic tags from item titles and descriptions, fusing these tag embeddings with item identifiers and visual features to construct enhanced item representations that better capture both domain-specific and cross-domain behavioral patterns [7]. Similarly, the LLMSeR framework utilizes LLMs to generate pseudo-prior items, effectively mitigating the long-tail user dilemma and the absence of collaborative signals in sequential recommender systems [19].
Furthermore, analysis of the underlying mechanisms reveals that LLMs contribute to CDSR through distinct alignment processes. Research into cross-attention mechanisms has identified "Orthogonal Alignment," a phenomenon where the model discovers novel information not present in the query input, which co-exists with the more common "residual alignment" mechanism [20]. This dual mechanism allows for more robust cross-domain alignment, addressing the challenge of domain semantic bias caused by differences in vocabulary and content focus between domains [6]. Additionally, methods such as TRAWL demonstrate that LLMs can effectively denoise raw external knowledge and adapt semantic representations through contrastive learning strategies, validating the effectiveness of combining semantic information with behavioral data in real-world online recommender systems [16]. These findings collectively suggest that LLMs not only improve quantitative metrics but also provide a more nuanced understanding of user preferences by actively accessing and transferring relevant knowledge across heterogeneous domains [18].

# Discussion

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR) represents a significant paradigm shift, addressing long-standing limitations in traditional recommender systems. While LLMs offer robust semantic reasoning and generalization capabilities, their application in cross-domain settings introduces complex challenges related to knowledge transfer, data privacy, and model efficiency. Recent research highlights that simply combining semantic information with behavioral data is insufficient; effective methods must navigate the difficulties of denoising raw external knowledge and adapting semantic representations to specific domains [16].
A primary concern in CDSR is the reliance on unrealistic assumptions, such as the existence of overlapping users or items across domains, which rarely holds in real-world scenarios [9][22][1]. To overcome this, frameworks like MergeRec have introduced the concept of data-isolated cross-domain sequential recommendation, where raw user interaction data cannot be shared across domains due to privacy constraints [9]. This approach utilizes model merging techniques, including merging initialization and pseudo-user data construction, to facilitate knowledge transfer without direct data sharing [9]. However, empirical studies indicate that naively training an LLM on combined domains or merging domain-specific LLMs can degrade performance compared to models trained solely on the target domain [22]. This suggests that the cause of suboptimal performance lies in the failure to correctly extract shared knowledge and align domain-specific sequences, often leading to prediction mismatches when current tokens and targets belong to different domains [17].
To mitigate these issues, advanced frameworks have proposed more nuanced strategies for knowledge extraction and alignment. For instance, the TRAWL method employs LLMs to extract relevant recommendation knowledge from raw external data and uses a contrastive learning strategy for adapter training, thereby addressing the challenges of denoising and semantic adaptation [16]. Similarly, the CoDiS framework addresses the "seesaw effect," where performance in one domain improves at the expense of another due to gradient conflicts, by using a context-aware disentanglement framework grounded in a causal view [1]. This approach aims to accurately disentangle domain-shared and domain-specific preferences, avoiding the spurious correlations that obscure true causal relationships in user preferences [1].
Furthermore, the quality of knowledge derived from LLMs remains a critical bottleneck. LLM-generated knowledge often suffers from hallucinations, content redundancy, and information homogenization, which can lead to performance deterioration if directly fed into recommendation models [14]. To counter this, the KSER framework introduces a knowledge selection and exploitation mechanism that filters high-quality knowledge from LLM outputs [14]. In the context of zero-shot cross-domain sequential recommendation (ZCDSR), domain semantic bias arising from vocabulary and content differences poses a persistent challenge [6]. Addressing this requires semantic bias-aware frameworks that enhance cross-domain alignment at both the item and sequential levels, such as through the use of generalization losses to align item embeddings across domains [6].
Beyond accuracy, the deployment of LLM-enhanced recommenders faces practical constraints, particularly regarding computational overhead and memory footprint. On-device recommendation is critical for applications requiring low latency and user privacy, yet the substantial resource requirements of LLMs make deployment on resource-constrained devices challenging [15]. The OD-LLM framework addresses this by integrating low-rank structural compression and other strategies to enable efficient on-device deployment for sequential recommendation tasks [15]. Additionally, the issue of noisy interactions in user sequences remains a significant impediment to performance. While LLMs offer a promising avenue for denoising due to their semantic reasoning abilities, direct application often yields nonsensical responses, and fine-tuned models may still lack reliability [13]. Therefore, future research must focus on developing robust denoising mechanisms that leverage LLM capabilities without introducing new sources of error.
