To address the statistical discrepancies between these data types, which can lead to near-zero solutions in naive alignment approaches, researchers employ self-supervised learning frameworks within shared encoders. # Applications

DNA foundation models have demonstrated significant utility across various domains of genomics, clinical medicine, and regulatory science, extending beyond simple sequence analysis to complex predictive tasks.

## Clinical Genomics and Disease Prediction

In the realm of clinical genomics, these models are increasingly applied to predict disease risks and treatment responses. For instance, in oncology, foundation models are being utilized to estimate mutation loads, a key predictor of response to cancer immunotherapy. A study developed a computational framework using a small set of 24 genes to estimate mutation load in lung adenocarcinoma, achieving high precision in independent validation cohorts, thereby offering a cost-effective alternative to whole-exome sequencing for clinical decision-making[9]. Additionally, deep learning systems leveraging genomic and clinical data have been applied to predict microsatellite instability (MSI) in colorectal cancer directly from histological slides, with multimodal inputs and multitask supervision further enhancing prediction accuracy[10].

## Variant Effect Prediction and Functional Genomics

A primary application of DNA foundation models is the prediction of the functional impact of genetic variants. Models such as Nucleotide Transformer (NT), DNABERT, and HyenaDNA have been systematically evaluated for their ability to predict the functional consequences of Single Nucleotide Variants (SNVs) using zero-shot scoring methods that do not require task-specific fine-tuning[5]. Research indicates that large multi-species models, particularly the Nucleotide Transformer, outperform other architectures in detecting functional consequences not only at the mutation site but also in adjacent regions, showing superior discriminative power across pathogenic, benign, and uncertain variant categories[5]. However, rigorous benchmarking has revealed that while models like Evo2 show promise, they possess systematic blind spots in short-range biological signals, such as codon usage bias, and may exhibit sensitivity to biologically neutral contextual features, raising concerns about their current clinical readiness for zero-shot pathogenicity prediction[7].

## Genomic Completion and Synthetic Data Generation

Foundation models are also being applied to address data sparsity and heterogeneity in genomic cohorts. CanBART, a generative foundation model trained on 144,000 tumor profiles, utilizes a masked-language architecture to learn tokenized representations of somatic alterations[11]. This model supports genomic completion, allowing for the reconstruction of missing gene data when sequencing panels differ in coverage. In simulated scenarios where data from a larger panel (MSK-IMPACT) was reduced to match a smaller panel (DFCI OncoPanel), CanBART successfully recovered over one-third of the missing genes, facilitating cohort comparison, biomarker discovery, and clinical-trial enrollment[11].

## Regulatory and Supervisory Frameworks

The application of AI in genomics and insurance has prompted regulatory scrutiny. In the United Kingdom, there is a growing focus on the prudential regulation and supervision of re-insurance undertakings in relation to AI considerations[12]. Critical analyses are being conducted to determine how the prudential provisions of the European Artificial Intelligence (AI) Act can be adjusted and adopted into the UK regulatory regime, specifically regarding risk management systems for re-insurers[12]. This regulatory development aims to address gaps in the supervisory approach to AI applications within the insurance value chain, ensuring that the use of advanced predictive models, including those in genomic profiling, is managed with appropriate prudential oversight[12].

# Challenges and Limitations

Despite the rapid advancement of DNA foundation models, significant challenges remain regarding their clinical applicability, generalizability, and regulatory oversight. A primary concern is the issue of equitable clinical translation, particularly concerning ancestry-related disparities. For instance, while polygenic risk scores (PRS) offer promising strategies for risk stratification in conditions such as short stature, substantial differences in PRS distributions and predictive performance across different ancestral groups limit their fair implementation, necessitating improvements in cross-ancestry generalizability for reliable use[13].
Furthermore, rigorous benchmarking has revealed systematic blind spots in current models. Studies evaluating DNA foundation models such as Evo and DNABERT-2 have identified limitations in their understanding of known biological constraints, including short-range biological signals like codon usage bias[7]. These models have also shown sensitivity to contextual features that should be biologically neutral, which challenges claims of zero-shot pathogenicity prediction and raises concerns about their clinical readiness[7]. While some large multi-species models, such as NT, have demonstrated superior discriminative power in detecting functional consequences of single nucleotide variants (SNVs) compared to other architectures, the performance of models like DNABERT and HyenaDNA varies, highlighting the need for robust evaluation metrics tailored to specific biological tasks[5].
Technical limitations also arise from the complexity of integrating diverse genomic data. Sequence-only representations often fail to fully capture regulatory information from additional DNA-centric modalities, such as chromatin accessibility[1]. Directly fusing these heterogeneous modalities is challenging due to the markedly different statistical structures of sparse, peak-shaped regulatory signals and dense sequence embeddings, which can lead to near-zero solutions in naive alignment approaches[1]. Additionally, context-specific regulatory sequence models, such as those based on convolutional neural networks, often struggle to generalize predictions across different cell types, limiting their utility in deciphering the dynamics of transcriptional regulation in diverse cellular contexts[2].
From a regulatory perspective, the deployment of such AI systems faces hurdles under frameworks like the EU Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act). Systemic data bias constitutes a major source of failure in real-world AI systems, yet existing legal frameworks do not adequately capture how bias originates, propagates, and manifests across the AI lifecycle[14]. The AI Act’s risk-based regulatory regime, while comprehensive, may not sufficiently address the specific technical bias mechanisms inherent in genomic AI applications[14]. Moreover, the concept of "systemic risk" for General-Purpose AI Models (GPAIMs) is criticized for institutionalizing uncertainty rather than resolving it, potentially creating regulatory blind spots and delegating key decisions to private actors[15]. These legal and technical challenges underscore the need for further development in both model architecture and regulatory frameworks to ensure the safe and effective use of DNA foundation models in genomics.

# Ethical and Regulatory Considerations

The deployment of DNA foundation models, such as SPACE, for genomic prediction raises significant ethical and regulatory challenges, particularly concerning data privacy, algorithmic bias, and compliance with emerging artificial intelligence legislation. As these models are increasingly integrated into clinical and research settings, they must navigate complex frameworks designed to protect participants and ensure equitable outcomes.
**Data Privacy and Informed Consent**
A primary ethical concern involves the handling of sensitive genomic data and the adequacy of informed consent. Informed Consent Forms (ICFs) are essential components of research involving human participants, providing necessary information about the study's impact and risks.[16] However, the length and complexity of ICFs have increased over recent decades due to heightened legal and ethical liabilities, which can affect participant comprehension and preferences.[16] The sharing of rich interview and genomic data, as seen in repositories like the Qualitative Data Repository (QDR), requires strict adherence to participant protection protocols to ensure that data sharing does not compromise individual privacy.[17][18] For foundation models trained on large, ancestrally diverse biobank data, ensuring that consent covers the broad range of potential downstream applications, including the use of in-context learning (ICL) capabilities, is a critical regulatory hurdle.[8]
**Algorithmic Bias and Equity**
Systemic data bias constitutes a major source of failure in real-world AI systems and represents a regulatory challenge that remains insufficiently addressed by existing legal frameworks.[14] Genomic prediction models often perform unevenly across populations due to large imbalances in sample availability across ancestry groups and the non-stationarity of genotype–phenotype effect sizes across the ancestry continuum.[8] This disparity limits clinical utility and raises ethical concerns regarding health equity. While models like SPACE aim to improve robustness to ancestry-driven effect heterogeneity, the propagation of bias from data collection and annotation through to model training and deployment remains a persistent risk.[14] The EU Artificial Intelligence Act (AI Act) introduces a comprehensive risk-based regulatory regime, but critics argue it does not adequately capture how bias originates and manifests across the AI lifecycle, particularly in sectors like healthcare risk prediction.[14]
**Regulatory Compliance and the EU AI Act**
The European Union’s AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) establishes a risk-based framework that distinguishes between prohibited practices, high-risk systems, low-risk applications, and general-purpose AI (GPAI) models.[19] DNA foundation models may fall under the category of GPAI models, especially if they are designated as presenting systemic risk, which triggers enhanced obligations under Chapter V of the Act.[20] The Act’s governance approach for GPAI models is functional and deployment-oriented, diverging from the technical, architectural understanding of foundation models, which can create challenges for legal certainty.[20] Furthermore, the concept of "systemic risk" in the Act has been critiqued for institutionalizing uncertainty rather than resolving it, potentially delegating key decisions about risk content to private actors.[15]
For AI-driven healthcare applications, there is also a concern regarding regulatory overlap. Manufacturers of AI-powered medical devices worry about facing double certification under both the EU Medical Device Regulation (MDR) and the AI Act.[21] However, analysis suggests that double certification is redundant for AI-based Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) that has already achieved MDR certification at Class IIa or higher, as Article 43(3) of the AI Act allows for the use of existing sectoral certifications.[21] This interplay between sector-specific medical regulations and general AI laws is crucial for the responsible deployment of genomic predictors in clinical settings.
