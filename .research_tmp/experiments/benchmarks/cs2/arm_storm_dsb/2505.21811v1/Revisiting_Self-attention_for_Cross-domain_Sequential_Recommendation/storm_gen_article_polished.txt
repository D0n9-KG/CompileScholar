# summary

**Revisiting Self-attention for Cross-domain Sequential Recommendation** is a research area focused on enhancing the accuracy and personalization of recommendation systems by integrating self-attention mechanisms with cross-domain knowledge transfer. Sequential recommendation systems, which predict user interactions based on historical behavior, are vital for e-commerce and online platforms but often suffer from data sparsity and the inability to capture evolving user preferences [1][2]. To address these limitations, cross-domain recommendation (CDR) frameworks transfer knowledge from source domains to target domains, particularly to mitigate the cold-start problem where users have limited interaction histories in the target domain [3][4].
Traditional Transformer-based models applied to sequential recommendation have achieved success but often lack personalization, failing to model complex user-item relationships or account for specific user characteristics [5]. Recent methodologies have evolved to incorporate multi-level user modeling, such as the DCUPSRec framework, which establishes relationships between users and items to capture diverse user traits [5]. Additionally, advanced approaches like Rec-SSP utilize gated fusion mechanisms to balance long-term preferences derived from aggregated reviews with short-term preferences from recent sequential patterns, thereby capturing fine-grained user intent [6].
The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) has further advanced the field by leveraging semantic reasoning and external knowledge to improve recommendation quality, especially for cold-start users [7][8]. Hybrid architectures, such as the Hybrid User-LLM system, dynamically combine latent sequential embeddings for "warm" users with interpretable textual summarization for "cold-start" users, achieving significant improvements in metrics like Hit@10 [7]. Furthermore, prompting strategies for LLMs are being refined to incorporate collaborative signals, with methods like NeighCF employing cluster-aware retrieval to address computational inefficiencies and redundancy in neighbor selection [8].
These developments highlight a shift towards more holistic and context-aware recommendation systems that integrate textual data, feedback mechanisms, and continuous-time semantic distribution alignment to ensure consistency under irregular interaction intervals [2][7]. By addressing challenges such as unstable probability mass migration and the need for privacy-preserving data utilization, recent research aims to create scalable solutions that adapt to the dynamic nature of user preferences across multiple domains [2][9].

# Background

Sequential recommendation systems have become vital components of modern e-commerce and online platforms, aiming to predict a user's next interaction based on their historical behavior sequences [1][2]. These systems are widely applied in various fields, including content delivery and location-based services, to enhance user experience and efficiency by providing personalized suggestions [2]. However, the effectiveness of these systems is often hindered by challenges such as data sparsity and the inaccuracy in identifying user preferences, which can lead to suboptimal recommendation quality [1]. While recent deep sequential models have achieved promising performance, they primarily focus on discrete sequence encoding and next-item scoring, often neglecting how predictive distributions evolve over the item space or remain semantically consistent under irregular interaction intervals [2].
To address the limitations of single-domain models, cross-domain recommendation (CDR) has emerged as a significant area of research. CDR aims to transfer knowledge across different domains to improve performance in a target domain, particularly addressing the cold-start problem where users have sparse presence in the target domain [3][4]. The interactions of cold-start users in a source domain play a pivotal role in predicting their interests in the target domain, with recent advancements often using tags or metadata as a bridge to transfer knowledge and align user preferences across domains [3]. For instance, the MSECDR framework utilizes Doc2Vec to reflect the sequentiality of user-item interactions in multiple domains, enabling the simultaneous learning of intra-domain, inter-domain, and other interaction sequences for representation learning [4].
Transformer models, inspired by their success in machine translation, have been introduced into sequential recommendation to leverage self-attention mechanisms, yielding satisfactory results [5]. However, these models often lack consideration of specific user information, making them non-personalized and unable to perform accurate multi-level modeling of users [5]. To mitigate this, approaches like DCUPSRec have been proposed to establish relationships between users and items within a sequential framework, modeling complex relationships based on diverse user characteristics [5]. Furthermore, balancing long-term and short-term preferences remains a challenge; models such as Rec-SSP integrate these preferences through gated fusion mechanisms, extracting long-term preferences from aggregated reviews and short-term preferences from recent sequential patterns [6].
The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) has further advanced the field by leveraging semantic reasoning and external knowledge [7][8]. LLMs have shown strong promise in recommendation tasks, particularly for cold-start users with limited interaction histories, where traditional methods struggle [7]. Hybrid architectures, such as the Hybrid User-LLM system, dynamically combine latent sequential embeddings of warm users with interpretable textual summarization of cold-start users, significantly improving metrics like Hit@10 [7]. Additionally, prompting strategies for LLMs are evolving to incorporate collaborative signals beyond individual user history, with methods like NeighCF employing cluster-aware retrieval to address the challenges of fixed-size neighbor selection and computationally expensive global retrieval [8]. These developments highlight the ongoing effort to refine self-attention mechanisms and sequential modeling to better capture user intent and improve cross-domain recommendation accuracy.

# Methodology

The methodology for cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDR) has evolved to address the limitations of traditional models, which often fail to capture the complex, multi-behavioral nature of user interactions across different platforms. Recent approaches integrate self-attention mechanisms, sequential embeddings, and hybrid architectures to enhance the accuracy and personalization of recommendations.

## Sequential Modeling and Attention Mechanisms

A core feature of modern recommendation systems is the dynamic sequence, where Transformer models and self-attention mechanisms are employed to process user interaction histories. While these models have achieved significant success in machine translation and sequential recommendation, early implementations were often non-personalized, lacking the consideration of specific user information necessary for multi-level modeling[5]. To address this, frameworks like DCUPSRec establish relationships between users and items within historical data, enabling the modeling of complex relations within a sequential framework[5]. Furthermore, advanced methodologies now focus on the evolution of predictive distributions over the item space. For instance, continuous-time semantic distribution alignment frameworks have been proposed to ensure that recommendation distributions remain semantically consistent under irregular interaction intervals, thereby preventing unstable probability mass migration[2].

## Cross-Domain Knowledge Transfer

Cross-domain recommendation aims to transfer knowledge from source domains to a target domain to improve performance, particularly in scenarios where data is sparse. The MSECDR framework utilizes Doc2Vec to reflect the sequentiality of user-item interactions across multiple domains, allowing for the simultaneous learning of intra-domain, inter-domain, and other interaction sequences[4]. This approach employs a deep neural network structure to combine the linearity and non-linearity of user-item interactions[4]. Additionally, addressing the cold-start problem in CDR involves leveraging the presence of users in multiple domains. Novel frameworks propose using metadata and semantic similarity to devise neighborhoods based on preference similarity, effectively transferring knowledge from source to target domains to predict the interests of cold-start users[3].

## Hybrid Architectures and Personalization

To balance long-term and short-term user preferences, hybrid architectures have been developed. The Rec-SSP model, for example, integrates long-term preferences extracted from aggregated user reviews with short-term preferences modeled through sequential patterns from recent interactions[6]. This multilevel design captures fine-grained opinions across items to understand evolving user intent[6]. Similarly, the Hybrid User-LLM system dynamically combines latent sequential embeddings of "warm" users with interpretable textual summarization for "cold-start" users[7]. This system utilizes a pretraining phase with an autoregressive transformer encoder followed by joint fine-tuning with a Large Language Model (LLM) using gated cross-attention, achieving significant improvements in hit rates compared to baselines[7].

## Incorporating Contextual and Feedback Data

Recent methodologies also incorporate external data sources to enhance user-item interactions. The EUICC-SRec approach introduces a counterfactual classifier, textual information, and user feedback to mitigate issues such as data sparsity and the misrepresentation of user interests[1]. In the context of intelligent transportation systems, agent-based recommendation engines employ hybrid imputation-reasoning architectures to maintain decision-making integrity despite incomplete or missing data caused by sensor failures or privacy constraints[10]. These systems simulate heterogeneous traveler preferences and real-time network conditions to provide context-aware mobility solutions[10]. Additionally, in multi-store assortment planning, methodologies distinguish between simultaneous and sequential offering strategies, where customers may incur disutility when choosing products from other stores, influencing the optimal assortment planning under each strategy[11].

# Experimental Setup

The experimental evaluation of self-attention mechanisms in cross-domain sequential recommendation is typically conducted using large-scale, real-world datasets to validate the effectiveness of proposed models in handling sparse and multi-behavior user interactions. A common benchmark involves datasets from major e-commerce and review platforms, such as Amazon and TripAdvisor, which provide extensive records of user-item interactions across multiple domains.[4] These datasets are crucial for assessing how well models can transfer knowledge across different domains to improve performance in a target domain, particularly for cold-start users who have limited interaction histories in the target domain but may have rich histories in source domains.[3]
To address the challenges of cold-start scenarios, experimental setups often incorporate hybrid architectures that combine latent sequential embeddings for warm users with interpretable textual summarization for cold-start users.[7] For instance, evaluations may measure performance metrics such as Hit@10, where hybrid systems have demonstrated improvements of up to 13.5% over baseline methods by dynamically integrating user-specific features.[7] Additionally, experiments frequently involve pretraining phases using autoregressive transformer encoders to predict the next item, followed by joint fine-tuning with Large Language Models (LLMs) using gated cross-attention mechanisms to enhance semantic reasoning and leverage external knowledge.[7]
In the context of prompt engineering for LLM-based recommenders, experimental designs also focus on the impact of collaborative signals. Strategies that incorporate neighbors' histories are evaluated against those relying solely on individual user history, with specific attention to the efficiency of neighbor retrieval methods.[8] For example, cluster-aware retrieval strategies are tested to ensure that neighbor selection is restricted to candidate-relevant subspaces, thereby avoiding the computational overhead of global retrieval and the redundancy issues associated with fixed-size neighbor selection.[8] These setups aim to verify that the proposed methods can effectively balance the utilization of sparse contexts and the management of dense ones in large-scale systems.[8]

# Results and Analysis

Research in sequential recommendation has yielded diverse results addressing challenges such as personalization, cold-start scenarios, and the integration of large language models (LLMs). Studies indicate that traditional transformer-based models, while effective in machine translation, often lack the capacity for multi-level user modeling, resulting in non-personalized outputs. To address this, the DCUPSRec model was developed to establish relationships between users and items within a sequential framework, aiming to accurately model complex user-item interactions by leveraging diverse user characteristics[5].
In the context of cold-start users, who present significant challenges due to sparse interaction histories, recent advancements have focused on cross-domain knowledge transfer and hybrid architectures. One approach utilizes user convergence to align preferences across different domains, employing metadata to devise neighborhoods based on preference similarity, thereby mitigating the gap between source and target domains[3]. Another study proposes a Hybrid User-LLM system that dynamically combines latent sequential embeddings for warm users with interpretable textual summarization for cold-start users. This architecture, which covers 100% of users, achieved a Hit@10 of 10.1%, representing a 13.5% improvement over baseline methods[7].
The integration of Large Language Models has also shown promise in enhancing recommendation quality through semantic reasoning. However, existing prompting strategies often overlook collaborative signals. The NeighCF method addresses this by employing a cluster-aware retrieval strategy that restricts neighbor search to candidate-relevant subspaces, thereby reducing computational costs associated with global neighbor retrieval and avoiding the redundancy issues found in fixed-size neighbor selection[8]. Furthermore, the EUICC-SRec methodology incorporates a counterfactual classifier, textual information, and user feedback to enhance user-item interactions, addressing issues of data sparsity and preference inaccuracy that often lead to suboptimal recommendation quality[1].
Beyond user modeling, the evolution of predictive distributions and semantic consistency has been analyzed. A continuous-time semantic distribution alignment framework was proposed to address the limitations of discrete sequence encoding, which can lead to unstable probability mass migration. This framework focuses on how predictive distributions evolve over the item space and remain semantically consistent under irregular interaction intervals[2]. Additionally, the Rec-SSP model demonstrates the importance of balancing long-term and short-term preferences. By integrating aggregated user reviews for long-term preferences and sequential patterns from recent interactions for short-term dynamics through a gated fusion mechanism, the model captures fine-grained opinions and evolving user intent more accurately[6].
In specific application domains, such as influencer marketing, the concept of "influencer-product congruity" has been analyzed using data from Douyin. An inverted U-shaped relationship was identified between congruity and sales, where products that loosely match, rather than perfectly duplicate, the influencer's pre-existing identity generate the highest sales. This pattern is driven by perceived novelty peaking at moderate congruity, while perceived risk rises sharply at high congruity levels[12]. These findings highlight the nuanced nature of user-item fit in sequential and cross-domain recommendation contexts.

# Discussion

The field of sequential recommendation has evolved significantly, moving from basic collaborative filtering to sophisticated deep learning architectures that address specific challenges such as data sparsity, cold-start problems, and the balance between long-term and short-term user preferences. Recent research highlights a shift towards more nuanced modeling of user behavior, integrating diverse data sources and addressing the limitations of traditional discrete sequence encoding.
A primary focus in recent literature is the integration of auxiliary information to enhance the accuracy of user preference modeling. For instance, the EUICC-SRec methodology introduces a counterfactual classifier, textual information, and user feedback to improve user-item interactions, addressing the issue that existing solutions often neglect user feedback which may misrepresent interests [1]. Similarly, Rec-SSP proposes a review-aware model that utilizes a gated fusion mechanism to balance long-term preferences extracted from aggregated reviews with short-term preferences derived from recent sequential patterns, thereby capturing fine-grained opinions and evolving user intent [6]. These approaches underscore the importance of multilevel design in understanding the complex dynamics of user behavior.
Furthermore, researchers are addressing the limitations of deep sequential models that primarily focus on discrete sequence encoding and next-item scoring. A continuous-time semantic distribution alignment framework has been proposed to address issues such as unstable probability mass migration and semantically inconsistent recommendation distributions, particularly under irregular interaction intervals [2]. This framework aims to ensure that predictive distributions evolve consistently over the item space, accumulating historical preference evidence more effectively than previous methods.
The challenge of cold-start users, where limited interaction histories hinder accurate recommendations, has also been a significant area of innovation. The Hybrid User-LLM system leverages Large Language Models to cover 100% of users by dynamically combining latent sequential embeddings for warm users with interpretable textual summarization for cold-start users [7]. This hybrid architecture demonstrates a notable improvement in performance metrics, such as Hit@10, compared to baseline models, highlighting the potential of integrating LLMs with traditional sequential recommendation techniques.
In addition to technical advancements in model architecture, the role of privacy and data utilization has been re-evaluated. Studies have introduced methods that utilize baseline estimates to learn user interests from past interactions without requiring extensive personal data sharing, which is often a barrier due to internet privacy concerns [9]. This approach provides an additional fine-tuning factor for neighborhood-based collaborative filtering systems, offering a privacy-preserving alternative to data-intensive hybrid systems.
Beyond pure recommendation algorithms, the application of these principles extends to related domains such as assortment planning and influencer marketing. In multi-store assortment planning, strategies for simultaneous versus sequential offering of products are analyzed to optimize sales while accounting for customer disutility [11]. Similarly, in the context of influencer marketing, the concept of "influencer-product congruity" has been identified as a key driver of sales, with an inverted U-shaped relationship observed where products that loosely match an influencer's identity generate higher sales than those that perfectly duplicate it [12]. These findings suggest that the principles of matching and congruity, central to recommendation systems, also play a critical role in broader commercial strategies.
Collectively, these studies indicate a trend towards more holistic and context-aware recommendation systems that integrate textual data, feedback mechanisms, and large language models to overcome traditional limitations. The ongoing challenge remains to balance the complexity of these models with the need for efficient, privacy-preserving, and scalable solutions that can adapt to the dynamic nature of user preferences.
