# summary

**Measure Domains Gap: A Similar Domain Selection Principle for Multi-Domain Recommendation** is a research framework designed to address the Negative Transfer Problem (NTP) in Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) systems.[1] Traditional recommender systems typically operate within a single domain, such as movies or music, but user preferences often extend across diverse web environments, necessitating cross-domain knowledge transfer to alleviate data sparsity.[2][3] However, existing MDR methods frequently assume that beneficial transfer information is consistent across all domains, leading to performance degradation when unselectively leveraging data from unrelated or low-quality source domains.[1]
The core contribution of the proposed principle is the dynamic selection of suitable source domains based on similarity, rather than applying a uniform transfer strategy.[1] By modeling complex transfer relationships, the framework aims to isolate distinct contributions of cross-domain interactions from those arising within individual domains, thereby preventing knowledge conflicts that cause negative transfer.[1][4] This approach contrasts with methods that rely on static historical data or single-structure transfers, which often fail to capture the heterogeneity of user intent across different behavioral domains.[4][5]
The methodology integrates principles from domain adaptation, where accurately estimating the domain gap is critical for successful knowledge transfer.[6] In this context, the selection of nearly optimal sources from large candidate pools is achieved by relying on marginal probability distributions and optimizing weighted combinations of sources.[7] This ensures that only relevant, high-quality information is utilized, effectively mitigating the noise introduced by dissimilar source domains and enhancing the robustness of the recommendation model.[8][9]
Experimental evaluations and analysis demonstrate that this selective mechanism allows models to adaptively assign weight factors to prediction losses, controlling gradient flows through domains that exhibit significant negative transfer.[10] By comparing multi-domain trained models with single-domain baselines, the framework validates that dynamic domain selection enhances recommendation performance while maintaining stability across evolving user interests.[5][10] Ultimately, the Similar Domain Selection Principle provides a theoretical and practical foundation for developing more robust multi-domain systems that selectively leverage relevant cross-domain information to improve accuracy and fairness.[1][11]

# Background

Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) has emerged as a critical area of research aimed at enhancing recommendation performance by effectively utilizing transfer information across different domains.[1] Traditional recommender systems typically operate within a single domain, recommending items related to the same category, such as movies or music.[2] However, user preferences often extend beyond a single domain, particularly in web environments where users access diverse types of information.[3] Consequently, cross-domain recommendation (CDR) seeks to alleviate data sparsity by transferring knowledge from source domains to a target domain.[12]
Despite the success of existing MDR methods, most adopt a single structure to transfer complex domain-shared knowledge, assuming that beneficial transferring information is consistent across all domains.[1] This assumption often leads to the Negative Transfer Problem (NTP), where unselectively leveraging information from all domains results in performance degradation due to knowledge conflicts or poor-quality source domains.[1] To address this, recent approaches have focused on modeling complex transfer relationships to avoid NTP.[1] For instance, the Similar Domain Selection Principle (SDSP) was proposed to dynamically select suitable source domains for transfer, representing an initial exploration into selecting domains based on similarity rather than applying a uniform transfer strategy.[1]
Furthermore, capturing user intent across heterogeneous behavioral domains remains a fundamental challenge in session-based recommender systems.[4] Existing multi-domain approaches frequently fail to isolate the distinct contribution of cross-domain interactions from those arising within individual domains, limiting their ability to build rich and transferable user representations.[4] To overcome this, frameworks such as MOSAIC have been developed to explicitly factorize user preferences into orthogonal components: domain-specific, domain-common, and cross-sequence-exclusive representations.[4] Similarly, other methods like Multi-TAP focus on capturing intra-domain heterogeneity in user preferences through semantic persona modeling to enable more effective and selective knowledge transfer.[12]
The broader context of domain adaptation also highlights the importance of accurately estimating domain gaps. In unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA), success largely relies on domain gap estimation, which poses significant challenges in source-free scenarios where source domain data is inaccessible during adaptation.[6] Techniques such as using multiple classifiers to learn source domain decision boundaries have been proposed to provide tighter upper bounds of the domain gap, thereby maximizing transferable source domain knowledge.[6] These advancements in measuring and bridging domain gaps at both the data and model levels provide the theoretical foundation for developing more robust multi-domain recommendation systems that can selectively leverage relevant cross-domain information.

# Methodology

The methodology for multi-domain recommendation and adaptation centers on the quantification of domain similarity to guide data selection and model training. A fundamental challenge in transfer learning is determining the relatedness of source domains to a target domain, as transferring knowledge from unrelated sources can degrade classifier performance. To address this, methods have been proposed to select nearly optimal sources from large candidate pools by relying on marginal probability distributions, allowing for the utilization of abundant unlabelled data. This selection process is extended to multi-source scenarios by optimizing a weighted combination of sources, where weights are computed using fast, boosting-like algorithms to ensure robustness and efficiency[7].
In the context of sentiment analysis and semantic segmentation, the selection of appropriate training data is considered as critical as the choice of the algorithm itself. Existing approaches often train on all available source domains, including dissimilar ones, which can introduce noise. To mitigate this, novel representations and metrics for domain similarity have been developed to evaluate the relevance of source data. For instance, in semantic segmentation, soft pseudo-labels are generated by integrating predicted object probabilities from multiple source models. The prediction of each source model is weighted based on the estimated domain similarity between the source and target datasets, thereby emphasizing the contribution of models trained on more similar sources while suppressing the influence of potentially misclassified pixels[8][9].
Beyond static similarity metrics, dynamic and multi-level information is increasingly utilized to refine domain gap evaluation. In partial domain adaptation, methods such as the Reinforced Transfer Network (RTNet) argue that relying solely on high-level deep features is defective due to domain shifts. Instead, these approaches exploit both high-level and pixel-level information to filter out outlier source samples, using reinforcement learning to select relevant data for knowledge transfer[13]. Similarly, in autonomous systems, domain gap metrics are used to determine when to adapt object detectors. By investigating the correlation between domain gap and detection accuracy, systems can selectively adapt only when necessary, using new data that differs from the current training distribution, thus reducing computational costs[14].
For cross-domain policy transfer and trajectory adaptation, the focus shifts to bridging domain gaps at the data level rather than through complex downstream modeling. The Cross-Domain Trajectory EDiting (xTED) framework employs a specially designed diffusion model to capture and adapt cross-domain trajectories, offering a flexible alternative to heavy, task-specific architectures[15]. In machine translation, data selection for multiple domains is achieved by introducing instance-level domain-relevance features and constructing a training curriculum. This curriculum gradually concentrates on multi-domain relevant and noise-reduced data batches, balancing performance across in-domain and out-of-domain scenarios[16].
Finally, in the realm of recommendation systems, the Cross-Domain Adaptive Recommendation System (CDARS) integrates real-time behavioral tracking with multi-domain knowledge graphs. Unlike conventional methods that rely on static historical data, CDARS dynamically adjusts recommendation strategies based on contextual factors such as real-time engagement and implicit preference drifts, effectively addressing the challenge of adapting to users' evolving interests across multiple content domains[5].

# Experimental Evaluation

Experimental evaluations in multi-domain learning and recommendation systems frequently assess the efficacy of domain gap measurement and selection strategies in mitigating negative transfer and improving model performance across diverse datasets. In the context of Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR), researchers have proposed models that estimate the degree of negative transfer for each domain to adaptively assign weight factors to prediction losses, thereby controlling gradient flows through domains with significant negative transfer [10]. This approach involves comparing the performance of a model trained on multiple domains against a model trained solely on a specific domain to evaluate the utility of cross-domain information [10].
In the field of machine translation, experimental results demonstrate that multi-domain curriculum learning, which utilizes instance-level domain-relevance features, can simultaneously reach or outperform individual domain-specific performance while providing solid gains over training without a curriculum [16]. These experiments highlight the importance of balancing and improving performance across all domains, including out-of-domain scenarios, through carefully constructed training curricula [16].
For semantic segmentation, domain adaptive training methods that integrate predicted object probabilities from multiple source datasets have been evaluated to ensure reliable performance when source datasets are not necessarily relevant to the target [8]. Experiments in this area show that weighting predictions based on estimated domain similarity and using soft pseudo-labels considering entropy can achieve comparative or better performance than previous multi-source domain adaptation methods [8].
In autonomous driving and object detection, the correlation between domain gap and detection accuracy has been investigated to determine when detectors should adapt to environmental changes [14]. Studies utilizing unique datasets to isolate sensor resolution domain gaps have identified distinct inference and training domain gaps, with the inference gap showing a strong dependence on the number of LiDAR points per object [17]. Furthermore, approaches that selectively adapt detectors using domain gap metrics as a criterion have shown potential to improve operational efficiency in real-world scenarios where environmental conditions vary [14].
In the domain of monocular depth estimation, lifelong learning frameworks designed to handle dynamic changes in target domains have been evaluated for their ability to maintain stability on original domains while achieving high plasticity on new ones [18]. These experiments utilize lightweight multi-head frameworks with domain-shared encoders and domain-specific predictors to overcome significant domain gaps and enable scale-aware depth prediction [18]. Additionally, in the broader context of transfer learning, experiments confirm that exploiting data from related areas can reduce the need for new labeled samples, validating the principle of extrapolating knowledge across tasks to learn more efficiently [19].

# Analysis

The analysis of the Similar Domain Selection Principle (SDSP) focuses on addressing the Negative Transfer Problem (NTP) inherent in Multi-Domain Recommendation (MDR) systems. While MDR methods aim to enhance recommendation performance by leveraging transfer information across different domains, they often employ a single structure to transfer complex domain-shared knowledge.[1] This approach can be detrimental when knowledge conflicts exist between domains or when a specific domain is of poor quality, as unselectively leveraging information from all domains leads to significant negative transfer.[1] Consequently, the SDSP is proposed as a simple and dynamic mechanism to model these complex transfer relationships by selecting suitable domains for information transfer, thereby avoiding the pitfalls of indiscriminate data utilization.[1]
This selection strategy is critical because the beneficial transferring information should vary across different domains rather than being applied uniformly.[1] The necessity of such selective mechanisms is further highlighted in Cross-Domain Sequential Recommendation (CDSR), where models may underperform compared to Single-Domain Sequential Recommendation (SDSR) in certain domains due to a lack of relation between domains or varying levels of data sparsity.[10] To mitigate this, advanced models estimate the degree of negative transfer for each domain and adaptively assign it as a weight factor to the prediction loss, effectively controlling gradient flows through domains that exhibit significant negative transfer.[10] By comparing the performance of multi-domain trained models with those trained solely on specific domains, these systems can evaluate and adjust for domain-specific inefficiencies.[10]
The principles of domain selection and relevance assessment extend beyond recommendation systems into other machine learning contexts, such as data selection for machine translation. In multi-domain machine translation, research has shown that carefully introducing instance-level domain-relevance features and constructing a training curriculum to concentrate on multi-domain relevant data can simultaneously reach or outperform individual domain performance.[16] This suggests that the core challenge of balancing and improving performance across multiple domains, including out-of-domain scenarios, is a shared problem across various AI applications.[16] Similarly, in partial domain adaptation, the selection of relevant source samples is crucial for knowledge transfer; however, relying solely on high-level deep features is often defective due to domain shifts.[13] Instead, exploiting both high-level and pixel-level information allows for more robust filtering of outlier source samples, a concept parallel to the need for precise domain selection in MDR to prevent negative transfer.[13]
Furthermore, the challenge of bridging domain gaps is addressed in decision-making tasks through frameworks like Cross-Domain Trajectory EDiting (xTED), which proposes bridging gaps universally at the data level rather than relying on complex downstream policy transfer procedures.[15] This approach highlights the flexibility required when reusing pre-collected data from different domains, especially when target domain data are limited.[15] While other fields, such as microgrid operations, face routine tasks of optimally allocating resources under constraints without foresight into future inputs, the underlying algorithmic foundations of managing dynamic inputs and deriving theoretical bounds on performance remain relevant to the adaptive nature of domain selection in recommendation systems.[20] Ultimately, the analysis underscores that effective multi-domain modeling requires dynamic, selective mechanisms to ensure that transferred knowledge enhances rather than degrades system performance.[1]

# Related Work

Research in the field of multi-domain recommendation and domain adaptation has evolved to address the limitations of single-domain systems, focusing on cross-domain information transfer, bias quantification, and the selection of relevant source data.
**Cross-Domain Recommendation Systems**
Traditional recommender systems typically operate within a single domain, such as movies or music, which restricts the diversity of items suggested to users.[2] To overcome this, cross-domain collaborative recommender systems have been developed to suggest items from various domains by exploiting the semantic content of networks and user preferences that extend across different types of information.[3] These systems aim to provide a common framework for classifying and evaluating state-of-the-art algorithms, often utilizing similarity measures between different classes to model user interests.[2] More recently, the Cross-Domain Adaptive Recommendation System (CDARS) has been proposed to address the challenge of adapting to users' evolving interests in real-time.[5] Unlike conventional methods that rely on static or historical data, CDARS integrates real-time behavioral tracking with multi-domain knowledge graphs to dynamically adjust recommendation strategies based on contextual factors such as engagement and sentiment fluctuations.[5] Additionally, approaches have been developed to help developers identify related domains and extract useful features from products in different domains, using shared features as bridges to search for potential reusable information.[21]
**Domain Adaptation and Similarity Metrics**
A critical component of multi-domain learning is the ability to measure and adapt to the differences between domains. In sentiment analysis, where sentiment-indicating words vary significantly between domains, extensive studies have been conducted on domain similarity metrics to select appropriate training data.[9] These studies propose novel representations and metrics that consistently outperform random and balanced baselines, demonstrating that the selection of training data is as important as the choice of algorithm.[9] In the context of partial domain adaptation (PDA), where only a subset of source classes is shared with the target domain, methods have been proposed to select relevant source samples for knowledge transfer.[13] While previous methods relied on high-level deep features, recent work argues for the inclusion of pixel-level information to better filter out outlier source samples, utilizing reinforced transfer networks to improve adaptation accuracy.[13] Furthermore, in unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA), particularly in source-free scenarios where source data is inaccessible, researchers have proposed using multiple classifiers to learn source domain decision boundaries.[6] This approach provides a tighter upper bound of the domain gap, allowing for the maximization of transferable source domain knowledge by inducing maximum disagreement among classifiers in the target domain.[6]
**Bias and Fairness in Recommendations**
Beyond performance and adaptation, the fairness of recommender systems has become a significant area of research. While recommender systems are widely applied, limited research has focused on quantifying the unfairness and biases present in these systems.[11] Prior work has largely concentrated on determining whether a system is discriminating, rather than computing the amount of bias present.[11] Recent studies have specifically focused on quantifying popularity bias, which stems from the output of recommendation models and leads to the over-recommendation of popular items that may be misaligned with user preferences.[11] Quantifying these biases is considered essential for ensuring fair and safe commercial applications, particularly for sensitive user groups.[11]
