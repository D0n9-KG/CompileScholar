# The VENUS Dataset

The VENUS dataset is a large-scale multimodal resource designed to address the limitations of existing large language models (LLMs) in incorporating nonverbal elements such as gestures, facial expressions, and body language.[4] By providing annotated videos with time-aligned text, facial expressions, and body language, VENUS serves as the foundational data for training MARS, a multimodal language model capable of understanding and generating nonverbal cues alongside text.[4] This dataset bridges the gap in conversational AI by enabling the creation of fully immersive conversational experiences that account for the critical aspects of intent and emotion conveyed through nonverbal communication.[4]
The construction of VENUS leverages a next-token prediction objective, combining textual data with vector-quantized nonverbal representations to achieve multimodal understanding and generation within a unified framework.[4] This approach allows the model to process and generate nonverbal signals effectively, addressing the challenge that existing LLMs fail to integrate these cues, which limits their capacity for realistic human-computer interaction.[4] The dataset's structure supports the analysis of how nonverbal behaviors function as interactional triggers, complementing broader research into transformative nonverbal cues such as facial expressions, eye behavior, and touch that influence interpersonal relationships.[5]
While VENUS focuses on a unified multimodal framework, it contributes to a broader ecosystem of nonverbal communication research that includes specialized datasets for specific modalities. For instance, other resources like FePh provide sequenced facial expression data in the context of sign language, highlighting the scarcity of annotated facial expression datasets in specific linguistic contexts.[6] Additionally, research into facial Action Unit (AU) detection and feature selection for nonverbal communication signals further underscores the importance of high-quality, annotated data for improving recognition performance and understanding human behavior.[7][8] The VENUS dataset, by integrating these diverse nonverbal signals with text, offers a comprehensive tool for advancing the state of the art in multimodal AI.[4]

# The MARS Model

MARS is a multimodal language model designed to understand and generate nonverbal cues alongside text, addressing the limitations of existing large language models (LLMs) that fail to effectively incorporate nonverbal elements such as gestures, facial expressions, and body language.[4] By bridging this gap, MARS aims to create more immersive conversational experiences by integrating these critical aspects of human interaction into a unified framework.[4]
The model is trained using the VENUS dataset, a large-scale collection of annotated videos featuring time-aligned text, facial expressions, and body language.[4] To achieve multimodal understanding and generation, MARS employs a next-token prediction objective that combines text with vector-quantized nonverbal representations.[4] This approach allows the model to process and output nonverbal data within the same architectural paradigm as text, facilitating a seamless integration of verbal and nonverbal communication channels.[4]

# Research Context

Nonverbal communication is integral to human interaction, with gestures, facial expressions, and body language conveying critical aspects of intent and emotion [5]. Despite this, existing large language models (LLMs) often fail to effectively incorporate these nonverbal elements, limiting their capacity to create fully immersive conversational experiences [4]. While speech encompasses a wealth of information, including content, paralinguistic, and environmental details, current chat-oriented LLMs that handle multi-modal inputs frequently fall short in generating appropriate responses due to a lack of principles for task definition and suitable evaluation metrics [9].
To address the gap in understanding and generating nonverbal cues, recent research has introduced multimodal language models designed to bridge the divide between text and nonverbal signals. For instance, the MARS model was developed to understand and generate nonverbal cues alongside text, leveraging the VENUS dataset, which comprises annotated videos with time-aligned text, facial expressions, and body language [4]. Similarly, the NWT model was introduced as an expressive speech-to-video system that learns its own latent representations, such as Memcodes, to predict video from audio without relying on domain-specific intermediate representations like pose keypoints [10].
In the domain of speech specifically, Non-verbal Vocalizations (NVs), such as laughter and sighs, are vital for conveying emotion and intention, yet most existing speech systems neglect them, compromising communicative richness [11]. Previous methods for acquiring NVs have been limited by high costs associated with manual annotation or the unnatural quality of rule-based synthesis [11]. To overcome these limitations, researchers have proposed scalable automatic annotation frameworks that leverage unified detection models to identify NVs in natural speech, resulting in datasets like NonVerbalSpeech-38K [11].
Furthermore, the broader field of multi-modal learning has seen advances in quantifying the contribution of different modalities. The SHAPE scores were proposed to measure the marginal contribution of individual modalities and the degree of cooperation across them, providing a systematic way to evaluate fusion methods on multi-modal datasets [3]. Additionally, the rapid development of multilingual LLMs has highlighted the need for high-quality, diverse datasets, leading to the creation of large-scale corpora like DCAD-2000, which covers thousands of languages and utilizes anomaly detection for data cleaning [12]. These developments underscore the growing importance of comprehensive, well-curated multi-modal resources for advancing AI capabilities in nonverbal and speech understanding.

# Applications

Research into nonverbal communication and multimodal interaction has led to diverse applications ranging from assistive technologies for individuals with disabilities to advanced social artificial intelligence systems. One significant application is in the development of assistive devices that facilitate natural dyadic conversations for visually impaired users. The "Expression" system, an integrated solution using Google Glass, provides a visual interface that allows for natural conversation by addressing technical challenges such as video acquisition over Wi-Fi and robust detection of facial features. Usability studies involving both visually impaired and blind-folded subjects demonstrated the system's utility in perceiving social signals and modeling behavioral expressions[2].
In the domain of social artificial intelligence, computational methods are being developed to endow machines with the ability to encode and decode the broad channel of social signals used by humans. This involves formulating "social signal prediction" problems to model the dynamics of signals exchanged among interacting individuals. By utilizing 3D motion capture data that includes body, face, and hand motions in triadic social scenarios, researchers aim to create machines that can genuinely communicate with humans by understanding these nonverbal cues[13]. Furthermore, the development of multimodal datasets, such as "Look and Tell," supports the training of embodied agents capable of understanding and engaging in situated dialogue. These datasets, which record synchronized gaze, speech, and video from egocentric and exocentric perspectives, provide benchmarks for evaluating how different spatial representations affect multimodal grounding in referential communication[14].
The analysis of nonverbal and spoken communication patterns also has practical applications in professional settings, particularly in healthcare. By using wearable multimodal sensors to investigate interpersonal communication behaviors, researchers can analyze longitudinal data to understand speaking patterns among healthcare providers, such as nurses. This approach helps identify differences in communication across shift schedules and working units, providing insights into the nonverbal and vocal cues that convey physical and psychological states[15]. Additionally, digital sensor technology is being applied in language learning environments to track and analyze the process of language acquisition through spoken interaction. A three-stage model for the application of digital sensors in applied linguistics has been developed to facilitate and track language learning, enabling multimodal data collection and analysis of how learners interact in digital environments[16].
Beyond specific human-computer interaction tasks, these technologies contribute to broader collaborative frameworks. Web-based approaches utilizing software agents act on behalf of users to facilitate collaboration, leveraging the internet as a tool for both information retrieval and task execution. These systems aim to achieve better results without burdening users, demonstrating the potential of intelligent agents in enhancing collaborative work through the integration of various communication channels[17]. Finally, the principles of multimodal interaction are being applied to address linguistic inclusivity. By moving beyond standard text-first pipelines, researchers are developing oral-first multi-agent architectures that respect indigenous data sovereignty and diglossia. This approach focuses on turn-taking, repair, and shared context, ensuring that AI systems align with lived oral practices rather than just serving primarily oral languages through text-based intermediaries[18].
