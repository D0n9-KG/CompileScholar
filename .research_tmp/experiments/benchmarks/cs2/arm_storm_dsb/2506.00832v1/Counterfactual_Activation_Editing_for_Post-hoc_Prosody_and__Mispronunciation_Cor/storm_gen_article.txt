# Background

The field of image and media editing has a history spanning more than two centuries, traditionally requiring significant artistic mastery and considerable time to modify individual images.[1] While recent advancements in generative modeling, including Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), Variational Autoencoders, and diffusion models, have enabled the synthesis of high-quality, realistic images, editing real-world content remains a complex challenge.[1] A primary difficulty lies in synthesizing new features while faithfully preserving the original characteristics of the source image.[1] This challenge is particularly acute in specialized domains such as medical imaging, where counterfactual images are only useful if a specific clinical change is introduced without silently altering patient characteristics that should remain fixed.[2] Existing diffusion editors may produce convincing images, but they often fail to ensure that edits are localized, diagnosis-specific, or anatomically faithful, necessitating frameworks that can decompose representations into distinct factors such as pathology, anatomy, and acquisition context.[2]
Similar challenges exist in the domain of 3D facial animation, where producing convincing animations that deceive human observers remains a laborious task.[3] Although motion capture technologies have facilitated the generation of high-quality animations by capturing subtle facial movements and emotional signals, even the most advanced systems fail to perfectly retranscribe these nuances.[3] Consequently, the editing of animations remains a costly process that requires specialized expertise and significant effort to meet quality standards.[3]
In the context of speech and language, the development of text-to-speech (TTS) systems has historically relied on subjective measures, such as the Mean Opinion Score (MOS), which are slow and expensive to compute due to the need for large numbers of human raters.[4] Recent research has shifted toward objective assessment metrics, such as those based on Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance using spectrograms and high-level features from Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) models, to cover both acoustic and linguistic information.[4] These objective methods have shown superior performance in correlation with human judgments compared to other baselines, particularly in low-resource language settings.[4]
Furthermore, the issue of mispronunciation and its correction has been studied in educational contexts, where immediate correction of mispronounced key utterances has been shown to significantly enhance classroom discussion and learner interaction compared to delayed correction.[5] This highlights the importance of precise and timely intervention in speech-related tasks, a principle that aligns with the need for accurate, localized editing in post-hoc prosody and mispronunciation correction systems.

# Methodology

The methodology for counterfactual activation editing in post-hoc prosody and mispronunciation correction draws upon principles from neural network optimization, structural equivalence, and objective assessment metrics. A core component of the approach involves the application of neural network models to predictive classification and data quality control, where architectures are designed to identify and correct specific error patterns. For instance, feed-forward networks can be trained on error-free data and subjected to stochastic transformations that reproduce known error patterns, such as measurement errors or mispronunciations, to generate a binary indicator of unit perturbation[6]. This process allows for the isolation of specific variables—such as employment, sales, or in the context of speech, prosodic features and phonetic accuracy—enabling targeted interventions without altering unrelated characteristics.
To ensure that the editing process is localized and does not inadvertently modify other aspects of the data, the framework utilizes state-factorized counterfactual editing. This method decomposes the representation into distinct factors, such as pathology (or error type), anatomy (or underlying structure), acquisition, and residual context, before performing a clinically or linguistically specified latent intervention[2]. By modifying only the target factor while keeping non-target factors fixed, the system ensures that the correction is diagnosis-specific and anatomically or structurally faithful. This approach addresses the challenge of ensuring that a requested change is introduced without silently altering patient or speaker characteristics that should remain constant[2].
The training and optimization of these networks are critical to the effectiveness of the editing process. Properly choosing and tuning an optimizer can significantly improve training speed and quality, and specialized optimizers may be evolved for specific tasks to outperform general-purpose solutions[7]. In the context of speech, the reliance on subjective measures like the Mean Opinion Score (MOS) is mitigated by using objective assessment metrics based on Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance, utilizing spectrograms and high-level features from Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) models to cover both acoustic and linguistic information[4]. This objective framework allows for efficient evaluation of the corrected outputs, ensuring that the method outperforms baselines in both utterance- and system-level correlations[4].
Furthermore, the theoretical underpinnings of the methodology consider the conditions under which distinct neural networks can implement identical input-output transformations. Understanding when the structure of a neural network can be perturbed gradually while preserving functionality is essential for maintaining the integrity of the correction process[8]. This biologically inspired mathematical method helps determine the theoretical constraints on how diverse neural circuits might develop to serve identical functions, imposing practical limits on the ability to uniquely infer the structure of underlying circuits from stimulus-response measurements[8]. By integrating these elements, the methodology provides a robust framework for counterfactual activation editing that is both precise and theoretically grounded.

# Experimental Setup

The experimental framework for counterfactual activation editing in post-hoc prosody and mispronunciation correction relies on the integration of objective assessment metrics and state-factorized editing techniques to ensure that linguistic modifications are localized and do not inadvertently alter other acoustic or linguistic characteristics. To address the limitations of subjective measures like the Mean Opinion Score (MOS), which are slow and expensive to compute, the setup utilizes an objective assessment metric based on Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance. This metric incorporates both spectrogram data and high-level features from an Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) model, thereby covering both acoustic and linguistic information to evaluate the quality of the edited speech[4].
The core of the editing process employs a state-factorized approach, analogous to frameworks used in medical imaging, which decomposes the speech representation into distinct factors such as pathology (or in this context, specific prosodic or phonetic errors), anatomy (or underlying speaker characteristics), acquisition, and residual context[2]. By isolating these factors, the system can perform clinically specified latent interventions—here, targeted corrections to prosody or mispronunciations—while ensuring that non-target factors remain fixed. This decomposition is critical for verifying that the requested changes are introduced without silently altering patient characteristics, or in the case of speech, speaker identity and unrelated linguistic features[2].
To validate the effectiveness of the editing, the system is tested against baselines using datasets that allow for rigorous comparison of utterance- and system-level performance. The evaluation focuses on the correlation between the objective DTW-based metrics and the actual quality of the speech, demonstrating that the proposed method outperforms other baselines by a large margin[4]. This setup ensures that the counterfactual edits are not only convincing but also anatomically faithful to the original speaker's characteristics, providing a robust framework for post-hoc correction in text-to-speech systems[4][2].

# Results

The application of neural network models to predictive classification for data quality control has demonstrated significant efficacy in identifying measurement errors. In the context of the Bank of Italy's business surveys, an architecture consisting of three feed-forward networks was developed to handle variables related to employment, sales, and investment. These networks, trained using the Resilient Propagation learning algorithm on error-free data subjected to stochastic transformations, achieved correct prediction rates of approximately 90 percent for employment, 94 percent for sales, and 75 percent for investment on training and validation sets.[6]
In the domain of speech synthesis, objective assessment metrics have been developed to address the limitations of subjective measures like the Mean Opinion Score (MOS). A proposed metric based on Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance, utilizing spectrograms and high-level features from Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) models, was evaluated on Thai TTS and the Blizzard Challenge datasets. Experimental results indicated that this method outperformed other baselines by a large margin in terms of correlation at both the utterance and system levels, effectively covering both acoustic and linguistic information.[4]
Regarding the pedagogical impact of error correction in English as a Foreign Language (EFL) classrooms, studies have examined the effects of immediate versus delayed correction of mispronounced key utterances. Analysis of collected data revealed that participants in a group where interlocutors were immediately corrected significantly outperformed those in a group where corrections were delayed until after the speech was finished. This suggests that immediate correction of mispronunciations can enhance classroom discussion and interaction, countering the potential negative effects of breaking the flow of communication.[5]
In the field of medical imaging, the PathEdit framework was introduced to ensure that counterfactual edits are localized and anatomically faithful. By decomposing abdominal CT representations into pathology, anatomy, acquisition, and residual context, the framework allows for clinically specified latent interventions where a pathology editor modifies only the target factor while non-target factors remain fixed. This approach addresses the limitations of existing diffusion editors, which may produce convincing images without guaranteeing that the edit is diagnosis-specific or useful on real clinical data.[2]
Additionally, in the area of optimization for artificial neural networks, the AutoLR evolutionary framework was used to evolve optimizers for specific tasks, such as image classification. The results showed that the evolved optimizers were competitive with human-made optimizers, highlighting the potential benefits of creating specialized optimizers from scratch rather than relying solely on parameter tuning of general-purpose solutions.[7]

# Discussion

The application of counterfactual activation editing for post-hoc prosody and mispronunciation correction represents a significant shift in how speech synthesis and quality assessment are approached, moving away from reliance on subjective metrics toward objective, factorized interventions. Traditional text-to-speech (TTS) development has long been hindered by its dependence on subjective measures, such as the Mean Opinion Score (MOS), which are slow, expensive, and require large datasets that are often unavailable in low-resource languages [4]. By proposing objective assessment metrics based on Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) distance and high-level features from Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) models, recent research has demonstrated that acoustic and linguistic information can be evaluated with high correlation to human perception, outperforming traditional baselines [4]. This objective foundation is crucial for the precise implementation of counterfactual editing, as it allows for the verification that specific linguistic or prosodic features have been altered without introducing unintended artifacts.
A core challenge in this domain is ensuring that corrections are localized and do not silently alter other patient or speaker characteristics that should remain fixed. In the context of medical imaging, frameworks like PathEdit have demonstrated the necessity of state-factorized counterfactual editing, where representations are decomposed into distinct factors (such as pathology, anatomy, and acquisition) to ensure that interventions modify only the target factor [2]. This principle of factorization is directly applicable to speech processing, where correcting a mispronunciation or adjusting prosody must not inadvertently change the speaker's identity, emotional tone, or the semantic content of the utterance. The ability to isolate and edit specific latent variables ensures that the resulting speech is anatomically or linguistically faithful to the original intent, a requirement that simple realism metrics or target-classifier flips cannot guarantee [2].
The efficacy of such editing techniques is further supported by advancements in neural network architectures capable of predictive classification and data quality control. For instance, feed-forward networks trained with algorithms like Resilient Propagation have shown high accuracy in identifying data affected by measurement errors, with correct predictions occurring in approximately 90% of records for specific variables [6]. This capacity for precise error identification and correction parallels the need in speech synthesis to detect and rectify mispronounced key utterances. In educational contexts, studies have shown that immediate correction of mispronunciations significantly enhances classroom interaction and learner performance compared to delayed correction [5]. Therefore, the ability to perform rapid, post-hoc corrections via counterfactual activation editing aligns with pedagogical findings that emphasize the importance of immediate feedback in verbal communication [5].
Furthermore, the integration of deep learning methods in related fields, such as 3D facial animation, highlights the broader trend of using neural models to refine and edit complex signals [3]. While facial animation focuses on capturing subtle movements and emotional signals, the underlying challenge of producing convincing, high-quality output that meets specific quality standards is analogous to speech synthesis [3]. The development of technologies that can capture and retranscribe subtle signals with high fidelity provides a robust framework for speech editing, where the goal is to maintain the naturalness and expressiveness of the speech while correcting specific errors. By combining the objective assessment capabilities of ASR-based metrics [4], the factorized editing principles from medical imaging [2], and the high-accuracy error detection of neural networks [6], counterfactual activation editing offers a comprehensive solution for post-hoc prosody and mispronunciation correction. This approach not only addresses the limitations of subjective evaluation but also ensures that corrections are precise, localized, and effective in enhancing the overall quality of synthesized speech.
