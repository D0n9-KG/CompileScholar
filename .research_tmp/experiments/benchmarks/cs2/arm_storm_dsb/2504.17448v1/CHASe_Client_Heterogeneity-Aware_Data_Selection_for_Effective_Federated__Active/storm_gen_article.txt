# Overview

Federated Active Learning (FAL) is an emerging paradigm that integrates the privacy-preserving capabilities of Federated Learning (FL) with the annotation efficiency of Active Learning (AL). While FL enables a large number of clients to collaborate in training models without sharing private data, thereby ensuring data security[1][2], it often faces challenges related to data heterogeneity and the high cost of obtaining ground truth labels[3]. FAL seeks to mitigate the burden of annotation under these realistic constraints by leveraging AL strategies to select the most informative samples for labeling[3]. This approach is particularly critical in low-budget regimes where the amount of available annotation is very limited, as FAL settings present additional complexities, such as data heterogeneity, compared to standard AL environments[3].
A significant challenge in FAL is the impact of Non-Independent and Identically Distributed (non-IID) data on model performance. Research indicates that skewed non-IID data can lead to a reduction in accuracy of up to 29% for neural networks trained in federated environments[2]. To address this, various optimization strategies have been proposed, including the use of cyclical learning rates and sharing pre-training methods on augmented data, which have demonstrated significant improvements in accuracy on benchmark datasets like CIFAR-10[2]. Furthermore, the integrity of FL systems is vulnerable to unreliable models and data poisoning attacks, necessitating robust client selection strategies that balance security and fairness[1].
To enhance the efficiency and robustness of FAL, recent studies have investigated specific AL strategies such as TypiClust, which has shown effectiveness in low-budget FAL settings despite distribution shifts in typicality[3]. Additionally, techniques like Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) and parallel Adapters are being integrated into FL frameworks to reduce computational and communication overheads, allowing for flexible deployment across diverse hardware with varying capabilities[4][5]. These advancements aim to ensure that FAL systems can maintain high performance and reliability while adhering to strict privacy constraints and resource limitations.

# Technical Framework

The technical framework for addressing client heterogeneity in federated learning is built upon the foundational architecture of Federated Learning (FL), a distributed optimization method that allows multiple client nodes to collaborate in training machine learning models without sharing raw data with a central server.[6][7][8] This decentralized approach is particularly critical in domains such as healthcare, manufacturing, and finance, where ethical, legal, and regulatory constraints often render the centralization of sensitive data infeasible.[8][9] In a standard FL setup, clients retain their private data locally and only exchange locally trained model parameters or gradients with a central server, which manages the collaborative learning process by aggregating these updates to build a shared global model.[10]
A primary challenge within this framework is the impact of Non-Independent and Identically Distributed (non-IID) data, which arises from the inherent heterogeneity of data across different clients.[2][5] Studies have demonstrated that skewed non-IID data distributions can lead to significant performance degradation, with accuracy reductions of up to 29% in neural network training.[2] To mitigate these issues, various technical strategies have been proposed. One approach involves the use of cyclical learning rates to dynamically adjust the learning rate during federated training, combined with sharing and pre-training methods on augmented data to improve algorithmic efficiency under non-IID conditions.[2] In the context of smart manufacturing, where data heterogeneity is a specific concern, frameworks have been developed that utilize customer degree selection methods based on model parameter variation, employing graph theory and Top-K mechanisms to filter participants and reflect data characteristics.[11]
Communication efficiency is another critical component of the technical framework, as the regular exchange of model parameters or gradients incurs substantial overhead, particularly in large-scale distributions.[5] To address this, techniques such as parallel Adapters for Federated Learning have been introduced, which can reduce communication overhead by approximately 90% while maintaining inference performance comparable to full model training.[5] Additionally, scalable communication infrastructures based on Information-Centric Networking, such as those built on Apache Kafka, have been proposed to manage latency and network congestion through a two-tier communication model that caches client updates at intermediate edge nodes.[7]
Optimization algorithms within the framework have also evolved to better handle heterogeneity. While Federated Averaging (FedAvg) remains the most popular algorithm due to its simplicity and privacy guarantees, recent advancements like FedExP propose adaptively determining the server step size based on dynamically varying pseudo-gradients to improve performance in overparameterized convex regimes.[12] Furthermore, to ensure reliability in heterogeneous environments, frameworks such as TrustFed introduce representation-aware client assignment mechanisms that provide distribution-free, finite-sample coverage guarantees for uncertainty quantification without requiring centralized data access.[9] These technical components collectively aim to balance the need for privacy preservation with the computational and statistical challenges posed by client data heterogeneity.

# Theoretical Background

Federated learning (FL) is an emerging distributed machine learning framework designed for privacy preservation, enabling multiple clients to collaboratively train a global model without sharing their private, potentially sensitive data with a central server.[8] In this architecture, clients retain their data locally and only share locally trained model parameters, allowing the central server to manage the collaborative learning process.[8] This approach has delivered promising results in real-life scenarios such as healthcare, energy, and finance.[8] However, a fundamental challenge in FL is the statistical heterogeneity among local data distributions, often referred to as Non-Independent and Identically Distributed (Non-IID) data.[13][14] This heterogeneity causes inconsistent optimized local models on the client side, leading to a significant reduction in the performance of the global model compared to standard centralized learning.[13]
Existing theoretical predictions suggest that data heterogeneity degrades the performance of the Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm.[15] However, practical observations indicate that FedAvg often converges well, contradicting these pessimistic theoretical predictions.[15] Recent analyses have shown that the key assumption of bounded gradient dissimilarity in previous theories is too restrictive for practical applications.[15] For simple quadratic problems, regimes exist where large gradient dissimilarity does not negatively impact convergence.[15] To better characterize these effects, new quantities such as "average drift at optimum" have been proposed to measure data heterogeneity and provide more accurate theoretical analyses of FedAvg.[15]
The impact of Non-IID data is significant across various model types and FL configurations. A detailed analysis reveals that Non-IID data influences both parametric and non-parametric machine learning models in both horizontal and vertical federated learning settings.[14] In specific applications, such as neural networks trained in environments with skewed Non-IID data, accuracy reductions of up to 29% have been observed.[2] Similarly, in federated deep retrieval models, Non-IID training data limits the negatives available for training, causing performance to drop significantly compared to centralized counterparts.[16] This issue is distinct from the commonly studied client drift problem, which refers to the gap between local and global model parameters.[13]
To address these theoretical and practical challenges, several optimization strategies and algorithmic modifications have been proposed. One approach involves using local drift decoupling and correction (FedDC), where each client utilizes an auxiliary local drift variable to track the gap between local and global model parameters, thereby bridging the consistency gap at the parameter level.[13] Other strategies include applying cyclical learning rates and developing sharing and pre-training methods on augmented data to improve efficiency under Non-IID conditions, which can increase accuracy on datasets like CIFAR-10 by approximately 36%.[2] Additionally, batch-insensitive losses have been proposed to alleviate the Non-IID negatives issue in federated movie recommendations, effectively improving the relative recall of deep retrieval models.[16]
Client selection methodologies also play a crucial role in mitigating heterogeneity. Traditional methods using model updates such as loss and bias to select participants often fail to accurately represent client heterogeneity or provide deterministic selection.[17] Novel approaches, such as Terraform, utilize gradient updates and deterministic selection algorithms to identify heterogeneous clients for retraining, achieving up to 47% higher accuracy.[17] Furthermore, communication efficiency remains a bottleneck in distributed FL, leading to the development of compressed and client-variance reduced methods like COFIG and FRECON.[18] These methods provide theoretical bounds on the number of communication rounds in nonconvex settings, accounting for client variance and compression operators.[18]
Beyond performance optimization, privacy and data quality are critical theoretical considerations. FL is susceptible to low-quality, corrupted, or malicious data, which can severely degrade model quality.[19] Since data is never revealed, traditional data valuation techniques are inapplicable; instead, novel techniques using "lazy" influence approximation and differential privacy allow for the filtering of corrupted data with high recall rates even under strong privacy guarantees.[19] Additionally, the right to be forgotten is addressed through methods like Forget-Stein Variational Gradient Descent (Forget-SVGD), which leverages non-parametric Bayesian approximate inference to unlearn data from specific agents while maintaining the integrity of the global model.[20] In the context of scientific workflows, which may involve proprietary modules and confidential data, formal frameworks are being developed to analyze module, data, and provenance privacy, ensuring provable privacy guarantees for provenance queries.[6]

# Experimental Evaluation

Experimental evaluations in the context of heterogeneity-aware data selection and federated learning have focused on validating the efficacy of methods that address data non-IIDness, communication overhead, and model robustness. A key area of investigation involves the impact of non-independent and identically distributed (non-IID) data on federated training performance. Studies have demonstrated that skewed non-IID data can lead to a reduction in accuracy of up to 29% for neural networks[2]. To mitigate this, optimization strategies such as cyclical learning rates and sharing methods on augmented data have been tested, showing improved efficiency and stability in heterogeneous environments[2].
In the domain of medical imaging, experimental results highlight the utility of uncertainty quantification in handling heterogeneous datasets. For instance, a federated learning approach for universal abdominal CT segmentation utilized model uncertainty for aggregation and predictive uncertainty for inference. By leveraging the inherent noise in stochastic mini-batch gradient descent to estimate a distribution over model weights, this method achieved effective segmentation across diverse scanners and capture settings while preserving patient privacy[21]. Similarly, in the field of salient object detection, semi-supervised approaches have been evaluated to reduce labeling burdens. Experiments showed that a proposed latent variable model with an energy-based prior, which identifies pixels with less confident predictions, achieved competitive performance using only 1/16 of the annotations from the original training dataset compared to state-of-the-art fully supervised models[22].
Communication efficiency and client variance reduction are also critical metrics in experimental assessments. Algorithms such as COFIG and FRECON have been evaluated for their ability to address communication bottlenecks and high client variance simultaneously. Theoretical and empirical analyses provided convergence bounds for these methods in nonconvex settings, demonstrating their effectiveness in reducing the number of communication rounds required for convergence[18]. Furthermore, the integration of parallel Adapters into federated learning frameworks has been tested across various datasets and non-IID data distributions. These experiments indicated that the adapter-based approach could achieve similar inference performance to full model training while reducing communication overhead by approximately 90%, proving its applicability in both cross-silo and cross-device settings[5].
Additional experimental work has focused on adaptive optimization and trustworthiness. The FedExP method, which adaptively determines the server step size based on dynamically varying pseudo-gradients, was evaluated in the overparameterized convex regime, revealing similarities between FedAvg and projection onto convex sets[12]. In terms of model reliability, a trustworthiness test grounded in stochastic proof-by-contradiction was applied to biomedical prediction tasks. This approach, which trains and tests on spurious labels permuted based on a potential outcomes framework, quantified model behavior through Fisher-style p-values, helping to distinguish between true clinical cues and spurious correlations[23]. Finally, data selection methods like DVC (Data Value Contribution) have been evaluated for their ability to select valuable training samples from massive, heterogeneous sources under budget constraints, addressing limitations in existing methods that oversimplify nonlinear transformations or fail to scale to larger multi-layer perceptrons[24].

# Related Work

**Active Learning and Federated Active Learning**
Active learning (AL) is a technique designed to reduce human annotation costs for machine learning systems by strategically selecting the most informative unlabeled data for annotation.[25] However, performing active learning individually may be insufficient due to restricted data diversity and limited annotation budgets.[25] To address these limitations, Federated Active Learning (FAL) has emerged as a framework that facilitates collaborative data selection and model training while preserving the confidentiality of raw data samples.[25] Despite its potential, existing FAL methods often fail to account for the heterogeneity of data distribution across clients and the associated fluctuations in global and local model parameters, which can adversely affect model accuracy.[25] The CHASe method is specifically designed to overcome these challenges by focusing on identifying unlabeled samples with high epistemic variations that oscillate around the decision boundary.[25]
**Federated Learning and Data Heterogeneity**
Federated learning (FL) is a powerful distributed learning scheme that allows numerous edge devices to collaboratively train a model without sharing their data, thereby ensuring data privacy.[2][26] However, a significant challenge in FL is the presence of Non-Independent and Identically Distributed (non-IID) data, which can lead to a reduction in accuracy of up to 29% for neural networks trained in environments with skewed data distributions.[2] While existing theory predicts that data heterogeneity will degrade the performance of the Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm, practical observations show that FedAvg often converges well, suggesting that previous theoretical assumptions regarding bounded gradient dissimilarity may be too pessimistic.[15] To address the impact of non-IID data, various optimization strategies have been proposed, including the use of cyclical learning rates and sharing methods on augmented data, which have shown improvements in accuracy on datasets like CIFAR-10.[2] Additionally, Clustered Federated Learning (CFL) has been developed to train multiple specialized models for groups of clients with similar data distributions, though the term has sometimes been applied ambiguously to strategies unrelated to data heterogeneity.[27]
**Communication Efficiency and Optimization**
Due to the communication bottleneck in distributed and federated learning applications, algorithms using communication compression have attracted significant attention.[18] The huge number, high heterogeneity, and limited availability of clients result in high client-variance, which further complicates the training process.[18] To address both communication costs and client-variance, methods such as COFIG and FRECON have been proposed, providing theoretical bounds on the number of communication rounds in nonconvex settings.[18] In contrast to approaches that condense models or messages into compact formats, ProgFed proposes a progressive training framework that inherently reduces computation and two-way communication costs while maintaining strong performance, with theoretical proofs of convergence at the same asymptotic rate as standard training on full models.[26] Furthermore, recent work has sought to generalize the vanilla averaging in FedAvg by treating client updates as pseudo-gradients; for instance, FedExP presents a method to adaptively determine the server step size based on dynamically varying pseudo-gradients to improve performance.[12]
**Data Selection and Privacy in Distributed Settings**
In many image and vision applications, collecting all data at a central location is challenging due to privacy and bandwidth restrictions, making federated learning an effective alternative.[28] A key challenge in this context is that only a subset of the data collected at each client is relevant for a specific learning task, while the rest may negatively impact model training.[28] Consequently, methods for distributedly selecting relevant data using benchmark models have been proposed to improve the efficiency of the learning process.[28] Beyond data selection, privacy remains a critical concern in scientific workflows and distributed systems, where proprietary modules and confidential data, such as health information, must be protected.[6] Research in this area has focused on formalizing privacy guarantees for module, data, and provenance privacy, ensuring that privacy constraints are maintained while maximizing utility for the user.[6]
