# Background

The deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) and other advanced AI architectures on edge devices presents a significant challenge due to the inherent mismatch between the computational demands of these models and the resource constraints of edge hardware. Edge computing processes data at the point of generation, offering advantages such as faster decision-making, reduced bandwidth usage, and improved privacy [1]. However, edge devices typically operate under strict limitations regarding processing power, memory, and energy consumption, which makes them unsuitable for running full-scale LLMs without optimization [1]. The growing gap between the increasing complexity of LLMs and the limited computational budgets of edge devices poses a key challenge for efficient on-device inference, despite gradual improvements in hardware capabilities [2].
To address these constraints, various strategies have been developed to enable efficient inference at the edge. One prominent approach is model quantization, which reduces memory usage and computational overhead. For instance, 4-bit quantization has been shown to reduce model memory usage by approximately 60–70% while maintaining accuracy within 2–5% of full-precision baselines [3]. However, uniform quantization often fails to account for the fact that different layers may respond differently to reduced precision, and memory consumption does not necessarily align with computational throughput [4]. Consequently, adaptive mixed-precision quantization mechanisms have been proposed to balance memory, latency, and accuracy based on user-defined priorities [4]. Similarly, for Mixture of Experts (MoE) models, which offer computational efficiency through sparse activation but suffer from excessive memory footprints, dynamic mixed-precision quantization frameworks like DyMoE have been introduced to prioritize expert quantization based on importance and depth-dependent sensitivity [5].
Beyond quantization, architectural and system-level optimizations are critical for managing the dynamic memory requirements of LLMs. Serving LLMs requires careful memory management for static weights, dynamic activations, and key-value (KV) caches [6]. While static weights are predictable, dynamic components such as KV caches change frequently during runtime, presenting challenges for efficient memory management [6]. Traditional serving systems often handle runtime memory and KV caches at distinct abstraction levels, which can result in suboptimal performance due to the isolation of these management tasks [6]. To mitigate these issues, techniques such as speculative decoding are being adapted for edge computing, where lightweight edge devices draft candidate tokens locally while a shared edge server verifies them, thereby orchestrating computation across heterogeneous devices [2].
Furthermore, the long-term stability of edge inference is affected by thermal dynamics. In application scenarios requiring continuous operation, such as robotics or environmental monitoring, the heavy computational load of neural networks can lead to thermal throttling and performance degradation within seconds [7]. Studies on low-cost edge platforms, such as the Raspberry Pi 4B, have demonstrated that hysteresis-based active cooling is essential to prevent thermal throttling and maintain prescribed frame rates [7]. Additionally, the integration of AI with operating systems is evolving to address these bottlenecks. Advances in machine learning and LLMs are enabling automation and self-optimization in OS components, shifting from heuristic-based designs to AI-enhanced systems that support short- and long-context inference and distributed training [8].
Finally, the reliability of edge inference in distributed environments, such as Extreme Edge Computing (XEC), introduces further complexities. In XEC, streaming workloads are distributed across consumer-owned devices, which exhibit volatile computational availability due to competing applications and unpredictable usage patterns [9]. This volatility poses a fundamental challenge in quantifying the probability that a device or ensemble of devices will maintain the processing rate required by streaming services [9]. As LLMs continue to be deployed in diverse environments, from datacenters to resource-constrained edge nodes, the need for comprehensive profiling frameworks and adaptive inference strategies remains paramount to ensure real-time responses and data privacy [3][2].

# Technical Methodologies

Efficient inference of Large Language Models (LLMs) and other deep neural networks on edge devices is achieved through a combination of algorithmic optimizations, hardware-aware co-design, and system-level orchestration. These methodologies address the inherent trade-offs between model accuracy, computational latency, memory footprint, and energy consumption.

## Model Compression and Quantization

Quantization is a primary technique for reducing the memory footprint and bandwidth requirements of models, enabling their deployment on resource-constrained devices. While uniform quantization offers a straightforward approach, it often fails to account for the varying robustness of different network layers, leading to accuracy degradation or suboptimal resource usage, particularly at low bitwidths [10]. To mitigate these issues, **heterogeneous** or **mixed-precision quantization** allocates different bitwidths to individual layers based on their sensitivity [4][10]. For instance, adaptive mixed-precision mechanisms analyze layer-wise characteristics to balance memory, latency, and accuracy under user-defined priorities [4].
Recent advancements include **Entropy-based coding** combined with quantization. The EntroLLM framework utilizes a combination of unsigned and asymmetric quantization to produce an entropy-reducing effect, which significantly improves the compressibility of weights via Huffman encoding [11]. This approach has demonstrated up to 30% storage savings over standard uint8 models and 65% over uint4 models, while also accelerating inference on memory-limited devices [11].
For Vision-Language Models (VLMs), which present unique challenges due to discrepancies in quantization sensitivity between vision and language components, frameworks like **SPEED-Q** employ staged processing with enhanced distillation [12]. This method systematically addresses low-bit weight-only quantization for models ranging from 1B to 2B parameters, which are particularly suitable for edge deployment [12]. Furthermore, to simplify the complex tuning and deployment processes associated with quantization, automated frameworks such as the **Hardware-Aware Quantization Agent (HAQA)** leverage LLMs to streamline hyperparameter tuning and hardware configuration, improving both deployment quality and ease of use [13].

## Pruning and Structural Optimization

Pruning techniques aim to remove redundant parameters or architectural components to reduce computational load. **Structured pruning** is particularly advantageous for edge deployment as it eliminates entire architectural components (such as channels or hidden units), maintaining compatibility with standard hardware accelerators without the need for specialized support required by unstructured sparsity patterns [14][15].
To address the performance degradation often associated with structured pruning, especially in zero-shot settings, methods like **NIRVANA** utilize a first-order saliency criterion derived from the Neural Tangent Kernel under Adam optimization dynamics [15]. This theoretically grounded strategy balances immediate zero-shot accuracy preservation with robust fine-tuning capability, incorporating adaptive sparsity allocation across layers [15]. Similarly, for Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), structural pruning paradigms such as layerwise and widthwise pruning are applied to the language model backbone, often combined with supervised fine-tuning and knowledge distillation to recover performance [16].

## Algorithm-Hardware Co-Design and Dynamic Scaling

Beyond static compression, dynamic adjustments to hardware states and model execution paths are critical for meeting strict latency and energy constraints. **EdgeBERT** exemplifies an in-depth algorithm-hardware co-design for latency-aware energy optimization in multi-task NLP [17]. It employs entropy-based early exit prediction to perform dynamic voltage-frequency scaling (DVFS) at sentence granularity, minimizing energy consumption while adhering to target latency [17]. This is further supported by adaptive attention spans, selective network pruning, and floating-point quantization to alleviate computation and memory overheads [17].
The interplay between quantization and voltage scaling is also a key area of research. Studies have analyzed the resilience of convolutional neural networks to SRAM-based errors and the relative energy impacts of using quantization and voltage scaling separately or jointly, highlighting the importance of considering hardware resilience in efficiency strategies [18].

## System-Level Orchestration and Inference Engines

At the system level, the challenge lies in orchestrating computation across heterogeneous devices and managing diverse service requirements. **Speculative decoding**, traditionally used for accelerating autoregressive generation, has been adapted for edge computing through frameworks that allow lightweight edge devices to draft candidate tokens locally using diverse draft models, while a shared edge server verifies them [2]. This approach leverages the heterogeneity of edge environments to improve overall inference efficiency.
The selection of appropriate optimization methods is complicated by diverse service requirements, such as those for chain-of-thought reasoning or agent services, which significantly increase inference costs [19]. Specialized LLM inference engines have emerged to integrate optimization methods like parallelism, compression, and caching into service-oriented infrastructures [19]. Comprehensive evaluations of these engines focus on ease-of-use, deployment, scalability, and suitability for throughput- and latency-sensitive applications [19]. Additionally, the intersection of AI and Operating Systems (OS) is gaining attention, with AI-enhanced systems aiming to automate and self-optimize OS components to better support edge inference and distributed training [8].

# System Architecture and Hardware Integration

The deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) and other deep learning models at the edge necessitates sophisticated system architectures that bridge the gap between high-level model requirements and the stringent constraints of edge hardware. These constraints typically include limited memory capacity, strict power budgets, and thermal limitations, which traditional cloud-centric architectures are not designed to handle. Consequently, recent research has focused on co-designing software and hardware components to optimize latency, energy efficiency, and resource utilization in resource-constrained environments.

## Memory Management and Hybrid Execution

A primary challenge in edge inference is the management of memory resources, particularly for large models where static weights, dynamic activations, and key-value (KV) caches compete for limited GPU or device memory. In datacenter and high-end edge scenarios, modern serving systems often employ a dual-level abstraction for memory management: static tensor abstractions for runtime memory and page table-based virtualization for KV caches to mitigate fragmentation [6]. However, this isolation can lead to suboptimal performance. To address this, specialized architectures for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models have been developed to handle sparse activation patterns efficiently. Since MoE models often exceed GPU memory capacity, they rely on CPU-GPU hybrid execution, where the GPU handles compute-intensive General Matrix Multiplication (GEMM) operations while the CPU processes lighter attention mechanisms [20]. To optimize this hybrid setup, expert prefetching schemes have been introduced that leverage internal model representations to speculate future experts, allowing memory transfers from CPU to GPU to overlap with computation, thereby reducing latency bottlenecks [21].

## Hardware-Aware Acceleration and Quantization

To further reduce computational overhead, edge architectures increasingly integrate hardware-aware quantization and specialized accelerator designs. The Hardware-Aware Quantization Agent (HAQA) represents an automated framework that leverages LLMs to streamline the quantization and deployment process, enabling efficient hyperparameter tuning and hardware configuration to improve deployment quality and ease of use [13]. At the hardware level, low-precision accelerators such as TREA are designed for object detection and classification, integrating dual-precision (4/8-bit) SIMD multiply-accumulate units with run-time bit truncation to eliminate conventional multiplier overhead and reduce accumulator bit-width [22]. Similarly, the FantastIC4 architecture minimizes the number of multipliers required for inference in fully-connected layers to just four, supporting efficient on-chip execution of compact model representations [23]. These hardware innovations are often co-designed with algorithmic techniques, such as structured pruning, to maintain high throughput while adhering to area and power constraints [22][23].

## Adaptive Inference and Thermal Management

Edge devices often operate under volatile conditions, including fluctuating available compute due to battery state, thermal throttling, or competing applications. To address this, adaptive inference frameworks like AdaLLaVA dynamically reconfigure operations in Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) during inference, accounting for input data and latency budgets to achieve varying accuracy-latency tradeoffs [24]. Another approach, width-adaptive inference, trains a single set of shared weights containing multiple nested subnetworks of increasing capacity, allowing the model to adjust its computational width based on available resources [25]. For instance, Slimmable ConvNeXt utilizes modern design elements like LayerNorm to facilitate channel-width slimming without the normalization overhead of classical slimmable networks [25].
Thermal management is also a critical component of edge system architecture, as heavy computational loads can lead to rapid thermal throttling and performance degradation. Studies on platforms like the Raspberry Pi 4B have demonstrated that hysteresis-based active cooling is essential to prevent thermal throttling and maintain prescribed frame rates for continuous operation [7]. Furthermore, in Extreme Edge Computing (XEC) scenarios where workloads are distributed across consumer-owned devices, analytical frameworks are used to quantify the computational reliability of device ensembles, ensuring that the probability of maintaining required processing rates is sufficient for streaming AI services [9]. These integrated approaches highlight a shift from static, heuristic-based designs to AI-enhanced, self-optimizing systems that can adapt to dynamic hardware and workload conditions [8].

# Evaluation and Performance Analysis

Evaluating the performance of Large Language Models (LLMs) at the edge requires a multi-faceted analysis that balances computational efficiency, memory management, and adherence to service-level objectives (SLOs). As inference becomes the dominant factor in cost and power consumption, surpassing training, recent studies have focused on optimizing specific phases of the inference pipeline, such as the prefill phase in Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) and the iterative token generation process.[26]

## Memory Management and Efficiency

Memory constraints are a primary bottleneck for edge deployment, necessitating advanced techniques for managing static weights, dynamic activations, and key-value (KV) caches. Traditional serving systems often isolate runtime memory management from KV cache management, leading to suboptimal resource utilization.[6] To address this, **NestedFP** proposes a memory-efficient serving technique that overlays FP8 parameters onto FP16 parameters, allowing both precision models to share the same FP16 memory footprint.[27] This approach enables systems to execute FP16 models by default while switching to FP8 during load surges to maintain throughput, thereby mitigating the additional memory overhead typically associated with storing dual model versions.[27]
Furthermore, the **MatKV** scheme addresses the energy-intensive nature of the prefill phase in RAG by precomputing KV vectors of RAG objects and materializing them in fast, power-efficient flash storage.[26] By reusing these precomputed vectors instead of recomputing them on costly GPUs, MatKV significantly reduces the time and energy required for processing long inputs, a critical improvement for edge environments with limited power budgets.[26]

## Scheduling and Latency Optimization

Effective request scheduling is crucial for meeting latency requirements, particularly given the unpredictability of generation lengths in LLMs. Conventional sequence-level scheduling (SLS) often suffers from low throughput due to static batching and first-come first-served (FCFS) policies, which delay short requests until long ones are completed.[28] In response, iteration-level scheduling (ILS) has been introduced to improve throughput via continuous batching, though it may limit parallel processing capabilities.[28]
For reasoning-based LLMs that utilize Chain-of-Thought (CoT) inference, the extended reasoning phase can delay user-visible output and inflate Time-To-First-Token (TTFT). The **PASCAL** scheduler addresses this by distinguishing between reasoning and answering phases, prioritizing reasoning to reduce TTFT while using controlled preemption during the answering phase to preserve Quality-of-Experience (QoE).[29] Experimental results indicate that PASCAL can reduce tail TTFT by up to 72% while maintaining SLO attainment for the answering phase.[29]

## Hardware-Aware Co-Design and Adaptive Inference

To further enhance efficiency, several frameworks employ algorithm-hardware co-design and adaptive inference strategies. **EdgeBERT** utilizes entropy-based early exit predication to perform dynamic voltage-frequency scaling (DVFS) at sentence granularity, minimizing energy consumption while adhering to target latency.[17] This is complemented by adaptive attention span, selective network pruning, and floating-point quantization to alleviate computation and memory overheads.[17]
Similarly, **AdaLLaVA** introduces an adaptive inference framework for Multimodal LLMs that dynamically reconfigures operations during inference based on input data and latency budgets.[24] This adaptability allows the system to respond to varying runtime conditions, such as resource contention, achieving optimal accuracy-latency tradeoffs.[24] At the hardware level, accelerators like **TREA** integrate dual-precision SIMD units with structured pruning strategies to achieve high throughput under stringent area-power-latency constraints, demonstrating that low-precision time-multiplexed architectures can significantly improve edge AI performance.[22]

## Thermal and Long-Term Stability

Long-term performance stability is also a critical evaluation metric for edge devices, where thermal throttling can degrade performance within seconds due to heavy computational loads. Studies on platforms like the Raspberry Pi 4B have shown that hysteresis-based active cooling is essential to prevent thermal throttling and maintain prescribed frame rates or throughput levels during continuous operation.[7] These findings highlight that performance evaluation must account not only for peak computational metrics but also for the sustained operational stability of edge hardware under thermal stress.[7]

# Limitations and Future Directions

Despite the advancements in deploying Large Language Models (LLMs) on edge devices, significant challenges remain regarding resource constraints and the interdependence of training and inference workloads. The massive parameter scales of LLMs impose critical demands on computational power and memory, which are inherently limited on edge hardware.[30] Furthermore, bandwidth bottlenecks at the network edge restrict distributed deployment and real-time responsiveness, creating a complex environment for efficient inference.[30] While existing lightweight optimization techniques have been explored to mitigate these memory limitations, they often result in significant degradation of model accuracy and performance, highlighting a persistent trade-off between efficiency and quality.[30]
Current approaches often treat fine-tuning and inference as isolated workloads, which overlooks their interdependence and leads to redundant deployments and delayed improvements in inference quality.[31] Although recent advances in federated parameter-efficient fine-tuning (FL PEFT) and low-latency inference have improved individual task performance, the lack of unified frameworks for co-execution on shared edge replicas remains a limitation.[31] Additionally, for Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models, the excessive memory footprint and I/O overhead inherent in multi-expert architectures pose formidable challenges for real-time inference on resource-constrained platforms.[5] Existing static methods for MoE inference struggle with a rigid latency-accuracy trade-off, failing to fully exploit the dynamic nature of expert importance.[5]
Future directions in this field focus on developing collaborative systems that enable lossless inference across multiple memory-constrained edge devices, such as the LIME system, to address the limitations of single-device deployment.[30] Research is also moving toward co-execution frameworks like CoLLM, which unify FL PEFT and inference on shared edge replicas and model parameters to optimize resource utilization and improve inference quality.[31] Furthermore, dynamic approaches are being developed to handle the specific challenges of MoE models; for instance, DyMoE introduces dynamic mixed-precision quantization that leverages insights into expert importance skewness and depth-dependent sensitivity.[5] By implementing importance-aware prioritization, depth-adaptive scheduling, and look-ahead prefetching, future systems aim to preserve semantic integrity in critical layers while overlapping I/O stalls to achieve high-performance edge inference.[5]
