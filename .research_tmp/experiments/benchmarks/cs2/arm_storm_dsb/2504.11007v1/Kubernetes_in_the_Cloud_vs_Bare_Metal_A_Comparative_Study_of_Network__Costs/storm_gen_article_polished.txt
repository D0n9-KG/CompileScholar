# summary

**Kubernetes in the Cloud vs. Bare Metal: A Comparative Study of Network Costs** is a technical analysis that evaluates the financial and performance implications of deploying containerized workloads on public cloud infrastructure versus dedicated physical hardware. The study addresses the growing complexity of network expenditures in cloud-native environments, where egress fees and overlay networking overheads can significantly exceed compute costs, particularly for data-intensive applications [1][2]. By integrating holistic cost models with empirical performance measurements, the research provides a framework for operators to optimize routing, peering, and resource allocation decisions, demonstrating that adjusting the routing for a small fraction of total flows can yield substantial cost reductions [3].
The comparative analysis highlights distinct trade-offs between the elasticity of cloud providers and the deterministic performance of bare-metal systems. While cloud environments offer scalability and access to auctioned underutilized resources, they introduce challenges such as performance interference from multi-tenancy and significant egress penalties for inter-region data transfers [4][5]. In contrast, bare-metal infrastructure, often preferred for high-performance computing and 5G network functions, provides direct hardware access and lower latency but requires operators to manage the full spectrum of provisioning and network complexity [6][2]. The study utilizes Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) models to quantify performance interference across shared CPU, memory, and I/O resources, enabling more accurate predictions of execution times and resource requirements in co-hosted scenarios [5].
Key findings indicate that network architecture choices, such as the use of Segment Routing over IPv6 (SRv6) with the Calico CNI plugin, can enhance traffic engineering capabilities in multi-datacenter Kubernetes deployments, offering advantages over traditional IP-in-IP overlays [7]. However, the adoption of cloud-assisted networks and service meshes introduces new overheads, including the risk of Economic Denial of Sustainability (EDoS) attacks that exploit auto-scaling mechanisms to inflate costs [8]. To mitigate these financial risks, the study advocates for cost-aware application design that decomposes infrastructure into computation, network, and storage components, allowing for precise evaluation of Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) across hybrid and multi-cloud scenarios [9][10].
The research concludes that while cloud platforms provide essential flexibility for modern IT workloads, bare-metal solutions remain critical for applications demanding strict Quality of Service (QoS) guarantees and high throughput [11][6]. Effective cost management requires a nuanced approach that balances the lower upfront capital expenditure of cloud services against the long-term operational costs of network traffic and security compliance [12][11]. By identifying specific "pain points" in container runtime selection and network scalability, the study offers actionable insights for enterprises navigating the transition to cloud-native architectures, emphasizing the need for specialized financial management disciplines like FinOps to optimize resource utilization [13][14].

# Methodology

The investigation into the comparative network costs of Kubernetes in cloud environments versus bare-metal systems employs a multi-faceted methodology that integrates cost modeling, performance measurement, and empirical experimentation. Central to this approach is the development of holistic cost models that allow operators to evaluate the financial implications of various routing, peering, and resource allocation decisions. By utilizing real traffic data from large carrier networks, these models demonstrate that adjusting the routing for a small fraction of total flows can significantly reduce overall costs, providing a framework for evaluating potential peering arrangements and other network operations problems [3]. This economic perspective is further extended to multi-provider hybrid cloud scenarios, where methodologies are applied to create cost-aware cloud applications by decomposing infrastructure into computation, network, and storage components [9].
To systematically evaluate these costs, researchers have proposed new costing models that combine compute, disk storage, and memory requirements to assess the strengths and weaknesses of different cloud service providers [10]. In the specific context of containerized applications, the methodology focuses on analyzing network usage and cost implications within Kubernetes clusters, an area previously underexplored compared to system-level optimization strategies [13]. This analysis often involves a combination of measurement analysis, experimentation, and cost modeling to understand how modern cloud-native applications, which utilize managed services and containerization technologies, impact financial outcomes as usage scales [13].
Performance interference and resource characterization are also critical components of the methodological framework. A Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA)-based model has been developed to estimate performance interference across containers caused by the use of CPU, memory, and I/O in co-hosted applications [5]. This approach provides resource characterization through human-comprehensible indices expressed as time series, enabling the analysis of interference over the entire execution lifetime of a service [5]. Experiments based on the combination of real services with different profiles executed in Docker containers suggest that such models can accurately predict overall execution time, thereby informing service design and resource allocation strategies [5].
Furthermore, the methodology incorporates the evaluation of specific networking technologies and their cost-benefit ratios in large-scale distributed scenarios. For instance, the integration of Segment Routing over IPv6 (SRv6) with Kubernetes, particularly through the Calico CNI plugin and its VPP-based variant, is examined for its potential to offer high-performance overlay networking and traffic engineering capabilities [7]. The benefits of such solutions are assessed against traditional overlays, such as IP in IP, to determine their viability in multi-datacenter environments [7]. Additionally, the methodology accounts for the significant impact of egress networking costs in data-intensive applications, where these costs can exceed compute costs [1]. To mitigate this, the study of dedicated network links, such as those provisioned via Internet2 Cloud Connect for high-performance computing bursts, provides technical details on how logically-dedicated links from major cloud providers can lower networking costs while managing the added complexity [1]. These approaches are contextualized within broader investigations into container runtimes, such as those conducted by the Aristotle Cloud Federation, which aim to identify user "pain points" and share lessons learned regarding the selection and use of containers for scientific research [14].

# Cloud Provider Analysis

The landscape of cloud computing is characterized by a diverse array of service offerings from major vendors, including Amazon Web Services (AWS), Microsoft Azure, and the Google Cloud Platform (GCP). While these providers have significantly increased their market share and revenue, there remains a lack of universal common ground regarding terminology, service functionality, and billing models.[15] This fragmentation is exacerbated by the fact that each cloud provider has developed its own service taxonomy, leading to an exponential growth in specialized services that complicates the selection process for enterprises moving away from privately-owned data centers.[15]

## Service Taxonomy and Cost Models

Cloud services are generally categorized into three primary models: Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS), Platform as a Service (PaaS), and Software as a Service (SaaS).[12] For organizations seeking to improve IT infrastructure capabilities, such as online media companies handling high traffic volumes, IaaS is often utilized to provide the necessary uptime and scalability.[12] However, the economic evaluation of these services is complex. Research indicates that there is limited investigation into the strengths and weaknesses of cost models relative to different usage requirements.[10] To address this, new costing models have been proposed that systematically evaluate cloud services by combining compute, disk storage, and memory requirements, allowing for the precise calculation of service provision costs from leading providers.[10]
In multi-provider hybrid cloud scenarios, the optimization of applications often focuses on economic efficiency. Traditional solutions typically allocate resources based on current prices, but newer methodologies aim to create cost-aware applications by breaking down infrastructure into computation, network, and storage components.[9] This approach allows for a more granular analysis of costs, which is critical when comparing cloud-based solutions against bare-metal or on-premises alternatives.

## Network Performance and Throughput

The value of cloud elasticity is tightly coupled to the capabilities of the networks connecting resources within public clouds and between on-premises facilities.[16] Benchmarking studies of the networking capabilities of AWS, Azure, and GCP have measured sustained throughput for file transfers within public clouds, as well as data fetching from on-prem resources to cloud instances and vice versa.[16] These measurements are essential for understanding the performance implications of cloud architectures, particularly when compared to the direct control offered by bare-metal environments.
Furthermore, the steady decline in IP transit prices over the past two decades has fueled traffic growth, while cloud providers have begun auctioning underutilized computing resources at lower prices than on-demand instances.[4] This economic dynamic has led to the development of cloud-assisted networks, which offload tasks to the cloud to provide more flexible and cost-effective network service chains.[4] However, the cost of hosting nodes and the network traffic imposed by emerging technologies, such as blockchain, further complicate the economic analysis of cloud versus bare-metal deployments.[17]

## Quality of Service and Risk Factors

Hybrid cloud computing models are increasingly adopted due to their IaaS architecture, customization options, and cost benefits.[11] The quality of these services is measured by Quality of Service (QoS) parameters defined by public cloud vendors, such as availability, scalability, and latency.[11] These parameters vary significantly from vendor to vendor, creating complexity in service assessment.[11] Cloud Service Level Agreements (SLAs) outline the QoS provisions, objectives, and exclusions, but tenants remain susceptible to risks such as data governance issues, denial of service attacks, and multi-tenancy concerns.[11]
Unlike traditional utilities, cloud computing involves a distinct amount of risk associated with data processing and storage.[11] This risk profile is a critical factor when comparing cloud providers to bare-metal solutions, where organizations retain full control over the physical infrastructure and data security. The ability to configure cloud services as needed offers flexibility, but it also introduces dependencies on the provider's network and service reliability, which must be carefully weighed against the capital and operational costs of maintaining bare-metal infrastructure.[12]

# Bare Metal Infrastructure Analysis

Bare metal infrastructure, characterized by the direct deployment of applications on physical hardware without the abstraction layer of virtualization or container orchestration, offers distinct performance and cost characteristics compared to cloud-native environments. In the context of high-performance computing and telecommunications, bare metal systems are often preferred for workloads that demand deterministic latency and maximum throughput, such as data plane packet processing in 5G networks. These applications, which include control, signaling, and media processing, have critical processing requirements that differ significantly from generic cloud-native IT applications, often necessitating direct hardware access to meet stringent Quality of Service (QoS) standards [6].
The economic and operational dynamics of bare metal infrastructure are heavily influenced by network costs and resource utilization. For data-intensive scientific applications, such as high-throughput computational tasks, networking costs can frequently exceed compute costs, particularly when egress traffic is involved [2]. While dedicated network links can lower these networking costs, they introduce additional complexity to the infrastructure management [2]. In contrast to cloud environments where providers auction underutilized resources at lower prices, bare metal deployments require operators to manage the full spectrum of hardware and network provisioning, making cost models for routing and peering decisions critical for optimizing traffic carrying costs [4][3].
Furthermore, the transition from hardware-bound Physical Network Functions (PNFs) to cloud-native deployments highlights the specific challenges associated with maintaining bare metal performance in hybrid scenarios. As Telecommunication Service Providers (TSPs) collaborate with public cloud providers, the shift towards containerized deployments on platforms like Kubernetes introduces technical and operational complexities that must be understood to ensure that the scalability and flexibility benefits do not compromise the performance guarantees inherent to bare metal or dedicated hardware setups [18]. The emerging paradigm of resource disaggregation, which enables the deployment of cloud-like services across pools of physical and virtualized resources, further blurs the lines between traditional bare metal and cloud infrastructure, emphasizing the need for network and system-level support for time-sensitive communication in mixed-criticality scenarios [19].

# Comparative Cost Factors

The economic evaluation of cloud infrastructure versus bare-metal deployments involves a complex interplay of compute, storage, memory, and network costs. While cloud providers offer a wide range of commercially available services, research indicates that there is limited systematic investigation into the strengths and weaknesses of their cost models relative to specific usage requirements.[10] A holistic costing model that combines compute, disk storage, and memory requirements is essential for accurately evaluating service provision costs, as demonstrated by studies calculating precise costs from leading cloud providers using real-world industrial data center workloads.[10]
Network costs constitute a significant portion of the total expenditure, particularly when considering the disparity between core internet hubs and remote regions.[4] The steady decline in IP transit prices over the past two decades has fueled traffic growth, yet the cost of mobile devices and associated connectivity remains significant.[4] For network operators, a holistic cost model can be used to evaluate routing and peering decisions, showing that adjusting the routing for a small fraction of total flows can significantly reduce the cost of carrying traffic.[3] In the context of cloud-assisted networks, providers often auction underutilized computing resources, such as virtual machines, at lower prices compared to on-demand instances, offering a more cost-effective way to manage flexible network service chains.[4]
The choice between transient (spot) and on-demand virtual machines (VMs) presents a critical trade-off between cost and reliability, particularly for Container Orchestration Platforms (COPs) like Kubernetes.[20] Transient VMs are significantly cheaper than on-demand VMs but can be revoked at any time, posing challenges for platforms supporting mixed workloads that include both delay-tolerant batch jobs and long-lived interactive services with high availability requirements.[20] To mitigate this, systems such as TR-Kubernetes have been designed to balance the attractive pricing of transient VMs with the reliability needs of critical services.[20]
Furthermore, performance interference and communication overheads directly impact operational costs. In cloud systems, performance interference occurs when various services are executed over the same physical infrastructure, leading to degradation compared to isolated execution.[5] Models based on Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) can estimate this interference across containers caused by CPU, memory, and IO usage, allowing service designers to identify optimal configurations to avoid unnecessary resource over-provisioning.[5] Similarly, in parallel computing and high-performance applications, the cost of communication—both intra-node and inter-node—is a key determinant of efficiency.[21][22] For instance, algebraic multigrid (AMG) solvers often lack parallel scalability due to high communication costs, which can be mitigated by restructuring communication schedules to leverage less costly intra-node transfers.[22]
Security and resilience also introduce economic factors. The emergence of Economic Denial of Sustainability (EDoS) attacks, such as the YoYo attack, exploits cloud auto-scaling mechanisms to cause oscillations between scale-up and scale-down phases.[8] This results in victims incurring economic damage by paying for extra resources required to process attacker-generated traffic, highlighting the need for resilience strategies that consider both performance degradation and financial impact.[8] Additionally, the consistency of data services in the cloud requires a trade-off between consistency guarantees and performance, with monetary cost being a crucial metric for evaluating the efficiency of these trade-offs.[23] Finally, the network traffic imposed by emerging technologies like blockchain increases the cost of hosting nodes, further complicating the comparative cost analysis between cloud and bare-metal environments.[17]

# Network Architecture and Overhead

The transition from traditional hardware-bound Physical Network Functions (PNFs) to cloud-native, containerized deployments managed through platforms like Kubernetes represents a fundamental shift in network architecture.[18] This softwareization of networks, enabled by paradigms such as Software-Defined Networking (SDN), Network Virtualization (NV), and Network Function Virtualization (NFV), offers significant advantages for scalability and flexibility but introduces complex technical challenges.[24] In cloud environments, the network architecture must accommodate the dynamic and ephemeral nature of virtualized resources, which often results in distinct overheads compared to bare-metal implementations.
A primary architectural consideration in Kubernetes-based cloud deployments is the management of overlay networks. While Kubernetes provides standardized APIs for container orchestration, practical issues arise when replicating applications across distributed edge, fog, and cloud sites, where feature incompatibility between different vendors can complicate network configuration.[25] To address connectivity in large-scale and distributed multi-datacenter scenarios, extensions to Kubernetes networking have been developed to utilize Segment Routing over IPv6 (SRv6). For instance, integrating SRv6 with the Calico CNI plugin, particularly the Calico-VPP variant, allows for high-performance overlay networking that supports Traffic Engineering, offering advantages over traditional IP-in-IP overlays.[7] However, the use of network overlays in cloud settings, such as for bulk data transfers between regions, can lead to unacceptable increases in egress prices if not carefully managed, necessitating cloud-aware routing strategies to balance performance and cost.[26]
The overhead associated with these virtualized network architectures is further compounded by the need for robust security and monitoring mechanisms. Anomaly detection in multitenant virtual networks presents significant challenges due to complex traffic patterns, requiring strategic placement of capture positions within both overlay and underlay networks to ensure efficacy.[27] Additionally, the segregation of SDN controller functionalities into microservices, as seen in intelligent control networks, can impact performance; while distributing these services across a Kubernetes cluster can decrease latency by up to 10.7% in some microgrid applications, it also introduces the complexity of managing distributed state.[28]
Performance interference is another critical overhead in cloud-native network architectures, where multiple services executing on the same physical infrastructure can lead to degradation compared to isolated execution.[5] Models based on Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) have been proposed to estimate this interference across containers caused by shared CPU, memory, and I/O resources, allowing service designers to predict execution times and optimize resource allocation.[5] Furthermore, the economic overhead of network traffic is a significant factor, with studies indicating that the transport protocol is the main driver of bandwidth consumption in distributed systems, and that segregating node roles can help reduce overall traffic costs.[17] Consequently, holistic cost models are increasingly being developed to help operators evaluate routing and peering decisions, demonstrating that adjusting the routing for even a small fraction of total flows can significantly reduce the cost of carrying traffic in large carrier networks.[3]

# Performance and Efficiency Metrics

The evaluation of performance and efficiency in cloud environments, particularly when comparing containerized deployments like Kubernetes against traditional infrastructure, involves analyzing multiple dimensions including consistency, resource interference, and network costs. In the context of cloud computing, where organizations migrate data to reduce processing costs and time, the trade-off between consistency guarantees and performance improvement is a critical area of study. Recent analyses argue that monetary cost should be integrated into these evaluations, proposing metrics such as "consistency-cost efficiency" to provide an economical assessment of service provider consistency levels[23].
Resource management within cloud-native architectures, such as those utilizing Kubernetes for microservices, faces specific challenges related to performance degradation. For instance, in service mesh implementations, fully releasing configurations to the data plane during business development can lead to noticeable performance issues. To address this, fine-grained traffic management is essential; algorithms like DATM utilize inter-service dependencies to manage data-plane traffic dynamically, enabling on-demand distribution that reduces memory usage and maintains cluster service stability[29]. Furthermore, performance interference is a significant concern when multiple services share physical infrastructure. Models based on Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) have been developed to estimate this interference across containers caused by shared CPU, memory, and I/O resources. These models provide human-comprehensible indices expressed as time series, allowing service designers to accurately predict overall execution times and identify potential bottlenecks in co-hosted applications[5].
Network costs and efficiency are also pivotal in determining the overall performance of cloud-assisted systems. The decline in IP transit prices has fueled traffic growth, yet the disparity between core hubs and remote regions remains a cost factor. Cloud providers often auction underutilized resources, such as virtual machines, at lower prices than on-demand instances, influencing the economic viability of network service chains[4]. In high-throughput scientific applications, egress networking costs can frequently exceed compute costs. To mitigate this, dedicated network links, such as those provisioned via Internet2 Cloud Connect for IceCube production compute, have been used to enable high-capacity egress (e.g., 100 Gbps) to on-prem storage, thereby lowering networking costs despite the added complexity[1][2]. Additionally, in the realm of blockchain, which relies heavily on network traffic, empirical studies indicate that the transport protocol is the primary factor impacting bandwidth consumption, while segregating node roles can help reduce overall traffic costs[17]. As Telecommunication Service Providers (TSPs) shift from hardware-bound Physical Network Functions to cloud-native, containerized deployments managed by Kubernetes, understanding these performance and cost dynamics is crucial for achieving scalability and cost efficiency in 5G and beyond networks[18].

# Case Studies

Several recent studies and industry implementations have examined the performance, cost, and usability of cloud-native technologies, providing empirical data relevant to the comparison of Kubernetes in cloud versus bare-metal environments.
**Scientific Computing and HPC Workloads**
A National Science Foundation-sponsored investigation by the Aristotle Cloud Federation focused on the challenges of selecting and using container runtimes, including Docker, Singularity, and X-Containers, for scientific research.[14] This study aimed to identify user "pain points" and included application performance characteristics, as well as user experiences with Kubernetes and container orchestration on both cloud and High-Performance Computing (HPC) platforms.[14] Similarly, a cross-platform usability study assessed 11 different HPC proxy applications and benchmarks across three major cloud providers (Microsoft Azure, Amazon Web Services, and Google Cloud) and six environments.[30] This research compared cloud environments against on-premises HPC clusters, performing scaling tests up to 28,672 CPUs and 256 GPUs to evaluate the fit of converged cloud environments for HPC use cases, which increasingly demand portability, reproducibility, and automation.[30]
**Network Overlays and Data Transfer Costs**
The economic implications of cloud networking are highlighted by research into bulk data transfers. A study on the Skyplane system demonstrated that while network overlays can significantly improve inter-region cloud bulk transfer throughput by routing data through indirect paths at the application layer, directly applying these overlays can lead to unacceptable increases in cloud egress prices.[26] Skyplane uses mixed-integer linear programming to determine optimal overlay paths and resource allocation, explicitly navigating the trade-off between price and performance for data transfer between cloud object stores.[26] This underscores the critical importance of cost-aware network design in cloud-native architectures.
**Service Mesh and Edge Computing**
In the context of microservice-based applications, a systematic analysis of state-of-the-art service mesh technologies evaluated their suitability for performance-demanding workloads in mobile edge cloud environments.[31] The study argued that current dependability-centric service mesh designs often fail to address the specific traffic management needs of emerging mobile edge cloud workloads, motivating further research into performance-efficient architectures.[31] This contrasts with the more centralized cloud environments where service mesh is widely adopted for generic IT workloads.[31]
**Infrastructure and Server Configuration**
Research into server selection and configuration has proposed technologies for automatic performance verification to meet user functional and performance requirements across various cloud compute servers.[32] This includes not only virtual machines on normal CPU servers but also container and bare-metal servers on strong GPU or FPGA servers.[32] The study notes that while early cloud systems relied on virtual machines for distributed processing, recent systems leverage advances in hardware power, with bare-metal and container performances generally outperforming virtual machines.[32] Additionally, the integration of distributed stream processing with microservice architectures has been benchmarked to evaluate the scalability of frameworks such as Apache Flink and Apache Kafka Streams in different execution environments.[33]
**IT Outsourcing and Media Infrastructure**
From a business perspective, the adoption of cloud services as a form of IT outsourcing has been analyzed for its impact on company capabilities.[12] For instance, detikNetwork utilized Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS) to improve its IT infrastructure capability, allowing it to handle huge traffic volumes and provide more uptime.[12] This case illustrates how cloud services can be configured as needed to meet the demands of online media businesses, aligning with the broader trend of using cloud platforms for scalable and resilient infrastructure.[12]

# Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) Analysis

The Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) for network infrastructure is increasingly influenced by the shift from traditional hardware-bound deployments to cloud-native architectures, particularly those managed through platforms like Kubernetes. As Telecommunication Service Providers (TSPs) and enterprises move toward cloud-native, containerized deployments for 5G and beyond networks, the TCO model must account for the trade-offs between the scalability and flexibility of cloud environments and the specific operational costs associated with network functions [18]. This transition introduces a complex set of technical and operational challenges that directly impact long-term financial outcomes, requiring a holistic evaluation of both direct and indirect costs [18].
A critical component of TCO analysis in this context is the evaluation of routing and peering decisions. Operators can utilize holistic cost models to evaluate the financial implications of various network configurations, demonstrating that adjusting the routing for even a small fraction of total flows can significantly reduce the cost of carrying traffic [3]. These models allow for the assessment of potential peering arrangements, which is essential for optimizing the cost structure in multi-cloud or hybrid environments where traffic flows between private clouds and multiple on-premises or public clouds [3][34]. In such multi-cloud deployments, the establishment of site-to-site encrypted tunnels to connect workloads adds a layer of complexity to the TCO, necessitating benchmarks of various tunneling and encryption technologies to inform design and development decisions [34].
Furthermore, the TCO of cloud-based network services is heavily dependent on the underlying resource pricing models. Cloud providers often auction underutilized computing resources, such as virtual machines, at lower prices compared to on-demand instances, which can significantly lower the TCO for elastic workloads [4]. However, a systematic costing model is required to accurately evaluate these services by combining compute, disk storage, and memory requirements, as commercial cloud services vary in their cost structures relative to different usage requirements [10]. For instance, analyzing real-world industrial data center workloads reveals that precise cost calculations from leading cloud providers are necessary to determine the most cost-effective service provision [10].
In the context of parallel and distributed systems, which are fundamental to cloud-native network functions, the TCO also encompasses execution and communication costs. The task allocation problem (TAP) aims to minimize total execution cost and internode communication cost, with newer models considering additive intranode communication costs in multicore cluster systems [21]. Understanding the complexity of these communication costs is key to optimizing the TCO in high-performance cloud simulation and modeling environments, where scalability issues are often tackled through the elasticity of on-demand cloud deployment [35]. By integrating these factors—routing efficiency, peering strategies, resource pricing, and communication overhead—operators can develop a comprehensive TCO analysis that supports the transition to cloud-native network architectures [3][21][18][35][10].

# Challenges and Limitations

The deployment of Kubernetes in cloud environments presents several distinct challenges and limitations, particularly regarding network performance, cost management, and security. While containerization offers scalability, the underlying network infrastructure often introduces bottlenecks that affect both throughput and financial efficiency.

## Network Performance and Scalability

One of the primary technical limitations is the degradation of network throughput as the number of pods increases. Studies evaluating popular container network interface plugins, such as Cilium and Calico, have demonstrated that while these technologies scale well in terms of policy management, they suffer from significant performance drops under high pod density. Specifically, increasing the number of pods from one to forty results in approximately 50% throughput degradation in both technologies, posing challenges for high-density multi-tenant environments like digital marketplaces [36].
Additionally, the management of service mesh configurations can lead to performance issues. Fully releasing configurations to the data plane during business development phases can result in noticeable performance degradation due to the overhead of fine-grained traffic management [29]. To mitigate this, novel configuration distribution algorithms have been proposed to manage data-plane traffic dynamically based on inter-service dependencies, aiming to reduce memory usage and improve efficiency [29].

## Cost Implications and Optimization

Financial considerations are a major limitation when running Kubernetes clusters in the cloud, as network costs are often underexplored compared to compute and storage expenses. Modern cloud-native applications utilizing managed services and containerization face complex cost structures that require specialized financial management disciplines, such as FinOps, to optimize [13].
Wide-area bulk data transfers between cloud regions are frequently slow and can bottleneck applications. While network overlays can improve inter-region throughput by routing data through indirect paths, directly applying these overlays can lead to unacceptable increases in cloud egress prices [26]. Systems like Skyplane have been developed to address this by using cloud-aware network overlays and mixed-integer linear programming to navigate the trade-off between price and performance, highlighting the complexity of optimizing network costs in multi-region cloud scenarios [26].
Furthermore, the lack of systematic research into the strengths and weaknesses of cloud cost models for different usage requirements complicates accurate budgeting. Proposed costing models that combine compute, disk storage, and memory requirements are necessary to evaluate the precise cost of service provision, as standard models may not accurately reflect the total cost of ownership for containerized workloads [10]. In multi-provider hybrid cloud scenarios, optimizing applications from an economic point of view requires breaking down infrastructure into computation, network, and storage components to maximize cost efficiency [9].

## Security and Privacy

Security and privacy present additional limitations, particularly in multi-tenant and federated learning contexts. In digital marketplaces, which rely on container-based networks for multi-tenancy, ensuring security policy scalability is critical, yet the interaction between network policies and pod scalability remains a challenge [36].
In the context of Federated Learning (FL) deployed on Kubernetes, device heterogeneity and the potential for malicious clients to access other resources in flat networking approaches pose significant privacy risks. Solutions such as the KubeFliner operator have been introduced to address these issues through isolation-based methods and privacy-preserving mechanisms, such as adding noise to data while managing a privacy budget, indicating that standard Kubernetes deployments may not inherently provide sufficient privacy safeguards for sensitive collaborative training tasks [37].

## Heterogeneity and Platform Specifics

The selection and use of container runtimes themselves present challenges for scientific and research computing. Investigations by the Aristotle Cloud Federation have identified "pain points" experienced by users when selecting and using containers like Docker, Singularity, and X-Containers, particularly regarding application performance characteristics and orchestration on cloud and HPC platforms [14]. These challenges extend to specialized environments, such as Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) networks, where the utilization of Kubernetes for virtual infrastructure management is still in preliminary exploration, with its suitability for supporting value-added services over aerial networks requiring further validation [38].
