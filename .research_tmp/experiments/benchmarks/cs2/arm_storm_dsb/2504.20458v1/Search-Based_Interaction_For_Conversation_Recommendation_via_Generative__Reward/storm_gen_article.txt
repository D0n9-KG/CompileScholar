# Background

Conversational Recommender Systems (CRSs) have emerged as a significant paradigm in the field of recommender systems, leveraging natural language interactions to capture user preferences and provide personalized recommendations.[1][2] Unlike traditional systems that rely on static user profiles or single-turn queries, CRSs utilize multi-turn dialogues to clarify complex and multifaceted user interests.[1] However, a fundamental challenge in this domain is the effective modeling of user preferences from dialogue histories, which are often information-scarce and may not fully reveal the user's true intent.[2] While interaction with users can help clarify these preferences, frequent user involvement can lead to a degraded user experience, creating a tension between the need for detailed preference elicitation and the need for efficient, non-intrusive service.[1]
To address the limitations of real-user interaction, recent studies have introduced Large Language Model (LLM)-based user simulators that generate natural language feedback and perform simulated multi-turn interactions to assist in recommendation.[2] These simulators allow recommender systems to learn from errors in a controlled environment without impacting real users.[3] For instance, frameworks like RecoWorld propose dual-view architectures where a simulated user and an agentic recommender engage in multi-turn interactions aimed at maximizing user retention.[3] In such systems, the user simulator reviews recommended items, updates its mindset, and generates reflective instructions when sensing potential disengagement, thereby creating a dynamic feedback loop that leverages the reasoning capabilities of modern LLMs.[3] Similarly, other approaches propose generative reward model-based simulated users, such as GRSU, which provide feedback to items recommended by CRSs to help the system capture intricate user preferences through multi-turn interaction.[1]
Despite the promise of these simulation-based approaches, significant challenges remain. Since simulators cannot access true user preference labels during inference, their feedback may deviate from actual user interests, causing errors to accumulate over multiple interactions and affecting the generalization of the recommender.[2] This issue is compounded by the inherent difficulties in aligning LLMs with human preferences. Traditional reward models are often trained as discriminative models relying solely on labeled human preference data, which limits their scalability and ability to generalize.[4] To overcome this, recent research has explored generative reward models that are trained via large-scale unsupervised learning on unlabeled data and then fine-tuned via supervised learning.[4] These generative approaches, such as GRAM-R$^2$, aim to elicit explicit reward reasoning by producing not only preference labels but also accompanying rationales, thereby serving as foundation models for reward reasoning with minimal additional fine-tuning.[5]
Furthermore, the integration of generative techniques into recommendation systems has introduced new modeling challenges. Generative recommendation using semantic IDs (SIDs) has shown performance gains by capitalizing on semantic information from language model embeddings.[6] However, existing autoregressive models for SIDs suffer from expensive inference due to sequential token-wise decoding and potential biases towards short-context relationships.[6] Alternative approaches, such as masked diffusion, have been proposed to model the probability of user interaction sequences more efficiently.[6] Additionally, LLM-based generative recommendation methods often introduce systematic bias when estimating item-level preference distributions, as they approximate these distributions via token-level generation, which can lead to incomplete coverage or distorted probabilities.[7] Addressing these modeling mismatches and the need for precise credit assignment in reinforcement learning settings remains an active area of research, with methods like Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) attempting to improve reasoning abilities through rule-based feedback, though they face limitations in coarse-grained credit assignment.[8]

# Methodology

The methodology for search-based interaction in conversation recommendation via generative reward integrates advanced reinforcement learning frameworks with modular agentic architectures to address the challenges of sparse rewards and subjective quality assessment in dialogue systems. Central to this approach is the development of Generative Reward Models (GRMs), which shift the paradigm from traditional discriminative models to generative ones capable of producing explicit reasoning alongside preference labels. Unlike standard reward models that rely heavily on large-scale labeled preference data, GRMs leverage unlabeled data through self-training or unsupervised pre-training to elicit reward reasoning, thereby reducing dependency on costly human annotations [5][4]. This generative approach allows the model to provide multi-dimensional analysis and explicit rationales for story or dialogue preferences, which is crucial for mitigating training instability and designing reliable reward signals for subjective tasks [9].
To manage the complexity of interaction and reward generation, the system employs a modular architecture of LLM-powered agents, often structured around roles such as Master, Planner, Executor, and Writer [10]. These agents collaborate dynamically to evaluate query complexity, decompose problems into executable plans, and orchestrate tool usage and content synthesis [10]. In the context of recommendation and conversation, this agentic framework facilitates a dual-view architecture where a simulated user and an agentic recommender engage in multi-turn interactions [3]. The user simulator updates its mindset based on recommended items and generates reflective instructions upon sensing potential disengagement, while the recommender adapts its strategy by incorporating these instructions and reasoning traces, creating a dynamic feedback loop that leverages the reasoning capabilities of modern LLMs [3].
The training process addresses the inherent sparsity of rewards in dialogue sessions, where feedback is typically only available at the end of an interaction [11]. To overcome this, the methodology decomposes adversarial training into steps that integrate a pre-trained language model as a discriminator to judge the quality of system actions relative to user actions, providing local dense rewards to guide agent exploration [11]. Furthermore, to ensure the validity of reasoning trajectories, the system employs techniques such as Groupwise Ranking Reward, which ranks verifier-passed trajectories for the same prompt in a single pass to redistribute rewards more effectively than standard reward models or unstable generative rewards [12]. This approach helps bridge the gap between answer correctness and reasoning validity, ensuring that the model does not rely on incomplete derivations or weak evidence [12].
Finally, the integration of Reinforcement Learning (RL) fine-tuning enhances the internal capabilities of the LLMs used in these agentic systems. Analysis indicates that RL post-training leads to an overall increase in average activation intensity, engaging more internal pathways and improving the model's ability to handle complex reasoning tasks beyond what supervised fine-tuning alone achieves [13]. By combining these generative reward mechanisms with agentic search and RL-based optimization, the methodology provides a robust framework for developing conversation recommendation systems that are both adaptive and capable of high-quality, reasoned interactions [14][15].

# Technical Details

The technical framework for search-based interaction in conversation recommendation via generative rewards integrates principles from reinforcement learning (RL), generative modeling, and semantic space optimization to address the limitations of traditional discriminative approaches. A core component of this system is the development of **Generative Reward Models (GRMs)**, which diverge from standard discriminative reward models that rely solely on labeled human preference data. Instead, these models are trained using both unlabeled and labeled data, leveraging the generative capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) to produce not only preference labels but also accompanying reward rationales [4][5]. This approach, exemplified by frameworks such as GRAM-R$^2$, allows for the elicitation of reward reasoning through self-training on abundant unlabeled data, thereby instilling explicit reasoning capabilities into the reward model [5]. By optimizing a regularized pairwise ranking loss via label smoothing, these generative models bridge the gap between generative and discriminative objectives, resulting in a foundation reward model that can be applied to a wide range of tasks with minimal additional fine-tuning [4].
To enhance the precision of credit assignment in reinforcement learning, particularly in scenarios involving verifiable rewards, the system addresses the coarse-grained feedback inherent in methods like Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR). Traditional RLVR methods often assign the same reward to every token, which hampers the model's ability to identify specific reasoning steps that lead to success or failure [8]. To overcome this, the technical architecture incorporates mechanisms for step-wise or trajectory supervision. This involves comparing the efficiency of standard reward models with Generative Rewards (GRs), where GRs are utilized to detect "reasoning-answer inconsistency"—a gap where an answer is correct but the derivation is weak or contradictory [12]. By providing more granular feedback, the system avoids the inaccuracies associated with value estimation in methods like PPO and the high computational costs of process reward models in online RL [8].
The interaction layer leverages **Semantic Space Conversation Planning** to optimize real-time dialogue efficiency. Existing simulation-based planning algorithms are often impractical for real-time applications due to the high computational cost of simulating future conversations with numerous LLM queries at each turn [16]. The proposed approach, such as SCOPE (Semantic space COnversation Planning with improved Efficiency), exploits the dense semantic representation of conversations to select optimal LLM responses without exhaustive simulation [16]. This is particularly critical in multi-turn interactions, such as those found in e-commerce or career goal recommendation, where user intent evolves iteratively [17][18]. In these domains, the cost of a bad recommendation is high, necessitating an interactive recommender framework that balances relevance, achievability, and aspirational goals [18].
Furthermore, the system addresses the challenge of sparse rewards in dialogue policy training, where rewards are typically only provided at the end of a session. To mitigate this, the technical design may decompose adversarial training into steps that integrate a pre-trained language model as a discriminator to provide local dense rewards, guiding the agent's exploration more effectively than methods that suffer from local optima or model collapse [11]. This dense reward signal is crucial for training dialogue policy agents to maximize accumulated rewards from users, ensuring that the conversation planning process remains both efficient and aligned with long-term user engagement goals [16][11].

# Evaluation

Evaluating systems for conversation recommendation and generative interaction presents significant challenges, particularly when balancing algorithmic efficiency with human-centric quality metrics. Traditional technical benchmarks often lack real-world relevance, while existing human preference evaluations suffer from unrepresentative sampling, superficial assessment depth, and single-metric reductionism[19]. To address these limitations, recent frameworks have introduced multidimensional, demographically aware measurement approaches. For instance, the HUMAINE framework collected multi-turn, naturalistic conversations from over 23,000 participants stratified across 22 demographic groups in the US and UK to evaluate state-of-the-art models across five human-centric dimensions[19]. This approach utilizes a hierarchical Bayesian Bradley-Terry-Davidson model with post-stratification to census data, revealing clear performance hierarchies among large language models[19].
In the context of recommender systems, evaluation must also account for the exploration–exploitation trade-off, where algorithms must balance selecting items with uncertain user interest against those with high predicted ratings[20]. Because humans are part of the interaction loop, long-term trade-off behavior depends on human variability, necessitating evaluation methods that characterize this dynamic[20]. Furthermore, generative models in recommendation systems (Gen-RecSys) are evaluated on their ability to improve accuracy and diversity by generating structured outputs and multimedia content, thereby creating more personalized and engaging user experiences[21].
Specific to dialogue and conversational agents, evaluation often focuses on mitigating issues such as the "generic response problem," where systems produce vague answers applicable to many user utterances[22]. Systems are increasingly evaluated using explicit turn-level feedback on coherence and engagement, which has been shown to be highly correlated with human evaluation[22]. In visual dialogue tasks, where agents must maintain consistent and natural dialogue while answering questions about images, evaluation criteria include the naturalness of responses and the ability to generate human-interpretable reasons for answers[23].
The integration of Reinforcement Learning (RL) into these systems introduces specific evaluation challenges related to reward sparsity and reasoning validity. In dialogue policy training, rewards are often sparse, provided only at the end of a session, which can lead to unaffordable interaction requirements for training[11]. To address this, some approaches decompose adversarial training to provide local dense rewards, evaluating the agent's ability to predict next actions effectively[11]. Additionally, in multimodal reasoning, a gap exists between answer correctness and reasoning validity, known as reasoning-answer inconsistency[12]. Evaluation methods now compare reward models (RMs) and Generative Rewards (GRs), noting that while RMs are efficient, their gains weaken as policy distributions shift, whereas GRs may provide unstable rewards[12]. New metrics, such as Groupwise Ranking Reward, are being developed to rank verifier-passed trajectories, ensuring that models not only produce correct answers but also valid derivations[12].

# Discussion

The integration of generative models and reward mechanisms into conversational recommendation systems represents a significant paradigm shift from traditional static pipelines to dynamic, interactive frameworks. Central to this evolution is the challenge of balancing exploration and exploitation within the dialogue process. Early approaches to interactive recommendation focused on determining optimal question strategies and managing the balance between soliciting user preferences and providing immediate recommendations, often controlled by adjustable parameters to optimize user engagement and satisfaction [24]. As these systems have matured, the field has moved toward more sophisticated neural architectures that categorize conversational agents into question answering, task-oriented dialogue, and general chatbots, each requiring distinct strategies for maintaining context and naturalness [25].
A critical component of modern conversational recommendation is the ability to generate human-like, contextually appropriate responses. This has led to the adoption of techniques such as Reinforcement Learning (RL) combined with Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs), which help overcome the limitations of maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) that often results in overly terse or unnatural answers [23]. In the domain of visual dialogue, for instance, maintaining a consistent dialogue while answering questions about images requires tight integration of generative models with attention mechanisms to provide interpretable reasons for recommendations [23]. Similarly, in broader recommender systems, the shift toward generative recommendation (GR) using semantic IDs has introduced new challenges. While autoregressive models are effective, they suffer from unidirectional constraints and error accumulation, where early prediction errors propagate through the sequence [26]. To address this, recent frameworks like LLaDA-Rec and masked diffusion models propose parallel or bidirectional generation strategies to better model inter-item dependencies and alleviate the inefficiencies of sequential token-wise decoding [26][6].
However, the transition from token-level generation to item-level preference modeling introduces systematic biases. Existing methods often approximate item-level distributions via token-level generation, leading to incomplete coverage due to beam search pruning or probability distortion from assuming token independence [7]. This modeling mismatch highlights the need for more robust reward mechanisms that can guide the generation process more effectively. The development of generalist reward models, such as GRAM-R$^2$, aims to bridge this gap by leveraging unlabeled data to elicit explicit reward reasoning, allowing the model to produce not just preference labels but also rationales that can be applied across various tasks with minimal fine-tuning [5].
Furthermore, the application of reinforcement learning in these systems faces the challenge of sparse rewards and coarse-grained feedback. Traditional methods often assign the same reward to every token, hindering precise credit assignment for specific reasoning steps [8]. To overcome this, novel approaches like Generative Exploration and Exploitation (GENE) automatically generate start states to encourage exploration and adaptively trade off between exploration and exploitation based on the agent's experience [27]. In the context of auto-bidding and other constrained optimization tasks, generative models like GRAD combine action-mixture experts to handle distribution shifts and meet real-world constraints such as Cost-per-Mille (CPM) and Return on Investment (ROI), demonstrating the versatility of generative reward-driven frameworks [28].
Finally, the reliance on external resources like knowledge graphs or large language models (LLMs) for enhancement has been questioned due to data dependency and computational overhead. Knowledge-free augmentation frameworks, such as NodeDiffRec, propose using diffusion to synthesize pseudo-items and interactions, thereby bridging semantic and structural gaps without external data [29]. As these systems become more prevalent, the potential negative consequences of algorithmic uncertainty in selecting items for users must be carefully managed to ensure that the intermediate role of these algorithms remains beneficial and transparent [20]. The ongoing development of conversational agents that jointly optimize retrieval and generation in multi-turn scenarios further underscores the complexity of handling evolving user intent and dynamic coordination in real-time interactions [30].

# Related Work

Research at the intersection of reinforcement learning (RL), large language models (LLMs), and conversational systems has advanced significantly, addressing challenges in dialogue management, recommendation, and reward modeling. This section reviews related work in three key areas: RL for dialogue and conversation planning, LLM-based recommendation systems, and the design of reward mechanisms for generative tasks.

## Reinforcement Learning in Dialogue and Conversation Planning

Reinforcement learning has been widely adopted to optimize dialogue policies by maximizing accumulated rewards from user interactions. However, a primary challenge in this domain is the sparsity of rewards, which are typically only available at the end of a dialogue session, leading to high interaction costs and potential local optima [11]. To mitigate this, some approaches decompose adversarial training into steps, utilizing pre-trained language models as discriminators to provide dense, local rewards that guide agent exploration [11]. In the context of real-time applications, simulation-based conversation planning algorithms often struggle with computational efficiency, as they require extensive LLM queries to simulate future states [16]. Recent methods, such as Semantic space COnversation Planning with improved Efficiency (SCOPE), exploit dense semantic representations to improve the efficiency of selecting optimal responses without exhaustive simulation [16]. Furthermore, multi-turn dialogue agents face the challenge of evolving user intent, requiring dynamic coordination between retrieval and generation. While deep search agents have shown effectiveness in single-turn scenarios, recent work introduces conversational agents that jointly optimize these components to handle the complexities of multi-turn interactions [30].

## LLM-Based Recommendation Systems

The advent of LLMs has transformed recommendation systems (RSs) by enabling natural language interactions for personalized recommendations, moving beyond standardized non-verbal feedback such as clicks or views [31]. These systems leverage LLMs' general reasoning capabilities to connect nuanced user preferences to items, often through interactive dialogues [31]. A significant challenge in conversational recommender systems (CRSs) is the scarcity of information in dialogue histories and the limitations of single-turn paradigms, which hinder the accurate modeling of complex user preferences [2]. To address this, LLM-based user simulators have been introduced to generate natural language feedback and perform simulated multi-turn interactions [2]. However, since these simulators lack access to true user preference labels during inference, their feedback may deviate from actual interests, causing error accumulation over multiple interactions [2]. To overcome this, frameworks like SMTPO utilize multi-step reasoning and reinforcement learning to optimize policy in a user simulator-guided manner [2]. Additionally, Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have been employed to develop user simulators that capture the underlying distribution of user behaviors, addressing the complexity of item distributions and limited historical logs [32].

## Reward Modeling and Post-Training Mechanisms

The effectiveness of RL in enhancing LLM capabilities depends heavily on the design of reward signals. While Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) provides a foundation, RL-based post-training has been shown to improve capabilities beyond what SFT alone achieves, though the underlying mechanisms remain underexplored [13]. Analysis using edge attribution patching suggests that online RL post-training leads to an overall increase in average activation intensity, indicating that more internal pathways are engaged [13]. In terms of reward paradigms, Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) and Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable Rewards (RLVR) are the dominant approaches, each with distinct limitations; RLHF suffers from interpretability issues and reward hacking, while RLVR is limited to correctness-based verification [33]. To bridge these gaps, Reinforcement Learning with Binary Flexible Feedback (RLBFF) has been proposed, combining the versatility of human preferences with the precision of rule-based verification by extracting binary principles from natural language feedback [33].
In specific generative tasks, such as creative storytelling, designing reliable reward signals for subjective quality remains a critical obstacle [9]. The Reinforcement Learning for Creative Storytelling (RLCS) framework addresses this by developing a Generative Reward Model (GenRM) that provides multi-dimensional analysis and explicit reasoning about story preferences, trained via supervised fine-tuning and refined with GRPO-based methods [9]. Similarly, in visual dialogue tasks, where maintaining consistent and natural dialogue is key, combining RL with GANs has been used to generate more human-like responses, overcoming the paucity of training data and the tendency of Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) to produce terse answers [23]. In the domain of error diagnosis, the Hide and Seek Game (HSG) framework employs a dynamic adversarial approach where one role generates subtle reasoning errors and the other seeks to detect them, enhancing both error stealth and diagnostic precision through co-evolution [34].
