# summary

Aligning Protein Conformation Ensemble Generation with Physical Feedback is a computational framework designed to integrate physical constraints into deep generative models for the simulation of protein dynamics. While static protein structure prediction has advanced significantly, the dynamic nature of proteins—characterized by transitions between conformational ensembles—remains a critical but underexplored area due to the limited availability of dynamic datasets and the high computational cost of traditional Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations.[1][2] This field addresses the gap between high-throughput sequence data and physically consistent dynamic models, aiming to generate accurate atom-level trajectories that reflect the underlying physical feedback loops governing biological function.[3]
A central challenge in this domain is the inconsistency between data-driven generative models, such as diffusion models, and the physical energy landscapes they are intended to sample. Standard approaches often fail to satisfy the Fokker-Planck equation at small diffusion timesteps, leading to learned scores that do not accurately represent the equilibrium molecular distribution.[4] To resolve this, recent methodologies employ techniques such as Energy-based Alignment (EBA) and Energy Preference Optimization (EPO), which calibrate generative models using physical feedback from energy-based models (EBMs) and stochastic differential equation sampling.[5][6] These methods allow for the efficient adaptation of pretrained generators into energy-aware samplers without requiring extensive additional MD trajectories.[6]
The framework relies on the integration of diverse data sources, including large-scale dynamic datasets like the Dynamic PDB, which provides atomic velocities, forces, and energies for thousands of proteins.[2] By combining these data with high-resolution experimental restraints and physics-informed energy functions, researchers can validate conformational probabilities and identify packing imperfections in globular proteins.[7][8] This approach moves beyond static structural models to capture the mechanochemical feedback loops that are essential for understanding development, disease, and molecular recognition.[9]
Applications of this aligned generation framework span from elucidating allosteric mechanisms and intrinsically disordered protein dynamics to improving structure-based drug discovery.[10][11][12] By accounting for receptor flexibility and the ensemble of conformations, these methods offer a more realistic assessment of ligand binding than traditional lock-and-key models.[12][13] Future directions focus on resolving multiscale consistency between coarse-grained and all-atom representations and leveraging reinforcement learning to optimize conformational ensembles for specific functional outcomes over extended timescales.[14][15]

# Scientific Problem and Motivation

The dynamic behavior of proteins, characterized by their ability to transition between a wide range of structures comprising a conformational ensemble, is fundamental to biological function and drug discovery.[1] Despite significant progress in static protein structure collection and prediction, the dynamic nature of these molecules has been largely overlooked in prior research, an oversight attributed to the limited availability, diversity, and heterogeneity of dynamic protein datasets.[2] While Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations provide a rigorous physical basis for studying these dynamics and characterizing free energy landscapes, they remain computationally expensive for long timescales, particularly when high barriers between metastable states preclude efficient sampling without substantial resources.[1]
Recent advances in deep generative models have accelerated conformation generation, yet these methods often fail to model temporal relationships or are restricted to monomeric proteins, creating a gap in generating accurate atom-level trajectories for complex biomolecular systems.[16] Furthermore, while diffusion models trained on equilibrium molecular distributions have proven effective for sampling biomolecules, the energy-based interpretation of the learned score is often inconsistent with the training distribution.[4] This inconsistency is traced to inaccuracies in the learned score at very small diffusion timesteps, where the model fails to satisfy the Fokker-Planck equation that governs the evolution of the score, leading to deviations from the correct data distribution.[4]
Addressing these challenges requires integrating physical feedback mechanisms into conformational ensemble generation. There is increasing evidence that both mechanical and biochemical signals play critical roles in development and disease, with complex organism development relying on feedback between mechanical and biochemical patterning events.[9] In the context of protein modeling, this suggests that accurate structural refinement and dynamic simulation must account for mechanical deformations and physical properties, such as atomic velocities, forces, and energies, which are often missing in static databases.[2][17] For instance, refining predicted protein structures using all-atom MD simulations can rival the quality of experimental data, but routine refinement by unrestrained simulations is infeasible due to slow rearrangements within the compact folded state.[17] Therefore, there is a critical need for computational frameworks that can bridge the gap between high-throughput sequence data and physically consistent dynamic models, ensuring that generated conformational ensembles align with the underlying physical feedback loops governing protein behavior.[3]

# Theoretical Framework

The theoretical underpinnings of aligning protein conformation ensemble generation with physical feedback draw from several distinct but interconnected domains, including statistical mechanics, energy-based modeling, and the integration of data-driven generative models with physical constraints. A central challenge in this field is the computational infeasibility of routine refinement of predicted structures via unrestrained molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, primarily due to slow rearrangements within the compact folded state[17]. To address this, modern approaches often employ energy-based models (EBMs) that operate at the atomic scale, trained solely on crystallized protein data to capture physicochemical properties relevant to protein stability and function[8]. These models aim to rival the quality of structures determined by X-ray diffraction by learning distributions over crystallographic structures, thereby providing a statistical framework for evaluating conformational states[5].
A critical component of this framework is the reconciliation of data-driven generative models with physical supervision. Standard energy-based objectives often lead to intractable optimization problems when attempting to integrate physical feedback into data-driven approaches[5]. To overcome this, methods such as Energy-based Alignment (EBA) have been proposed to align generative models with feedback from physical models, efficiently calibrating them to balance conformational states based on energy differences[5]. Similarly, Energy Preference Optimization (EPO) leverages stochastic differential equation sampling to explore the conformational landscape, incorporating a novel energy-ranking mechanism based on list-wise preference optimization[6]. EPO introduces a practical upper bound to approximate the intractable probability of long sampling trajectories in continuous-time generative models, allowing for the efficient adaptation of pretrained generators into energy-aware samplers without the need for extra MD trajectories[6].
The consistency between learned generative models and physical distributions is further examined through the lens of diffusion models. While diffusion models trained on equilibrium molecular distributions are effective for sampling biomolecules, the energy-based interpretation of the learned score is often inconsistent with the training distribution[4]. This inconsistency is traced to inaccuracies in the learned score at very small diffusion timesteps, where the model fails to satisfy the Fokker-Planck equation that governs the evolution of the data distribution[4]. Addressing these theoretical gaps is essential for ensuring that the forces derived from the score of such models accurately reflect the physical interactions within the molecular system[4].
Furthermore, the framework incorporates insights from multiscale modeling, which seeks to combine the accuracy of fixed-charge all-atom (AA) representations with the sampling speed of coarse-grained (CG) representations[14]. The challenge lies in maintaining reciprocal consistency between these distinct scales, particularly in CG-to-AA conversions, which are prone to stochastic failure and may generate models with packing imperfections[14]. Theoretical analyses of these imperfections, such as deviations of torsion angles from equilibrium values, provide a basis for understanding how local structural details influence global conformational ensembles[7]. By integrating these multiscale perspectives with advanced sampling techniques, such as those inspired by mechanical deformation in metallurgy, researchers can develop more robust procedures for identifying collective variables and sampling along deformation modes in all-atom simulations[17]. This comprehensive theoretical framework thus bridges the gap between high-fidelity physical simulations and efficient, data-driven generative modeling, enabling the accurate exploration of protein conformational ensembles essential for understanding biological function[6][5].

# Data and Physical Feedback Sources

The generation of accurate protein conformational ensembles relies on the integration of diverse data sources and physical feedback mechanisms to overcome the limitations of static structural models. A primary challenge in this field is the historical oversight of protein dynamics, which has been attributed to the limited availability, diversity, and heterogeneity of dynamic protein datasets [2]. To address this gap, researchers have proposed enhancing existing static 3D protein structural databases, such as the Protein Data Bank (PDB), by integrating dynamic data and additional physical properties [2]. A notable example is the introduction of the Dynamic PDB, a large-scale dataset encompassing approximately 12,600 proteins, each subjected to all-atom molecular dynamics (MD) simulations lasting 1 microsecond to capture conformational changes [2]. This dataset provides a comprehensive suite of physical properties, including atomic velocities, forces, and potential and kinetic energies, which are essential for training models that understand the dynamic behavior of proteins [2].
In addition to large-scale dynamic datasets, high-resolution experimental data serves as a critical source of physical feedback for refining conformational models. The distribution of conformations for specific residues, such as methionine and lysine, in high-resolution structures has been shown to correlate well with the Boltzmann factor of torsion energy derived from high-level quantum-mechanics calculations of model compounds [7]. This correlation between experimental structural data and theoretical energy landscapes allows for the validation of conformational probabilities and the identification of packing imperfections in globular proteins [7]. Furthermore, experimental restraints, such as distance distributions obtained from pulsed dipolar spectroscopy, are utilized to distinguish uncertainty due to intrinsic disorder from uncertainty due to a lack of experimental restraints in intrinsically disordered peptide chains [18].
Physical feedback is also incorporated through the use of energy-based models and force fields that capture physicochemical properties relevant to protein stability. Energy-based models trained solely on crystallized protein data have demonstrated performance close to that of the Rosetta energy function, a state-of-the-art method in protein structure prediction and design [8]. These models capture physicochemical properties through their hidden representations, providing a data-driven alternative to traditional energy functions that rely on decades of tuned physical principles [8]. Additionally, emerging machine learning strategies involve creating atomistic force fields by preparing reference datasets of atomic environments and forces using density functional theory or higher-level methods [19]. These force fields enable high-fidelity molecular dynamics simulations by predicting atomic forces directly from configurations, thereby generating accurate potential energy surfaces that guide the sampling of conformational ensembles [19].
The integration of these data sources and physical feedback mechanisms is further supported by advanced sampling techniques that address the high energy barriers between metastable conformational states. Methods for enhancing conformational sampling often project the free energy landscape onto a reduced set of dimensions known as collective variables (CVs) [1]. For instance, refinement procedures inspired by metallurgy identify collective variables for mechanical deformations using coarse-grained models and then sample along these deformation modes in all-atom simulations [17]. This approach allows for the control of equilibration by mechanically deforming samples, facilitating the production of structural models that rival the quality of those determined by X-ray diffraction [17]. By combining large-scale dynamic datasets, high-resolution experimental restraints, and physics-informed energy models, the field is moving toward a more comprehensive understanding of protein conformational ensembles and their underlying physical feedback loops.

# Methodology

The generation of protein conformation ensembles that align with physical feedback involves a convergence of computational modeling, generative artificial intelligence, and thermodynamic principles. Recent methodologies have shifted from traditional, computationally expensive simulations toward data-driven approaches that integrate physical constraints to ensure the biological relevance of predicted structures.

## Integration of Physical Feedback

A critical challenge in using data-driven models is ensuring that the generated ensembles satisfy physical laws, as standard energy-based objectives can lead to intractable optimization or inconsistencies with the training distribution [5][4]. To address this, methods such as Energy-based Alignment (EBA) have been introduced to align generative models with feedback from physical models [5]. EBA efficiently calibrates generative models to balance conformational states based on energy differences, thereby integrating physical supervision into the learning process [5].
Furthermore, the consistency between the learned score of a diffusion model and the underlying energy landscape is a focal point of recent research. Inconsistencies often arise at very small diffusion timesteps, where the model fails to satisfy the Fokker-Planck equation governing the evolution of the score [4]. To mitigate this, energy-based diffusion models have been proposed to ensure that the derived forces acting on molecular systems are consistent with the equilibrium distribution [4].

## Generative Models and Diffusion Frameworks

A significant advancement in ensemble generation is the application of generative modeling, particularly denoising diffusion models, which learn distributions over crystallographic structures to enable efficient conformation sampling [5]. These models have proven effective for sampling biomolecules from equilibrium molecular distributions [4]. Theoretically, masked diffusion models (MDMs) can be interpreted as solutions to energy minimization problems in discrete optimal transport, where kinetic, conditional kinetic, and geodesic energy formulations are mathematically equivalent under specific mask schedules [20]. This theoretical unification allows for the parameterization of interpolation schedules, such as via Beta distributions, to optimize sampling efficiency without modifying the underlying model architecture [20].

## Energy-Based Scoring and Structural Prediction

Complementing generative approaches, energy-based models (EBMs) operate at the atomic scale to score protein conformations. These models can be trained solely on crystallized protein data, bypassing the need for complex, hand-tuned energy functions that incorporate decades of physical knowledge [8]. Benchmarks on rotamer recovery tasks indicate that such data-driven EBMs can achieve performance close to state-of-the-art methods like the Rosetta energy function, capturing relevant physicochemical properties in their hidden representations [8].
In the context of structure prediction, methodologies often rely on template-based or template-free modeling techniques [21]. The quality of the resulting structural models is heavily dependent on the accuracy of the query-template alignment when a template is available [21]. For flexible backbone modeling, methods such as Backrub, derived from observations of alternative conformations in high-resolution crystal structures, are used to generate near-native conformational ensembles from a single input structure [22]. These ensembles can then be used for applications such as designing sequences consistent with specific conformations or predicting tolerated sequences at protein-protein interfaces [22].

## Theoretical and Computational Frameworks

Beyond deep learning, rigorous theoretical frameworks continue to inform the computation of thermodynamic quantities in many-body systems. Variational methods for canonical ensembles with differentiable temperature have been proposed to provide thermodynamic quantities as continuous functions of temperature, offering an unbiased Boltzmann distribution at optimality [23]. This approach contrasts with conventional Markov chain Monte Carlo methods, which require many steps to equilibrate, and with earlier deep learning methods that were limited to single trained temperature points [23].
Additionally, combinatorial algorithms are being applied to discrete representations of conformational space. For instance, protein 3D-conformations can be encoded into dominance partition sequences (DPS), which partition molecular conformational space into regions with identical sequences [24]. By building sets of 3D-conformations compatible with a given DPS on a cubic lattice, researchers can structure these sets as graphs to compute the mean energy of conformations within specific cells [24].

## Broader Context: Mechanochemical Feedback

The methodologies for generating and analyzing protein ensembles are increasingly viewed within the broader context of mechanochemical feedback loops. There is growing evidence that both mechanical and biochemical signals play crucial roles in development and disease, with feedback occurring at the molecular level via mechanosensation and as an emergent property at the cellular and tissue level [9]. Understanding how dynamic changes in tissue geometry and cell fate specification interact with molecular conformations is essential for developing comprehensive models of biological systems [9].

# Biological Applications

The generation and analysis of protein conformational ensembles have become integral to understanding various biological processes, ranging from fundamental developmental mechanisms to the molecular basis of disease. By moving beyond static structural models, researchers can capture the dynamic nature of biomolecules, which is essential for elucidating how proteins function in complex physiological environments.

## Development and Disease Mechanisms

Recent evidence highlights the critical role of mechanical and biochemical signals in both development and disease, suggesting that the development of complex organisms relies on feedback loops between mechanical and biochemical patterning events.[9] These feedback mechanisms operate at the molecular level through mechanosensation and can also emerge as system-level properties at the cellular and tissue scale.[9] Conformational ensembles provide a framework for understanding how dynamic changes in tissue geometry, flow, and rheology interact with cell fate specification, serving as key platforms for these mechanochemical feedback loops.[9] Furthermore, allostery, an inherent property of biomacromolecules, is increasingly recognized as a dysfunctional factor in disease and a target for drug discovery.[10] The ability to model protein ensembles allows for the investigation of how evolution has exploited allosteric properties through sequence selection and post-translational modifications to regulate biological function.[10]

## Molecular Recognition and Allostery

Conformational ensembles are vital for analyzing molecular recognition and distinguishing between competing binding models, such as induced fit and conformational selection.[25] In the context of allostery, ensembles help unravel the intricate communication pathways that enable long-range conformational changes.[25] For instance, studies on the fungal circadian clock photoreceptor Vivid (VVD) have utilized computational platforms integrating Markov state models and machine learning to quantify the contribution of covalent bond formation to global conformational changes.[26] This approach revealed an atomistic allosteric mechanism, highlighting the unexpected importance of specific loops in the light-driven conformational shift, thereby advancing the development of VVD as an optogenetic tool.[26] Additionally, the concept of allostery extends beyond proteins to DNA and RNA, underscoring the broad applicability of ensemble-based analysis in biological systems.[10]

## Intrinsically Disordered Proteins

Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) represent a significant fraction of the human proteome and challenge the classical structure-function paradigm, as they lack stable tertiary structures in their functional forms.[11] To address this, IDPs are often described as an equilibrium of rapidly inter-converting conformers.[11] Efficient algorithms, such as flexible-meccano, have been developed to generate these ensembles based on amino acid-specific conformational potentials and volume exclusion, providing a more accurate description of IDP dynamics than static structures.[11] This ensemble-based approach is crucial for predicting experimental data and understanding the functional diversity of disordered regions in proteins.[11]

## Structure-Based Drug Discovery

The dynamic nature of proteins has profound implications for drug design, shifting the theoretical framework from the rigid lock-and-key model to the induced-fit model and beyond.[12] Incorporating receptor flexibility is considered crucial for improving docking-based virtual screening.[13] Since proteins possess an ensemble of conformations, docking ligands into a single static structure may fail to capture the true binding landscape.[12] To address this, ensemble docking algorithms have been developed to simultaneously dock ligands into multiple protein structures, such as NMR conformers or crystallographic ensembles, without the significant computational cost of sequential docking.[27][13] For example, the application of ensemble docking to HIV-1 protease and Chk1 inhibitors has demonstrated improved accuracy in predicting binding modes and identifying novel potent compounds compared to single-structure approaches.[27][13] This method allows for a more realistic assessment of ligand binding by accounting for the structural variations inherent in protein ensembles.[27]

# Evaluation and Validation

The evaluation of protein conformation ensemble generation methods typically involves benchmarking against established energy functions and experimental data to assess the physical accuracy and predictive power of the generated models. A primary metric for evaluating energy-based models (EBMs) is the rotamer recovery task, which involves predicting the conformation of a side chain from its context within a protein structure. This task has been widely used to evaluate energy functions for protein design, and recent EBM approaches trained solely on crystallized protein data have achieved performance close to that of the Rosetta energy function, a state-of-the-art method in the field [8].
To validate the physical realism of generated ensembles, researchers often compare computational predictions with experimental techniques such as neutron scattering and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) relaxation experiments. For instance, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have been shown to agree well with neutron scattering data, which provides information on global flexibility, and NMR data, which offers residue-specific order parameters [28]. These combined experimental and simulation results allow for a detailed description of dynamical changes, such as modifications in the coupling between dominant regions and the closing of substrate access channels, thereby validating the model's ability to capture specific physicochemical properties [28].
Furthermore, the accuracy of conformational predictions is often assessed by their ability to recapitulate known structural states, particularly in challenging cases such as "metamorphic" proteins. Energetic frameworks based on ensemble descriptions of protein thermodynamics have been validated by their success in recapitulating the structures of engineered sequences that adopt specific native folds, such as *Streptococcus* protein G, demonstrating the framework's capability to quantify free energy differences between energy landscape minima and denatured states [29].
In the context of structure refinement, which is closely related to ensemble generation, the quality of computational models is evaluated by their ability to bring approximate initial templates closer to the true native structure. Current methods, including those relying on molecular dynamics simulations or iterative structure optimization, are assessed on their capacity to achieve near-experimental accuracy, although consistent refinement remains a challenge due to issues with energy function accuracy and the reliable ranking of multiple models [30]. Additionally, the potential of residue-residue distance predictions to predict protein flexibility rather than just static structure is evaluated by classifying residue pairs as rigid, flexible, or neither, based on reference structures, providing a further layer of validation for dynamic models [31].

# Limitations and Challenges

The generation of protein conformational ensembles faces significant hurdles, primarily stemming from the inherent uncertainty in experimental data and the computational complexity of sampling diverse structural states. For intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs), which lack a stable tertiary structure, the conformational ensembles are not fully determined by experimental observations alone.[18] This uncertainty arises from both the lack of sufficient experimental restraints and the intrinsic disorder of the chains, making it difficult to distinguish between these sources without specific data types such as distance distribution restraints obtained from pulsed dipolar electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy.[18] Furthermore, the selection of conformational ensembles consistent with solution experiments is highly dependent on the initial pool of conformers, and currently available tools are often limited by their ability to perform adequate conformational sampling.[32]
Traditional computational methods for calculating thermodynamic quantities of many-body systems at thermal equilibrium, such as Markov chain Monte Carlo, require many steps to reach equilibrium, posing a challenge for efficient computation.[23] While recent deep learning methods offer direct sampling capabilities, they often operate only at a single trained temperature point and risk biased sampling, limiting their generalizability across different thermodynamic conditions.[23] Additionally, the structure optimization procedure in protein modeling is still largely dominated by traditional tools that minimize pre-defined statistical energy functions, which may not be optimal for formulating the entire conformation space of proteins.[33]
The reliance on specific design criteria and energy functions also presents limitations. In the design of nucleic acid sequences, commonly used approaches such as sequence symmetry minimization and minimum free-energy satisfaction primarily implement negative design paradigms and may lack the positive design components necessary to optimize affinity for target structures.[34] Similarly, in the context of loop prediction, obtaining meaningful statistics requires large cutoffs for matching interatomic distances of stem residues to ensure the best possible loop candidates are included, resulting in large sets of potential templates that can complicate the selection process.[35] These challenges highlight the need for more robust frameworks that can integrate physical feedback, such as potential energy components, into generative models to synthesize realistic molecular trajectories and improve the accuracy of conformational predictions.[36]

# Future Directions

The integration of physical feedback into protein conformation ensemble generation is poised to advance through several key methodological and theoretical developments. A primary area of focus is the resolution of inconsistencies between learned diffusion scores and physical energy landscapes. Recent analyses indicate that standard diffusion models often fail to satisfy the Fokker-Planck equation at very small diffusion timesteps, leading to energy-based interpretations that are inconsistent with the training distribution [4]. Future work aims to address these inaccuracies by developing energy-based diffusion modes that ensure the correct evolution of the data distribution, thereby aligning the generative process more strictly with physical laws [4].
Furthermore, the challenge of multiscale consistency remains a critical frontier. While all-atom (AA) representations offer high accuracy, they are computationally expensive, whereas coarse-grained (CG) models provide necessary sampling speed but lack atomic detail [14]. Current automated tools for converting CG models back to AA representations, such as the "Backward" method, are prone to stochastic failure and often produce models with significant errors [14]. Future directions involve developing more robust, reciprocal consistency frameworks that can seamlessly bridge these scales, ensuring that the combinatorial synergy of multiscale modeling is realized without the loss of structural integrity during scale transitions [14].
In the realm of sampling efficiency and conformational search, there is a growing emphasis on leveraging optimization theory to guide stochastic processes. The extension of replica exchange Monte Carlo (REMC) methods using multi-objective optimization Pareto front information has shown promise in facilitating the selection of replicas for exchange, thereby accelerating convergence to lower energy conformations [37]. Future research may further integrate such Pareto-optimal strategies with generative models to directly provide min-min and hybrid scenario solutions, enhancing the ability to explore complex conformational spaces efficiently [37].
Additionally, the mitigation of geometric artifacts in generative models is an active area of investigation. Techniques such as Structural Energy-Guided Sampling (SEGS) have demonstrated that enforcing multi-view consistency at sampling time can significantly reduce artifacts like the "Janus problem," where objects appear correct from one angle but distorted from others [38]. Applying similar energy-guided sampling strategies to protein structures could help ensure that generated ensembles maintain physical plausibility and geometric consistency across all viewpoints, without the need for retraining or weight modification [38].
Finally, the field is moving towards more sophisticated reinforcement learning frameworks that incorporate long-term physical feedback. While model-based reinforcement learning (MBRL) with autoregressive world models suffers from compounding errors, diffusion world models offer a path forward by generating trajectory segments jointly [15]. The introduction of advantage-guided diffusion, such as Sigmoid Advantage Guidance (SAG) and Exponential Advantage Guidance (EAG), allows for reweighted sampling of trajectories based on long-term return estimates [15]. Future applications of these guides in protein dynamics could enable the generation of conformational ensembles that are not only structurally valid but also optimized for specific functional or energetic outcomes over extended timescales [15].
