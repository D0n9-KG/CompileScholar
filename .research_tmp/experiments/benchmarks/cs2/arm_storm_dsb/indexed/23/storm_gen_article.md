# summary

Proximal algorithm unrolling is a technique that bridges traditional iterative optimization methods and deep learning by unfolding iterative algorithms into deep neural network architectures. This approach enables the learning of algorithm parameters, such as thresholds and step-sizes, from training data, resulting in significant improvements in convergence speed and reconstruction accuracy compared to standard iterative solvers.[1] By integrating the structural interpretability of iterative algorithms with the representational power of neural networks, these methods can adaptively compute parameters to handle complex inverse problems more effectively than static approaches.[1]
A primary application of this methodology is in variational image reconstruction, particularly for total variation (TV) minimization, where networks estimate data-adapted, spatially and temporally dependent regularization parameter-maps.[2] These architectures typically employ distinct subnetworks: one to estimate the regularization parameters from input data and another to unroll the iterations of the underlying iterative solver.[2] The overall network is trained end-to-end in a supervised learning fashion using pairs of clean and corrupted data, often without requiring access to ground truth labels during the training process.[2]

In deblurring, compact neural networks are developed by unrolling traditional iterative algorithms to handle non-uniform blurs, allowing for the independent processing of image regions.[3] In compressed sensing, improved algorithms such as the fast iterative parametric improved threshold algorithm (FIPITA) utilize modified threshold functions and restart mechanisms to address the accuracy and efficiency limitations of standard fast iterative soft threshold algorithms (FISTA).[4]

# Theoretical Foundations

The theoretical underpinnings of proximal algorithm unrolling and flexible reconstruction networks are rooted in the stability and convergence properties of iterative procedures defined by continuous functions on metric spaces. Research into the stability of these iteration processes has extended the classical contraction principle to utilize altering-distance functions and extended altering-distance functions, the latter being piecewise continuous. These extensions allow for the relaxation of conditions required for maps to be contractive, permitting the achievement of stability through large contractions or subjecting the maps to specific altering-distance functions[6]. Furthermore, fixed point theorems involving $T$-contractions and $w$-distances in partially ordered metric spaces provide a robust framework for analyzing convergence, with generalized altering distance functions serving as a primary tool in their proofs[5].
In the context of sparse recovery (SR), where traditional iterative algorithms often exhibit limited convergence accuracy and rate, deep unfolding methods have introduced significant theoretical improvements. By unfolding iterative SR algorithms into deep neural networks and learning parameters from training data, such as in the learning proximal operator method (LePOM) and analytical LePOM (ALePOM), the convergence behavior is dramatically enhanced. However, theoretical analysis indicates that the recovery error bound of ALePOM may require further reduction as network parameters are learned to accommodate all training data. To address this, improved architectures like I-ALePOM introduce long short-term memory (LSTM) cells into each layer, facilitating the adaptive computation of thresholds and step-sizes to optimize the recovery process[1].
The application of these methods to nonconvex minimization problems, such as those arising in reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) with $\ell_0$-quasi norm, relies on rigorous convergence guarantees. Algorithms designed to solve such nonconvex problems, often employing mathematical programs with equilibrium constraints (MPEC) and proximal-based strategies, are theoretically proven to generate sequences that converge to the local optimal solutions of the problem[7]. This theoretical assurance is critical for ensuring the reliability of reconstruction networks in handling degraded data.
Additionally, the integration of variational image reconstruction techniques, particularly total variation (TV) minimization, is supported by methods for the fast estimation of data-adapted, spatially and temporally dependent regularization parameter-maps. These approaches, inspired by algorithm unrolling, utilize distinct subnetworks to estimate regularization parameters and unroll iterations of the underlying iterative algorithm. The overall network is trained end-to-end in a supervised manner, often without the need for access to ground truth labels, thereby enhancing the flexibility and efficiency of the reconstruction process[2]. Similar principles apply to image deblurring, where unrolling traditional iterative deblurring algorithms into compact neural networks allows for the independent deblurring of image regions, with performance evaluated against state-of-the-art networks using standard datasets[3][8].

# Methodology

The methodology of proximal algorithm unrolling involves transforming traditional iterative optimization algorithms into deep neural network architectures, allowing for the end-to-end training of parameters to enhance reconstruction performance. This approach bridges the gap between model-based physical interpretability and the speed and accuracy of data-driven deep learning methods.

## Network Architecture Design

A core component of the unrolling methodology is the design of subnetworks that correspond to the steps of the underlying iterative algorithm. In variational image reconstruction, such as total variation (TV) minimization, the network may rely on distinct subnetworks: one to estimate data-adapted, spatially and temporally dependent regularization parameter-maps from input data, and another to unroll iterations of the iterative solver [2]. Similarly, in sparse recovery problems, methods like the improved Analytical Learning Proximal Operator Method (I-ALePOM) introduce long short-term memory (LSTM) cells into each layer of the unrolled network. This modification allows the network to adaptively compute thresholds and step-sizes, addressing the issue of recovery error bounds that occur when standard parameters are learned to cater to all training data [1].
For specific imaging tasks, such as deblurring images affected by non-uniform blur, compact neural networks are developed by unrolling traditional iterative deblurring algorithms and adapting them to independently process regions of an image [8][3]. In the context of computational microscopy, where systems employ non-localized point spread functions (PSFs), unrolled architectures like the partially connected unrolled Richardson-Lucy (PC-RLN) network are proposed. These physics-informed networks integrate model-based insights into data-driven architectures to reconstruct high-quality images from complex optical measurements, offering a balance between the computational intensity of traditional iterative methods and the lack of physical interpretability in standard deep neural networks [9].

## Parameter Optimization and Convergence

The unrolling process facilitates the optimization of algorithm parameters, such as thresholds and step-sizes, which are traditionally fixed or manually tuned in classical iterative methods. By treating these parameters as learnable weights, the network can be trained in a supervised learning fashion using pairs of clean and corrupted data, often without the need for access to ground-truth labels for the intermediate steps [2]. In compressed sensing applications, improved algorithms like the Fast Iterative Parametric Improved Threshold Algorithm (FIPITA) utilize mended threshold functions and restart adjustment mechanisms, with parameters carefully selected to reduce reconstruction error and speed up convergence [4].
Recent frameworks, such as SSM-Net, combine state-space modeling (SSM) from the Mamba architecture with the Fast Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm (FISTA). This integration allows the network to capture local and global dependencies with linear computational complexity, significantly reducing computation time compared to Transformer-based methods while using momentum updates inspired by FISTA to improve convergence speed during deep iterative reconstruction [10]. Theoretical analyses of these unrolled procedures often extend contraction principles to ensure stability, with conditions for contractivity relaxed to allow for large contractions or the use of altering-distance functions [6][5]. Furthermore, in nonconvex minimization problems within reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, unrolled or proximal-based strategies are designed to ensure that generated sequences converge to local optimal solutions, providing a theoretical guarantee for the effectiveness of the learned reconstruction process [7].

# Applications

The unrolling of iterative algorithms into neural network architectures has found diverse applications across various fields of image processing and reconstruction, offering a balance between computational efficiency and physical interpretability. In the domain of image deblurring, compact neural networks have been developed by unrolling traditional iterative deblurring algorithms to handle non-uniform blur effects. These networks are designed to independently deblur regions of an image and have been evaluated against state-of-the-art methods such as SRN-Deblur and DUBLID using industry-standard datasets like GOPRO and Kohler[8][3].
In computational microscopy, unrolled neural network architectures, such as the partially connected unrolled Richardson-Lucy (PC-RLN) network, are employed to reconstruct high-quality images from complex optical measurements involving non-localized point spread functions (PSFs). These physics-informed networks bridge the gap between computationally intensive traditional iterative optimization methods and deep neural networks that often lack physical interpretability[9].
For variational image reconstruction, particularly in total variation (TV) minimization, algorithm unrolling is used to estimate data-adapted, spatially and temporally dependent regularization parameter-maps. This approach utilizes distinct subnetworks to estimate parameters and unroll iterative algorithm steps, enabling end-to-end training without the need for labeled data[2]. Additionally, in the field of compressed sensing, frameworks like SSM-Net combine state-space modeling with fast iterative shrinking threshold algorithms (FISTA) to achieve high reconstruction quality with linear computational complexity, significantly reducing computation time compared to Transformer-based methods[10].
Applications also extend to medical imaging, where criteria for stopping iterative algorithms such as Maximum Likelihood Expectation Maximization (MLEM) and Ordered Subset Expectation Maximization (OSEM) are developed for positron emission tomography (PET) reconstruction. These studies analyze the properties of these algorithms to improve reconstruction efficiency in medical imaging systems[11]. Furthermore, iterative threshold compressed sensing reconstruction (ITCSR) algorithms have been proposed for reconstructing sparse graph signals in the graph Fourier domain, utilizing threshold functions to enhance performance and ensure convergence[12].

In the context of image deblurring, unrolled neural networks are evaluated by comparing their capabilities against state-of-the-art models such as SRN-Deblur and DUBLID.[3][8] These evaluations often involve investigating the impact of varying patch sizes and point spread function (PSF) sizes on deblurring performance, using industry-standard datasets like GOPRO and Kohler.[3]

A key advantage of modern unrolled networks is their computational efficiency. The SSM-Net architecture, for example, employs a Mamba-based state-space module that captures local and global dependencies with linear computational complexity, significantly reducing computation time compared to Transformer-based methods.[10] In computational microscopy, unrolled architectures like the partially connected unrolled Richardson-Lucy (PC-RLN) network offer faster inference than traditional iterative optimization methods, which, while physically interpretable, are computationally intensive.[9]

# Advantages and Limitations

The unrolling of iterative algorithms into deep neural networks offers significant advantages in terms of computational efficiency and reconstruction accuracy, particularly in complex inverse problems such as image deblurring, sparse recovery, and compressed sensing. By transforming traditional iterative procedures into learnable network architectures, these methods can adapt parameters to specific data distributions, often outperforming their non-learned counterparts. For instance, in image deblurring, unrolling traditional iterative algorithms allows for the development of compact neural networks that can independently deblur regions of an image, effectively handling non-uniform blurs and reducing reconstruction errors compared to standard soft thresholding functions[3][8]. Similarly, in sparse recovery, deep unfolding methods like the improved Analytical Learning Proximal Operator Method (I-ALePOM) utilize components such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) cells to adaptively compute thresholds and step-sizes, leading to dramatic improvements in convergence rates and reduced recovery error bounds compared to static iterative algorithms[1]. Furthermore, in variational image reconstruction, such as Total Variation (TV) minimization, unrolled networks can estimate data-adapted, spatially and temporally dependent regularization parameter-maps, enabling fast and accurate reconstruction without the need for manual parameter tuning[2].
Despite these benefits, the application of unrolled networks and related iterative procedures faces certain limitations and challenges. One primary concern is the computational intensity of traditional iterative optimization methods, which, while physically interpretable, can be slow; deep neural networks offer faster inference but may lack this physical interpretability, a gap that physics-informed architectures like the partially connected unrolled Richardson-Lucy (PC-RLN) network attempt to bridge[9]. Additionally, the stability and convergence of these iterative processes depend heavily on the specific conditions of the underlying functions. In mathematical contexts, the stability of iteration procedures defined by continuous functions on metric spaces requires that the maps satisfy specific contractive conditions, such as being large contractions or subject to altering-distance functions, to ensure convergence[6]. In more complex settings, such as partially ordered metric spaces, the application of fixed point theorems with T-contractions and w-distances is necessary to guarantee the existence of solutions, highlighting the theoretical constraints that must be met for these algorithms to be effective[5]. Moreover, in nonconvex minimization problems, such as those involving ℓ0-quasi norms in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces, the convergence of the generated sequences is typically guaranteed only to local optimal solutions rather than global optima, necessitating careful algorithm design using strategies like mathematical programs with equilibrium constraints (MPEC) and proximal-based methods to ensure effectiveness[7].
