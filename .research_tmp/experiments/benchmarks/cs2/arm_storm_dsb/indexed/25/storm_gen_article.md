# summary

Universal Domain Adaptation (UDA) for semantic segmentation is a subfield of computer vision that addresses the challenge of transferring knowledge from a labeled source domain to an unlabeled target domain without constraints on the label sets.[1] Unlike traditional unsupervised domain adaptation methods, which often assume identical or subset label spaces, UDA acknowledges that the target domain may contain private classes not present in the source, a scenario critical for real-world applications where data distributions and categories vary significantly.[1][2] Semantic segmentation, defined as assigning a class label to every pixel in an image, is a fundamental component of autonomous vehicle vision stacks and remote sensing, yet its deployment is frequently limited by the high cost of pixel-level annotation and the computational constraints of embedded systems requiring real-time inference.[3][4]
The primary motivation for UDA is the scarcity of labeled data that fits specific deployment requirements, a limitation that has driven the development of methods to bridge the gap between labeled source domains and unlabeled target domains.[5][6] While synthetic datasets generated in controlled virtual environments can provide automatic pixel-level annotations, learning well-generalized representations remains challenging due to probabilistic and geometric discrepancies between virtual and real-world imagery.[6] Consequently, UDA aims to learn models on a supervised source domain that perform effectively on an unsupervised target, even when the target domain contains unknown categories, thereby relieving the need for laborious manual annotation.[1][7]
Recent advancements in UDA for semantic segmentation have focused on strategies to handle distribution shifts and label discrepancies, with self-training and pseudo-labeling serving as central methodologies.[8] However, these approaches face challenges such as the conflict between "learning ability" and "adapting ability," as well as the generation of sparse pseudo-labels that can lead to suboptimal models.[8][9] To mitigate these issues, novel frameworks have been proposed, including Two-phase Pseudo Label Densification (TPLD) to propagate confident predictions, and Optimal Transport (OT)-based methods like UniOT to detect common classes without predefined thresholds.[1][9] Additionally, active sample selection strategies have been introduced to address the distortion of target-domain structural information caused by unconditional mapping.[10]
Beyond static datasets, UDA frameworks are increasingly being adapted for non-stationary distribution shifts in large-scale or streaming data scenarios, where data statistics become a function of time.[11] In these dynamic environments, models are adapted sequentially over incoming unsupervised batches without requiring additional annotated data, addressing the limitations of hand-tuned values in diverse class ratios.[1][11] These developments collectively aim to provide comprehensive methodologies for deploying robust semantic segmentation models in real-world applications where data availability and computational resources are constrained, such as in autonomous driving and remote sensing.[3][5]

# Overview

Universal Domain Adaptation (UDA) for semantic segmentation addresses the challenge of transferring knowledge from a source domain to a target domain without constraints on label sets, a significant advancement over traditional unsupervised domain adaptation methods that often assume identical or subset label spaces.[1] Semantic segmentation, defined as the problem of assigning a class label to every pixel in an image, is a critical component of autonomous vehicle vision stacks for facilitating scene understanding and object detection.[3] However, the deployment of high-performing semantic segmentation models is often limited by the requirement for extensive pixel-level annotation and the computational constraints of embedded systems, which necessitate low-latency, real-time inference.[3]
The primary motivation for UDA in this context is the scarcity of labeled data that fits specific deployment requirements, a limitation that has driven rapid growth in ad-hoc approaches to bridge the gap between labeled source domains and unlabeled or weakly labeled target domains.[5][6] While synthetic datasets generated in controlled virtual environments, such as video games or traffic simulators, can automatically provide pixel-level annotations, learning well-generalized representations remains challenging due to probabilistic and geometric discrepancies between virtual and real-world imagery.[6] Consequently, UDA aims to learn models on a supervised source domain that perform effectively on an unsupervised target, even when the target domain contains private classes not present in the source.[1]
Recent advancements in UDA for semantic segmentation have focused on various strategies to handle distribution shifts and label discrepancies. A popular framework involves self-training, which endows models with the ability to learn reliable semantics from labeled source images and adapt to the target domain by generating pseudo-labels on unlabeled images.[8] However, this approach faces a conflict between "learning ability" and "adapting ability," where adjusting the proportion of target-domain training samples strengthens one while weakening the other.[8] To mitigate issues such as sparse pseudo-labels that lead to suboptimal models, novel frameworks like Two-phase Pseudo Label Densification (TPLD) have been proposed to propagate confident predictions and utilize spatial correlations.[9] Furthermore, to address the distortion of target-domain structural information caused by unconditional mapping, active sample selection strategies using multiple anchors have been introduced to better characterize multimodal distributions and select informative samples.[10]
Beyond static datasets, UDA frameworks are increasingly being adapted for non-stationary distribution shifts in large-scale or streaming data scenarios, where data statistics become a function of time.[11] In these dynamic environments, models are adapted sequentially over incoming unsupervised batches without requiring additional annotated data.[11] Additionally, methods such as UniOT employ Optimal Transport (OT) to handle universal domain adaptation by detecting common classes without predefined thresholds and recognizing different categories among target-private samples, thereby addressing the limitations of hand-tuned values in diverse class ratios.[1] These developments collectively aim to provide comprehensive methodologies for deploying robust semantic segmentation models in real-world applications where data availability and computational resources are constrained.[5]

# Background

Semantic segmentation, the task of assigning a class label to every pixel in an image, is a fundamental component of computer vision, particularly for applications such as autonomous driving and scene understanding.[3][4] While fully convolutional models have proven successful for dense prediction tasks in supervised settings, their performance often degrades significantly under domain shifts that may appear mild to human observers.[12] For instance, training a model on data from one geographic region or weather condition and testing it on another can result in substantial performance drops due to pixel-level distribution shifts.[12] This challenge is exacerbated in fields like remote sensing, where the diverse nature of satellite and aerial imagery, combined with a lack of labeled data covering this diversity, makes it difficult for machine learning algorithms to generalize across multiple domains.[13]
The reliance on human-based image labeling for training deep semantic segmentation models presents a significant bottleneck, leading to the exploration of unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) as a promising alternative.[14] UDA aims to learn models on a supervised source domain that perform well on an unsupervised target domain, thereby relieving the need for laborious annotation.[7] Early approaches to UDA in semantic segmentation often employed adversarial learning techniques to align global domain distributions.[12] However, aligning marginal feature distributions does not guarantee the alignment of class-conditional distributions, a limitation that is particularly evident in tasks with larger domain discrepancies, such as object detection and segmentation.[15] Furthermore, traditional UDA methods often assume that all source categories are present in the target domain, which is not always the case in practical scenarios involving partial or open-set categories.[7]
To address these limitations, recent research has focused on more nuanced adaptation strategies. Some methods distinguish between "stuff" categories, which share similar appearances across domains, and "thing" categories, which exhibit larger differences, applying different alignment strategies for each.[16] Other approaches have introduced active sample selection to prevent the distortion of essential structural information in the target domain when mapping distributions unconditionally.[10] Additionally, the field has expanded to handle non-stationary distribution shifts in streaming data scenarios, where models are adapted sequentially over incoming unsupervised batches without additional annotated data.[11] More advanced frameworks now aim to handle arbitrary category shifts and incorporate meta-learning schemes with feature disentanglement to derive domain-invariant features with generalization guarantees.[17][7] These developments highlight the ongoing effort to create robust, efficient, and universally applicable domain adaptation methods for semantic segmentation.[3]

# Problem Formulation

Universal Domain Adaptation (UniDA) for semantic segmentation addresses the challenge of transferring knowledge from a source domain to a target domain without constraints on the label sets, a scenario often referred to as the universal or open-set setting.[1][18] Unlike closed-set domain adaptation, which assumes that the classes depicted in the target images are exactly the same as those in the source domain, UniDA acknowledges that the target domain may contain private classes that are not present in the source.[1][2] This distinction is critical because most existing methods rely on manually specified or hand-tuned threshold values to detect common samples, making them difficult to extend to realistic scenarios with diverse ratios of common classes.[1]
A central issue in UniDA is the identification of target common samples for domain alignment, as both domains may hold private classes.[1] In the unsupervised scenario, it is generally not possible to identify each specific new class in the target data; instead, the goal is to automatically detect which samples belong to these new classes and discard them from the recognition process.[2] This is often approached by relying on the intuition that source and target samples depicting known classes can be generated by a shared subspace, whereas target samples from unknown classes originate from a different, private subspace.[2] To facilitate this, scoring schemes have been developed to effectively identify samples of shared classes, which are then used to select which target samples to pseudo-label during training.[18]
Furthermore, the problem formulation must account for significant distribution shifts between domains. In visual recognition tasks, such as semantic segmentation, models trained on one data domain may not generalize well to other domains without annotations for model finetuning due to pixel-level distribution shifts.[19][12] These shifts can be particularly pronounced in dynamic or streaming data scenarios, where dataset statistics become a function of time, necessitating sequential adaptation over incoming unsupervised data batches.[11] Additionally, in complex time series applications, the dynamic temporal structure variations across domains lead to feature shifts in both time and frequency representations, while label distributions may differ significantly, posing difficulties in addressing label shifts and recognizing labels unique to the target domain.[20] Consequently, effective UniDA frameworks must align feature representations across domains while correcting for misalignments and handling the presence of target-private samples without predefined thresholds.[1]

# Methodology

The methodology for Universal Domain Adaptation (UDA) in semantic segmentation addresses the challenge of transferring knowledge from a source domain to a target domain where the label sets are not identical, often involving private classes in either domain. Recent advancements have focused on overcoming the limitations of traditional closed-set assumptions by developing strategies that handle open-set scenarios, where new, unknown classes may be present in the target data.[2] A primary objective in these methodologies is the identification of shared classes between domains to facilitate effective domain alignment, as most existing approaches struggle with diverse ratios of common classes and the inability to distinguish between different categories of target-private samples.[1]
To address the issue of identifying common samples without relying on manually specified or hand-tuned threshold values, researchers have proposed using Optimal Transport (OT) within a unified framework.[1] For instance, the UniOT method employs an OT-based partial alignment with adaptive filling to automatically detect common classes, thereby extending applicability to more realistic UDA scenarios.[1] In the universal scenario where only some classes are shared, scoring schemes have been developed to effectively identify samples belonging to these shared classes.[18] These scores are utilized to select which target domain samples should be pseudo-labeled during training, while additional loss terms encourage label diversity within each batch to improve performance over state-of-the-art benchmarks.[18]
Self-training and pseudo-labeling remain central to many UDA methodologies, although they face challenges such as the generation of sparse pseudo-labels due to the reliance on confident predictions.[9] To mitigate the resulting insufficient training signals, frameworks like Two-phase Pseudo Label Densification (TPLD) have been introduced.[9] TPLD utilizes sliding window voting in the first phase to propagate confident predictions by leveraging intrinsic spatial correlations in images, followed by a confidence-based easy-hard classification in the second phase to densify the labels.[9] Furthermore, co-training procedures have been proposed for synth-to-real UDA, where unlabeled real-world images are labeled by intermediate deep models trained on both labeled synthetic images and previously labeled real-world data, allowing for mutual improvement between domain-adapted models.[14]
Adaptation strategies are applied at various levels, including the input (image) level, internal feature representation, and output prediction.[5] At the feature level, contrastive learning has been adapted to enhance feature discriminability; however, standard paradigms often ignore class weights, making it difficult for features to cluster around corresponding class weights.[21] To solve this, Probabilistic Contrastive Learning (PCL) removes $\ell_2$ normalization and replaces features with probabilities, guiding the probability distribution towards a one-hot configuration to minimize the discrepancy between features and class weights.[21] Additionally, adversarial approaches have been employed to perform global and category-specific adaptation, using fully convolutional domain adversarial learning to align global domains before enabling category-specific adjustments.[12]
In scenarios where real-world data is scarce, such as in one-shot unsupervised sim-to-real domain adaptation (OSUDA), methodologies focus on relieving the need for large amounts of real data.[22] This is achieved by constructing a pseudo-target domain through stylizing simulated data with the limited available real-world samples to remedy the lack of real data knowledge.[22] Other approaches, such as forward-backward cyclic (FBC) training, aim to address the limitation that aligning marginal feature distributions does not guarantee the alignment of class conditional distributions, a problem that is particularly evident in object detection but relevant to broader domain adaptation tasks.[15] Finally, consistency regularization terms have been added to semi-supervised UDA by modeling inter-pixel relationships in network outputs, which has been shown to improve performance on benchmarks like GTA5 to Cityscapes.[23]

# Notable Algorithms and Frameworks

Research in universal domain adaptation for semantic segmentation has produced several distinct algorithmic approaches designed to address challenges such as domain shift, category shift, and the presence of unknown classes. These frameworks generally fall into categories that focus on feature alignment, adversarial learning, and open-set or boundless adaptation strategies.

## Feature and Adversarial Alignment Methods

Early and foundational work in this area introduced unsupervised adversarial approaches to pixel prediction problems, noting that fully convolutional models often suffer significant performance degradation under domain shifts, such as changes in geographic region or weather conditions.[12] To mitigate this, methods have been developed that employ both global and category-specific adaptation techniques, using fully convolutional domain adversarial learning to align the feature spaces of source and target domains.[12]
More recent advancements have focused on refining how features are aligned to improve discriminative power. One approach proposes learning discriminative feature representations of patches in the source domain by discovering multiple modes of patch-wise output distribution through the construction of a clustered space.[19] This framework uses an adversarial learning scheme to push the feature representations of target patches closer to the distributions of source patches within this clustered space, thereby adapting the source data to the unlabeled target domain without requiring per-pixel annotations.[19]
Another significant development involves the use of low-level information to guide high-level semantic transfer. A semantic-edge domain adaptation architecture was proposed to explicitly use low-level edge information, which typically exhibits a smaller inter-domain gap than high-level semantic features, to guide the transfer of semantic information.[24] This method utilizes an independent edge stream to generate high-quality semantic boundaries over the target domain and employs an edge consistency loss to align target semantic predictions with these produced boundaries.[24]

## Open-Set and Boundless Adaptation

A major challenge in real-world applications is the "open world" scenario, where unlabeled test images may contain unknown categories not present in the labeled training data. This setting is often referred to as open-set domain adaptation segmentation (OSDAS), where the classifier must identify unknown-class pixels while accurately classifying known-class pixels.[25] To solve this, researchers have investigated the distribution of unknown-class pixels and utilized goodness-of-fit tests to distinguish between known and unknown classes.[25]
In the broader context of visual recognition, open-set domain adaptation aims to automatically detect samples belonging to new, unknown classes and discard them from the recognition process.[2] This is achieved by relying on the intuition that source and target samples depicting known classes can be generated by a shared subspace, whereas target samples from unknown classes originate from a different, private subspace.[2]
Moving beyond simply discarding unknown classes, the concept of "boundless" adaptation has been introduced, which aims for explicit test-time prediction for new classes that do not exist in the source domain.[26] This framework leverages both domain adaptation and zero-shot learning techniques to bridge the source-target domain gap while learning to map new classes' labels to relevant visual representations.[26] The performance of such boundless adaptation is further enhanced by using self-training on target-domain pseudo-labels.[26]

## Universal and Scale-Aware Frameworks

Universal domain adaptation presents additional complexities, such as category shift and scale shift, where the label space of the source domain may differ from the target, and object scales can vary dramatically.[27] To address these issues, the Universal Scale-Aware Domain Adaptive Faster RCNN (US-DAF) was proposed to reduce negative transfer effects while maximizing transferability and discriminability.[27] This method incorporates a Filter Mechanism module to facilitate feature alignment of common classes and suppress interference from private classes, thereby overcoming negative transfer caused by category shift.[27] Additionally, it includes mechanisms to fill the gap in scale-aware adaptation, ensuring robust performance across varying object scales.[27]

## Auxiliary Techniques and Loss Functions

Several auxiliary techniques have been developed to enhance the stability and effectiveness of domain adaptation training. In semi-supervised learning approaches applied to unsupervised domain adaptation, the entropy minimization method is popular but can suffer from biased gradients towards easy-to-transfer samples.[28] To balance the gradient of well-classified target samples, the maximum squares loss has been proposed, which prevents the training process from being dominated by these easy samples.[28]
Furthermore, spatial propagation networks have been introduced to learn affinity matrices for vision tasks, providing a generic framework that can model dense, global pairwise relationships of an image.[29] By constructing a row/column linear propagation model, these networks can output all similarities in a purely data-driven manner, which can be applied to various affinity-related tasks within the segmentation pipeline.[29]

# Architectures

The architectural design of models for unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) in semantic segmentation has evolved significantly, moving from traditional convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to hybrid and transformer-based systems. These architectures are generally structured to bridge the domain gap between labeled source data and unlabeled target data, often employing encoder-decoder frameworks with specific modules for feature alignment and context awareness.

## Transformer-based Architectures

Recent research has highlighted the potential of Transformer architectures in UDA, revealing that they can outperform outdated network designs when systematically benchmarked. The DAFormer method, for instance, utilizes a Transformer encoder paired with a multi-level context-aware feature fusion decoder to effectively adapt synthetic data to real-world images without requiring target annotations[30]. Similarly, DETR-style detectors have been adapted for domain shift settings, where properties such as mitigating domain shift on backbone and decoder output features are crucial for favorable results[31]. In these setups, advanced domain alignment methods are applied to both the backbone and detector components to enhance performance[31].

## Hybrid and CNN-based Architectures

Hybrid architectures that combine the local feature extraction capabilities of CNNs with the global context modeling of Transformers have also been proposed. One such approach involves a hybrid transformer architecture consisting of a CNN-based encoder and a transformer-based decoder, specifically designed for remote sensing applications like land cover classification[32]. In this design, a feature adjustment module refines multiscale feature maps extracted from an EfficientNet backbone before integrating them into the transformer decoder[32].
Other CNN-centric approaches focus on improving feature representation and reconstruction. For example, adversarial-prediction guided multitask networks have been introduced to learn adaptation for novel unlabeled target domains by learning encoding representations for both supervised segmentation on the source domain and unsupervised reconstruction of target data[33]. This is often combined with multi-level adversarial learning to improve the discriminative ability of the model on the target domain[33]. Additionally, deep generative adversarial networks (GANs) have been employed with double-layered upsampling based on max-pooling indexed deconvolution to improve the extraction of low-resolution features and compensate for image size loss, thereby reducing dependence on large labeled datasets[34].

## Specialized and Emerging Architectures

Beyond standard image segmentation, architectural innovations have extended to other data modalities and specific compression tasks. In the realm of point cloud semantic segmentation, graph-based frameworks have been proposed to explore local-level feature alignment, addressing the semantic ambiguity often caused by global-level feature alignment in existing methods[35]. Furthermore, in the context of layered image compression, encoder-decoder matched semantic segmentation (EDMS) frameworks have been developed to obtain accurate semantic segments in the decoder without requiring extra transmission bits, using special convolutional neural networks to enhance inaccurate segments[36].
These diverse architectural strategies reflect the ongoing effort to balance computational efficiency, feature representation quality, and domain invariance in unsupervised semantic segmentation tasks.

# Datasets and Benchmarks

Research in universal domain adaptation (UDA) for semantic segmentation relies on a variety of datasets that capture the complexities of domain shifts, including differences in visual appearance, geometric structures, and category distributions. A primary source of data for these tasks involves synthetic datasets generated within controlled virtual environments, such as video games or traffic simulators, which allow for the automatic generation of pixel-level annotations.[6] These synthetic sources are frequently paired with real-world imagery to evaluate the transfer of knowledge from labeled source domains to unlabeled target domains.[6]
Specific benchmarks have been established to address the challenges of sim-to-real adaptation, particularly in fields like autonomous driving and robot vision.[22] In these scenarios, traditional UDA methods often assume the availability of abundant unlabeled real-world data during training; however, recent benchmarks explore more constrained settings, such as one-shot unsupervised sim-to-real domain adaptation (OSUDA), where only a single real-world data sample is available to guide the adaptation process.[22] To mitigate the scarcity of real data in such benchmarks, methods often construct pseudo-target domains by stylizing simulated data to better approximate real-world conditions.[22]
Beyond standard sim-to-real pairs, benchmarks for universal domain adaptation must account for arbitrary category shifts, where source and target domains may contain private classes not present in the other.[1][7] This necessitates datasets that reflect diverse ratios of common and private classes, challenging models to identify common samples for alignment without relying on manually specified thresholds.[1] Furthermore, the evaluation of UDA methods extends to remote sensing applications, utilizing satellite and aerial imagery datasets for tasks such as built-up region segmentation.[13] These datasets present unique challenges due to the diverse nature of geographical locations, terrains, and weather conditions, as well as the lack of strong spatial context compared to ground-level imagery.[13] Additionally, emerging benchmarks incorporate multi-modal data to test the adaptability of models trained independently on uni-modal sources, addressing scenarios where paired multi-modal data is unavailable or costly to collect.[37]

# Applications

Semantic segmentation, which involves assigning a class label to every pixel in an image, serves as a foundational component in various computer vision tasks, particularly where dense classification of scenes is required.[4][3] The application of domain adaptation techniques, specifically Unsupervised Domain Adaptation (UDA), has expanded the utility of these models by addressing the high cost of pixel-wise annotations and the performance degradation caused by domain shifts.[5]

## Autonomous Driving

One of the most prominent applications of semantic segmentation is in autonomous driving, where it facilitates scene understanding and object detection.[4][3] However, deploying top-performing segmentation models on autonomous vehicle platforms presents challenges due to limited computational resources and the strict requirement for low-latency, real-time inference.[3] To address the data-scarcity and labeling costs inherent in this field, researchers have proposed using synthetic images with automatically generated labels alongside unlabeled real-world images.[14] This approach frames the problem as an unsupervised domain adaptation task, where co-training procedures allow models to adapt from synthetic to real-world domains without requiring manual annotations for the real data.[14] Additionally, the robustness-efficiency trade-off is a critical consideration, leading to the development of compact models capable of running on low-memory embedded systems while maintaining real-time performance.[4]

## Remote Sensing

In the field of remote sensing, advances in deep learning have enabled efficient data analysis for applications such as land cover classification and change detection.[32] Hybrid architectures, such as those combining Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) with Transformer decoders, have been proposed to perform semantic segmentation of remote sensing images by refining multiscale feature maps.[32] A specific application of these techniques is in flood assessment, where satellite-based optical video sensors are used to capture transient dynamics.[38] In this context, hybrid transformer-encoder and CNN-decoder networks are employed to extract flood extents from satellite video, which are then used to validate 2D hydraulic model predictions.[38] This demonstrates the effectiveness of domain-adaptive semantic segmentation in processing complex environmental data for hydraulic modeling and validation.[38]

## Image Compression

Semantic segmentation also plays a role in layered image compression, a technique that encodes compact representations of input images and uses up-sampling networks for reconstruction.[36] To improve the quality of reconstructed images, some methods transmit semantic segments alongside compressed image data, although this increases the compression ratio due to the extra bits required.[36] To mitigate this, frameworks such as Encoder-Decoder Matched Semantic Segmentation (EDMS) have been developed to obtain accurate semantic segments in the decoder without requiring additional transmission bits, thereby enhancing the efficiency of the compression process.[36]

## Education and Laboratory Simulation

Beyond traditional computer vision tasks, the principles of remote access and simulation have been applied in educational contexts, such as the UniMAP e-Lab project.[39] This system utilizes communication and control technology to provide laboratory experiences for students who cannot attend conventional labs, addressing constraints related to space and distance learning.[39] While distinct from the deep learning applications in driving or remote sensing, this highlights the broader application of simulated environments and remote data processing in engineering and technology education.[39]

# Challenges and Limitations

Despite the progress in unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) for semantic segmentation, several significant challenges and limitations persist, hindering the robust deployment of these models in real-world applications. A primary obstacle is the reliance on source data availability and specific data structures. Many existing approaches assume that source data is accessible during the adaptation phase and that it consists of paired multi-modal data.[37] These assumptions are often problematic in practical scenarios where source data may be unavailable due to privacy, security, or economic concerns.[37] Furthermore, assuming the existence of paired multi-modal data entails significant data collection costs and fails to leverage widely available, freely distributed pre-trained uni-modal models.[37]
The difficulty of obtaining high-quality annotations remains a major bottleneck. Semantic segmentation models require a large number of samples with pixel-level labels, which is labor-intensive and expensive.[5][19][28] This lack of data fitting specific requirements is the main limitation in the deployment of these techniques.[5] Consequently, models trained on one data domain often fail to generalize well to other domains without annotations for model finetuning.[19] Even when UDA methods are employed to transfer knowledge from labeled source domains to unlabeled target domains, they often suffer from severe domain shift issues.[40] For instance, performance can be surprisingly poor under domain shifts that appear mild to human observers, such as changes in geographic region or weather conditions, due to pixel-level distribution shifts.[12]
Another critical limitation is the assumption regarding category overlap between source and target domains. Traditional UDA methods assume that all source categories are present in the target domain.[7] In practice, little may be known about this overlap, and unlabeled test images in an open world may contain unknown categories and have different distributions from the labeled images.[25] While some methods address partial or open-set categories, they often assume that the particular setting is known a priori, limiting their universal applicability.[7] This necessitates more robust frameworks that can handle arbitrary category shifts and identify unknown-class pixels effectively.[7]
Additionally, current methods for class-conditioned domain alignment often rely on pseudo-label estimations of the target domain, which can suffer from pseudo-label bias in the form of error accumulation.[41] This bias can lead to a "domain-discriminator shortcut" in misaligned classes, degrading performance.[41] Similarly, when applying entropy minimization methods to UDA for semantic segmentation, the gradient of the entropy is often biased towards samples that are easy to transfer, potentially causing the training process to be dominated by well-classified target samples while neglecting harder cases.[28] Addressing these biases and ensuring balanced gradient updates remain active areas of research to improve the robustness of domain adaptation models.[41]
