# summary

Generative Retrieval (GR) represents a paradigm shift in information retrieval by reformulating the task as the direct generation of document identifiers (docids) rather than relying on traditional index-then-retrieve pipelines.[1][2] Unlike conventional methods that depend on explicit indexing structures, GR models store relevance information within their parameters, enabling end-to-end optimization and seamless integration with large language models (LLMs).[3] This approach offers potential improvements in efficiency and allows for a more unified architecture where the retrieval process is handled by a single neural network.[3]
Despite its advantages, the application of GR to dynamic corpora presents significant challenges, as traditional models are typically trained on static document collections and become brittle when new documents are continuously added.[4] Deploying Differentiable Search Indices (DSIs) in situations where the corpus changes over time is computationally expensive, as reindexing often requires re-training the entire model.[5] Furthermore, research indicates that continual indexing of new documents can lead to considerable "forgetting" of previously indexed documents, resulting in unstable learning and performance degradation.[5]
To address these limitations, recent research has focused on developing continual learning frameworks for GR. The CLEVER model, for instance, introduces Incremental Product Quantization to encode new documents into docids with low computational cost, aiming to mitigate the issues associated with dynamic corpora.[4] Similarly, the DSI++ framework addresses the continual learning challenge by analyzing and mitigating forgetting events during training, ensuring that models can maintain performance across different scales and document identifier representations.[5] Additionally, methods like Nonparametric Decoding have been proposed to overcome the fixed information capacity of parametric models by utilizing external memory, thereby enhancing the model's ability to handle diverse and evolving data.[6]
Beyond dynamic indexing, the generalization capabilities of GR in zero-shot and out-of-distribution scenarios remain a critical area of exploration. Frameworks such as ZeroGR leverage natural language instructions to extend GR across a wide range of IR tasks without task-specific training, addressing the brittleness of supervised models in real-world applications.[7] Other approaches, like QueStER, bridge GR and query reformulation by generating explicit keyword-based search specifications, combining the generalization benefits of generative rewriting with the efficiency of lexical indexing.[8] These developments highlight the ongoing effort to make GR systems more resilient, scalable, and adaptable to the complexities of modern information retrieval environments.[9]

# Theoretical Framework

The theoretical foundation of generative retrieval (GR) represents a paradigm shift from traditional information retrieval systems, which rely on static index-then-retrieve pipelines, to an end-to-end approach where relevance is stored within model parameters [8]. In this framework, the retrieval task is reframed as a sequential generation problem, where a large language model (LLM) directly predicts document identifiers (DocIDs) based on a given query [10][11]. This approach aims to replace traditional search index data structures with a single large-scale neural network, offering the potential for improved efficiency and seamless integration with generative models [3].
A central theoretical challenge in GR is the design of document identifiers that balance semantic richness with tractable search spaces [12]. Traditional codebook methods construct hierarchical semantic trees to constrain generation, but their numeric identifiers often fail to leverage the pretrained natural language understanding capabilities of LLMs [12]. Conversely, using text-based DocIDs provides greater semantic expressivity but inflates the decoding space, making the system brittle to early-step errors [12]. To address this, recent theoretical developments propose hybrid approaches, such as C2T-ID, which iteratively replace numeric labels with high-frequency metadata keywords to enhance fluency and semantic alignment [12]. Furthermore, the reliance on left-to-right auto-regressive decoding introduces fundamental limitations, including a mismatch between DocID generation and natural language generation, where an incorrect token at an early step can lead to entirely erroneous retrieval [10]. This has led to the exploration of alternative decoding mechanisms, such as discrete diffusion processes, which allow for dynamic balancing of retrieval efficiency and accuracy [10].
The application of GR to dynamic corpora introduces specific theoretical constraints related to continual learning and out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization. Traditional GR models assume a static document collection, yet practical scenarios require the ability to incrementally index new documents while preserving query performance for both previously and newly indexed content [4]. Theoretically, full retraining of the model for each new document is computationally expensive and impractical [13]. Consequently, frameworks like CLEVER and MixLoRA-DSI have been developed to address this by employing incremental product quantization and expandable mixture of Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) experts, respectively [4][13]. These methods aim to achieve sublinear parameter growth by selectively introducing new experts only when a significant number of OOD documents are detected, thereby maintaining model stability and efficiency in evolving environments [13].
Additionally, the integration of GR with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems presents unique theoretical challenges regarding feature locality and parametric knowledge [14]. While LLMs offer strong semantic understanding, their massive parametric knowledge can hinder the effective usage of global information across the corpus, often leading to the use of document summaries rather than full documents [14]. To bridge the gap between the generalization benefits of generative query rewriting and the efficiency of lexical indexing, models like QueStER have been proposed, which use reinforcement learning to generate explicit keyword-based search specifications executed by standard retrievers like BM25 [8]. This hybrid approach theoretically combines the robustness of generative models with the scalability of traditional indexing, addressing the brittleness of pure GR models in out-of-domain settings [3][8].

# Methodology

The methodology for evaluating generative retrieval (GR) over dynamic corpora involves a systematic reproduction and assessment of representative GR approaches to address the limitations of static document collections. A primary focus of this evaluation is the performance of models when document collections evolve continuously, a scenario that is prevalent in real-world applications but rarely studied in existing literature [15]. The experimental framework distinguishes between different types of document identifiers (docids), specifically comparing text-based and numeric-based designs to understand their impact on generalization and efficiency [15].
A key component of the methodology is the analysis of docid design granularity. The evaluation reveals that the fine-grained nature of docid design significantly influences performance in dynamic settings. Models utilizing text-based docids demonstrate superior generalization to unseen documents compared to those using numeric-based docids [15]. Specifically, the more fine-grained the docid design, the better the model's performance over dynamic corpora, with such models surpassing traditional BM25 baselines and achieving results comparable to dense retrieval methods [15]. In contrast, while GR models with numeric-based docids offer high computational efficiency, their performance drops significantly when applied to dynamic corpora, highlighting a trade-off between efficiency and adaptability [15].
To address the challenge of continually updating model-based indexes without the prohibitive cost of full retraining, the methodology incorporates the evaluation of continual learning frameworks. One such approach is the Continual-LEarner for generatiVE Retrieval (CLEVER) model, which aims to incrementally index new documents while preserving the ability to answer queries using both previously and newly indexed relevant documents [4]. The CLEVER model employs Incremental Product Quantization to encode new documents into docids with low computational cost, addressing the practical continual learning problem inherent in dynamic environments [4].
Additionally, the methodology examines alternative optimization strategies to mitigate training-inference inconsistencies and improve robustness. For instance, the Adaptive Prefix-Aware Optimization (APAO) is evaluated to address the issue where standard training assumes ground-truth history is always available, ignoring the pruning of low-probability branches during beam search inference [16]. This inconsistency can lead to the premature discarding of correct items if their initial token prefixes have low scores [16]. Furthermore, the evaluation includes the MixLoRA-DSI framework, which combines an expandable mixture of Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) experts with a layer-wise out-of-distribution (OOD)-driven expansion strategy [13]. This approach enables sublinear parameter growth by selectively introducing new experts only when a significant number of OOD documents are detected, thereby outperforming full-model update baselines with minimal parameter overhead and lower training costs [13].
The broader context of these methodologies is informed by the evolution of neural information retrieval models, which have seen significant enhancements in effectiveness and robustness [17]. The evaluation also considers the implications of generative AI failures and risks, ensuring that the proposed GR systems are resilient and secure [18]. By integrating these diverse methodological approaches—from docid design and continual learning to optimization strategies and robustness assessments—the study provides a comprehensive framework for deploying generative retrieval systems in dynamic, real-world scenarios [9][15][4][16][13].

# Experimental Setup

The experimental evaluation of generative retrieval (GR) over dynamic corpora focuses on addressing the challenges of continual learning, where document collections are not static but continuously updated with new data. To assess the efficacy of various GR frameworks in this context, researchers utilize standard large-scale benchmarks such as NQ320k and MS MARCO Passage, which provide the necessary volume and diversity of documents to test incremental indexing capabilities [13][4].
A primary objective in these experiments is to evaluate the computational efficiency of updating model-based indexes without resorting to full retraining, a process that is often computationally expensive and impractical under resource constraints [13]. For instance, the MixLoRA-DSI framework is tested to demonstrate that it can outperform full-model update baselines while maintaining minimal parameter overhead and substantially lower training costs [13]. Similarly, the CLEVER model is evaluated on its ability to encode new documents into document identifiers (docids) with low computational cost, ensuring that the system can preserve the ability to answer queries using both previously and newly indexed relevant documents [4].
The experimental design also incorporates metrics for robustness and generalization, particularly in zero-shot scenarios which are prevalent in real-world applications [7][17]. Frameworks like ZeroGR are assessed on their capacity to extend GR across a wide range of IR tasks using natural language instructions, leveraging an LM-based docid generator to unify heterogeneous documents such as text, tables, and code [7]. Furthermore, experiments often dissect the robustness of these models against adversarial attacks and out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, recognizing these as pivotal components for ensuring reliability in practice [17].
To address the limitations of traditional left-to-right auto-regressive decoding, which can suffer from mismatches between DocID generation and natural language generation, experiments with diffusion-based models like DiffuGR are conducted [10]. These setups evaluate how formulating DocID generation as a discrete diffusion process allows for a dynamic balance between retrieval efficiency and accuracy, overcoming the issue where an incorrect early token leads to entirely erroneous retrieval [10]. Additionally, the integration of reasoning capabilities is tested through frameworks like Reason-for-Retrieval (R4R), where preliminary studies compare standard GR against methods that generate free-form chain-of-thought reasoning before constrained docid decoding, aiming to improve alignment with the docid space [11].

# Results and Analysis

The evaluation of generative retrieval (GR) models over dynamic corpora reveals distinct performance characteristics depending on the design of document identifiers (docids) and the specific challenges posed by evolving document collections. Systematic reproduction and evaluation of representative GR approaches indicate that models utilizing text-based docids demonstrate superior generalization capabilities when encountering unseen documents compared to those using numeric-based identifiers [15]. While numeric-based docids offer high computational efficiency, their performance degrades significantly in dynamic environments where the corpus is continuously updated [15].
A key finding in the analysis of docid granularity is that finer-grained docid designs correlate with better performance over dynamic corpora [15]. Specifically, GR models with more fine-grained text-based docids have been observed to surpass the performance of traditional BM25 baselines and achieve results comparable to dense retrieval methods [15]. This suggests that the semantic expressivity provided by text-based identifiers is crucial for maintaining retrieval accuracy as the document collection evolves, whereas the brittleness of numeric identifiers limits their adaptability [15][12].
The challenge of continual learning in GR, specifically the ability to incrementally index new documents while preserving query performance, has been addressed by models such as CLEVER (Continual-LEarner for generatiVE Retrieval) [4]. CLEVER introduces Incremental Product Quantization to encode new documents into docids with low computational cost, addressing the practical need for dynamic indexing without full retraining [4]. However, the inherent limitations of constrained auto-regressive generation remain a subject of scrutiny. Analysis from a Bayes-optimal perspective highlights that constraints and beam search strategies can limit the generalization capabilities of GR models, particularly on out-of-distribution corpora [3]. The mismatch between DocID generation and natural language generation, where an incorrect token at an early step leads to erroneous retrieval, further complicates the robustness of these systems in dynamic settings [10].
Furthermore, the integration of reasoning capabilities into GR has shown potential to improve performance. Preliminary studies indicate that prompting Large Language Models (LLMs) to generate chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning before constrained docid decoding outperforms standard GR methods [11]. However, this approach is limited by verbose reasoning that is often poorly aligned with the docid space, motivating the development of more tailored reasoning mechanisms like Reason-for-Retrieval (R4R) [11]. In the context of broader retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) systems, which often rely on dense retrievers, explainability frameworks such as RAG-E have revealed critical misalignments between retriever rankings and generator usage, with generators ignoring top-ranked documents in a significant portion of queries [19]. These findings underscore the complexity of ensuring reliable and efficient retrieval in dynamic, real-world scenarios where document collections are not static [15][4].

# Discussion

The replication and exploration of Generative Retrieval (GR) over dynamic corpora highlight a critical tension between the efficiency of end-to-end neural architectures and the practical challenges of maintaining model relevance as data evolves. While GR offers a paradigm shift by replacing traditional index-retrieve-then-rank pipelines with a single generative model that directly produces document identifiers (DocIDs), this approach introduces specific vulnerabilities regarding generalization and adaptability [2][7]. A primary concern in dynamic environments is the issue of catastrophic forgetting, where models lose previously learned knowledge when updated with new data. Recent empirical evaluations suggest that while generative models may exhibit different robustness characteristics compared to discriminative models, the root causes of forgetting remain a significant hurdle for continual learning in retrieval systems [20]. Consequently, full retraining of GR models to accommodate new documents is often computationally expensive and impractical, necessitating more efficient update mechanisms [13].
To address the challenge of dynamic corpora, novel frameworks such as MixLoRA-DSI have been proposed to enable sublinear parameter growth by selectively introducing new experts only when significant out-of-distribution (OOD) documents are detected [13]. This approach contrasts with traditional methods that allocate new experts for every new corpus, thereby reducing training costs while maintaining performance on benchmarks like NQ320k and MS MARCO Passage [13]. However, the effectiveness of these updates is intrinsically linked to the design of the document identifiers themselves. Designing DocIDs that balance rich semantic information with tractable search spaces remains a fundamental challenge; for instance, numeric identifiers derived from hierarchical clustering may fail to leverage the pretrained natural language understanding of large language models, whereas text-based identifiers can inflate the decoding space and increase brittleness to early-step errors [12]. Methods like C2T-ID attempt to resolve this by iteratively replacing numeric labels with high-frequency metadata keywords, enhancing both semantic expressivity and fluency [12].
Furthermore, the decoding strategy employed in GR significantly impacts the quality and reliability of retrieval results. Traditional left-to-right auto-regressive decoding suffers from a mismatch between DocID generation and natural language generation, where an incorrect token at an early step can lead to entirely erroneous retrieval outcomes [10]. Additionally, standard decoding strategies often struggle to dynamically balance the trade-off between retrieval efficiency and accuracy, a crucial requirement for practical applications [10]. To mitigate these issues, approaches such as DiffuGR formulate DocID generation as a discrete diffusion process, allowing for more robust handling of stochastic masking and potentially improving the model's ability to recover from early errors [10]. In the context of broader Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) systems, the opacity of how retrievers and generators interact poses further challenges for deployment in high-stakes domains [19]. Explainability frameworks, such as RAG-E, are essential for quantifying retriever-generator alignment, as empirical analysis has revealed that generators frequently ignore the retriever's top-ranked documents, leading to critical misalignments in output quality [19].
Finally, the integration of GR into dynamic systems must be viewed within the broader context of AI safety and risk management. As generative AI is applied to an ever-growing set of domains, the risks of harm due to failures in design, deployment, or downstream usage expand [18]. A systematic analysis of publicly reported incidents indicates that most harms arise from specific failure modes that can be mapped to impacted stakeholders [18]. Therefore, while the technical advancements in dynamic GR—such as efficient parameter updates and improved DocID design—offer promising solutions for scalability and accuracy, they must be accompanied by rigorous evaluation frameworks that address trust, alignment, and the potential for unintended harms in real-world deployments [9][18].
