# summary

**TaxAgent** is a theoretical and methodological framework that integrates Large Language Models (LLMs) with Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) to design and evaluate adaptive fiscal policies.[1] By transitioning from reactive generative AI to **Agentic AI**, the system proactively orchestrates complex, multi-turn workflows to translate natural-language statutes into executable logic, addressing the limitations of traditional optimal taxation models that often fail to account for taxpayer heterogeneity and irrational behavior.[2][1] The framework is notable for its ability to simulate strategic economic environments where both individual behaviors and government strategies evolve, offering a dynamic alternative to static policy analysis.[3]
The methodology employs a hierarchical decision-making structure consisting of bounded rational worker agents, or H-Agents, and a planner agent designated as the TaxAgent.[3] H-Agents are instantiated as persona-conditioned prompts sampled from U.S. Census-calibrated demographic statistics, allowing them to maximize text-based utility functions and exhibit realistic labor supply responses.[3] At the upper level, the TaxAgent utilizes in-context reinforcement learning to iteratively optimize piecewise-linear marginal tax schedules, balancing equity and productivity while anchoring proposals to current U.S. federal brackets.[3][1] This integration enables the simulation of heterogeneous utilities and the generation of demographically realistic agent populations, facilitating the assessment of fiscal policies in a dynamic environment.[3]
Experimental results indicate that TaxAgent achieves superior equity-efficiency trade-offs compared to established benchmarks, including the Saez Optimal Taxation model, current U.S. federal income taxes, and free market scenarios.[1] The adaptive nature of the LLM-driven policy design allows for more effective management of economic disparities and productivity losses than static or less flexible taxation frameworks.[1] By embedding an internal tax calculator into large-scale overlapping generations models, the framework explicitly models key tax provisions, ensuring that the macroeconomic implications of omitted tax details are accounted for in policy analysis.[4]
Despite its potential, the framework faces significant legal, technical, and ethical challenges, primarily concerning the liability for autonomous AI decisions and the risk of LLM "hallucinations" in legally critical domains.[5][6] To mitigate these risks, TaxAgent incorporates **Simulation-in-the-Reasoning (SiR)** to ground reasoning in empirical reality and emphasizes human oversight as an essential procedural safeguard against the risks of increasing reliance on algorithmic tools.[7][8] The framework advocates for a "consultative" AI paradigm that prioritizes veracity and traceability, acting as a tool to amplify professional judgment rather than replace it, while addressing gaps in responsibility attribution and liability through concepts such as "Operational Agency."[5][6]

# Theoretical Framework

The theoretical underpinnings of TaxAgent integrate concepts from agentic artificial intelligence, legal informatics, and computational economics to address the challenges of applying Large Language Models (LLMs) to fiscal policy and tax preparation. Central to this framework is the transition from reactive generative AI to **Agentic AI**, which proactively orchestrates complex, multi-turn workflows and makes autonomous decisions rather than merely responding to prompts [2]. This shift is critical for legal-critical software, where the system must navigate ambiguity and manage long-term goals, such as translating natural-language statutes into executable logic [9].
A significant theoretical challenge in this domain is the **oracle problem** in test-case generation, where determining correct outputs requires interpreting law [9]. To address this, the framework incorporates **metamorphic testing**, specifically introducing higher-order metamorphic relations that compare system outputs across structured shifts among similar individuals [9]. This approach allows for the verification of system behavior without requiring a single, definitive "correct" answer for every input, thereby mitigating the risks associated with LLM **hallucinations**—the generation of false information [5]. While Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is a common mitigation strategy, the framework acknowledges its limitations and advocates for a "consultative" AI paradigm that prioritizes veracity and traceability, acting as a tool to amplify professional judgment rather than replace it [5].
To ground the LLM's reasoning in empirical reality, the framework draws on the concept of **Simulation-in-the-Reasoning (SiR)** [7]. SiR embeds domain-specific simulators directly into the LLM reasoning loop, transforming reasoning from narrative plausibility into a falsifiable, hypothesis-simulate-analyze workflow [7]. In the context of fiscal policy, this allows the agent to formulate hypotheses regarding tax strategies or policy impacts, invoke simulators to evaluate results under different demand patterns or demographic shifts, and refine strategies through verification [7]. This is complemented by the integration of **domain-specific databases** and real-time data, which overcomes the limitations of general-purpose language models by providing specialized, up-to-date information for tax-related inquiries [10].
The economic modeling component of the framework utilizes **agent-based modeling** to design and assess policies in strategic environments with hierarchical decision-making [3]. This involves creating bounded rational worker agents, instantiated as persona-conditioned prompts sampled from demographic statistics, who choose labor supply to maximize text-based utility functions [3]. At a higher level, a planner agent employs in-context reinforcement learning to propose tax schedules, enabling the simulation of heterogeneous utilities and the generation of demographically realistic agent populations [3]. This approach addresses the gap in existing economic simulation platforms, which often remain limited to simplified tasks, by implementing a scalable and modular testbed that connects diverse economic tasks with AI algorithms [11]. By embedding an internal tax calculator into large-scale overlapping generations models, the framework explicitly models key tax provisions, ensuring that the macroeconomic implications of omitted tax details are accounted for in policy analysis [4].
Finally, the framework emphasizes the critical role of **human oversight** in automated legal decision-making [8]. While agentic AI offers autonomy, the framework recognizes that human oversight serves as an essential procedural safeguard against the risks of increasing reliance on algorithmic tools [8]. The theoretical model posits that without awareness of the inherent limitations of human control over complex technical systems, oversight can become an empty procedural shell [8]. Therefore, the design of TaxAgent integrates human-in-the-loop mechanisms to ensure that the AI's proactive execution remains aligned with legal, ethical, and economic frameworks, addressing gaps in responsibility attribution and liability [2].

# Methodology

The TaxAgent framework employs a novel integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) with Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) to design and evaluate adaptive tax policies within a macroeconomic simulation environment.[1] This approach addresses the limitations of traditional optimal taxation models, such as the Saez Optimal Taxation, which often fail to account for taxpayer heterogeneity and irrational behavior.[1] The methodology is structured around a hierarchical decision-making process that simulates strategic economic environments.[3]
At the lower level of the simulation, the model utilizes bounded rational worker agents, referred to as H-Agents (households), to simulate real-world taxpayer behaviors.[1][3] These agents are instantiated as persona-conditioned prompts sampled from U.S. Census-calibrated income and demographic statistics, ensuring the generation of large, demographically realistic agent populations.[3] Each agent chooses labor supply to maximize text-based utility functions that are learned in-context, allowing the model to capture heterogeneous utilities and complex behavioral responses to fiscal changes.[3]
At the upper level, a planner agent, designated as the TaxAgent, acts as the government entity responsible for policy design.[1][3] This agent employs in-context reinforcement learning to iteratively optimize tax rates, balancing equity and productivity.[1] Specifically, the planner proposes piecewise-linear marginal tax schedules anchored to current U.S. federal brackets, facilitating mechanism design and the strategic nudging of economic outcomes.[3] By embedding this internal tax calculator and decision-making loop within the simulation, the framework enables the assessment of fiscal policies in a dynamic environment where both individual behaviors and government strategies evolve.[3]
The performance of the TaxAgent methodology is benchmarked against several established standards, including the Saez Optimal Taxation model, current U.S. federal income taxes, and free market scenarios.[1] This comparative analysis allows for the evaluation of equity-efficiency trade-offs, demonstrating how the integration of LLMs with ABM can yield superior policy outcomes compared to traditional static or non-adaptive models.[1]

# Experimental Results

The experimental evaluation of TaxAgent focuses on its ability to design adaptive tax policies that balance equity and productivity within a macroeconomic simulation framework. By integrating Large Language Models (LLMs) with agent-based modeling (ABM), the study simulates heterogeneous household agents (H-Agents) that exhibit real-world taxpayer behaviors, while the TaxAgent iteratively optimizes tax rates[1]. The primary objective of these experiments is to assess the performance of TaxAgent against established benchmarks, including the Saez Optimal Taxation model, the current U.S. federal income tax system, and free market scenarios[1].
The results indicate that TaxAgent achieves superior equity-efficiency trade-offs compared to these traditional models[1]. This suggests that the adaptive nature of the LLM-driven policy design allows for more effective management of economic disparities and productivity losses than static or less flexible taxation frameworks[1]. The simulation highlights the model's capacity to address taxpayer heterogeneity and irrational behavior, areas where previous models like Saez Optimal Taxation have been noted to fail[1].
In the context of broader fiscal policy analysis, the experiments also align with findings from heterogeneous-agent models that emphasize the importance of detailed tax provisions. While conventional approaches often use smooth tax functions to approximate complex tax laws, recent studies show that embedding explicit tax calculators into large-scale models can generate similar macroeconomic patterns while capturing variations in household tax-preferred consumption and labor supply[4]. The TaxAgent framework leverages this complexity to refine policy outcomes, demonstrating that the integration of LLMs can enhance the precision of fiscal policy design in dynamic economic environments[1][4].

# Legal and Regulatory Implications

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into fiscal policy design, such as through the TaxAgent framework, introduces significant legal and regulatory challenges that extend beyond traditional software compliance. A primary concern is the liability for decisions made by AI systems that operate with a high degree of independence yet lack legal personhood. This paradox fractures existing legal doctrines grounded in human-centric notions of *mens rea* (intent) and *actus reus* (act), making it difficult to assign responsibility when harmful conduct or outcomes are unforeseeable[6]. To address this, scholars have proposed the concept of "Operational Agency," a legal fiction that evaluates an AI’s observable characteristics—such as goal-directedness and safety architecture—to trace and apportion culpability among developers, deployers, and users[6]. Without such frameworks, the unpredictability of how courts handle AI-related harms creates incalculable risks for investment and innovation, potentially depriving society of the benefits these technologies offer[12].
Regulatory uncertainty is further compounded by the inadequacy of current legal liability systems to handle the specific risks of emerging technologies. In the European Union, policy debates have focused on compulsory liability insurance for AI-related harm, though critics argue that insurance alone is insufficient to cover the full spectrum of risks created by digital technologies[13]. For liability insurance to be effective, it must be established in a balanced, evidence-based manner, specifically when there is a risk of serious damage leading to insolvency[13]. Additionally, the application of statutory law to AI actions remains a subject of theoretical debate. The "social salience theory" suggests that statutes should apply to act-tokens that are socially salient within their linguistic and social contexts, offering a more robust approach to statutory application than theories based solely on legislative intention or literal classification[14].
Human oversight is another critical regulatory component, particularly under the European AI Act, which mandates human control over algorithmic systems[15]. However, testing compliance with these oversight requirements presents significant challenges, as it requires balancing simple checklist-based approaches with resource-intensive empirical testing of effectiveness[15]. There is a risk that human oversight becomes an "empty procedural shell" if not implemented with awareness of its inherent limitations, potentially failing to protect fundamental rights while serving as a justification for algorithmization[8]. In the context of fiscal policy, where decisions have broad economic impacts, ensuring that human oversight is substantive rather than merely procedural is essential for maintaining public trust and legal compliance[8][15].
Furthermore, the use of LLMs in policy design raises issues regarding data governance and intellectual property. The rapid advancement of general-purpose AI models has increased concerns about copyright infringement in training data, with current regulatory frameworks remaining predominantly reactive[16]. Critical gaps exist in pre-training data filtering and license collection, which threaten both creator rights and the sustainability of AI development[16]. While existing solutions like transparency tools and access control mechanisms address specific aspects of the problem, they do not prevent initial copyright violations, highlighting the need for more proactive regulatory measures[16].
Finally, the financial and economic implications of AI-driven policy tools require robust safeguards to prevent the reproduction of market inefficiencies. A framework for responsible AI in financial and economic planning should be anchored in principles such as fiduciary duty, technical robustness, and auditability[17]. To ensure these principles are met, a novel blueprint for auditing generative AI systems has been proposed, involving a three-layered approach: governance audits of technology providers, model audits prior to release, and application audits of downstream uses[18]. This multi-layered auditing strategy is crucial for addressing the governance challenges posed by AI systems that display emergent capabilities and are adaptable to a wide range of tasks, including complex fiscal policy simulations[18].

# Limitations

The application of Large Language Models (LLMs) to fiscal policy and tax administration faces significant technical, legal, and ethical limitations. A primary technical challenge is the phenomenon of "hallucinations," where models generate false information, which poses severe risks in legally regulated domains.[5] While Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is often employed as a mitigation strategy, it has inherent limitations and cannot fully resolve the issue of veracity.[5] Furthermore, LLMs exhibit notable gaps in highly specialized, knowledge-intensive areas such as taxation, often neglecting real-world practical capabilities in favor of isolated natural language processing tasks.[19] The dynamic nature of tax regulations also presents a challenge for maintaining accurate software artifacts, as current methods for updating tax preparation software are time-consuming and error-prone, relying heavily on manual code analysis and expert interpretation.[20]
Legal and regulatory frameworks currently struggle to address the complexities of AI-generated outputs and agency. Existing statutes often exhibit anthropocentric biases, predicating liability on demonstrable human intent, which renders them ill-equipped to handle algorithmic deception or autonomous decision-making.[21] This creates a "moral crumple zone" where responsibility attribution is fractured, particularly when AI systems exhibit "fluid agency" that entangles human and machine inputs, making provenance indeterminate.[22] In the context of intellectual property, there are significant gaps in laws regarding AI-generated outputs, with some jurisdictions lacking specific provisions that create doctrinal inconsistencies and enforcement inefficacies.[23] Additionally, the enforceability of restrictive terms of use attached to AI models is questionable, as model creators have rarely pursued monetary penalties or injunctive relief for violations, suggesting that these legal tools may not be as robust as intended.[24]
Ethical concerns further complicate the integration of AI in fiscal contexts. AI-powered greenwashing has emerged as a challenge within corporate sustainability governance, exacerbating the opacity of environmental disclosures and subverting regulatory oversight.[21] Moreover, the rapid advancement of general-purpose AI models has increased concerns about copyright infringement in training data, yet current regulatory frameworks remain predominantly reactive rather than proactive, with critical gaps in pre-training data filtering and license collection.[16] These limitations highlight the need for a shift from incrementally improving generative models to adopting a "consultative" AI paradigm that prioritizes veracity, traceability, and human oversight.[5]

# Future Directions

Research into the application of Large Language Models (LLMs) for fiscal policy design, such as the TaxAgent framework, suggests several promising avenues for future development, ranging from enhanced simulation fidelity to broader legal and regulatory integration. A primary direction involves improving the realism and complexity of agent-based models to better capture human behavior. Current approaches, such as the LLM Economist, utilize persona-conditioned prompts sampled from demographic statistics to simulate bounded rationality and heterogeneous utility optimization[3]. Future work may expand these simulations to include more complex socio-economic dynamics, such as the emergence of informal economic activities and tax evasion, which have historically been modeled using computational frameworks that account for the "big bang" of such behaviors in populations[25]. By integrating these dynamics, researchers can assess how adaptive tax policies interact with non-compliance and informal market structures.
Another significant area of advancement is the integration of empirical grounding and simulation directly into the reasoning loop of LLMs. The Simulation-in-the-Reasoning (SiR) framework demonstrates that embedding domain-specific simulators into the LLM reasoning process transforms narrative plausibility into a falsifiable, hypothesis-simulate-analyze workflow[7]. Applying this to fiscal policy could allow LLMs to not only propose tax schedules but to rigorously test their macroeconomic implications against dynamic simulators before finalization. This approach addresses the challenge of ensuring that policy recommendations are not merely theoretically sound but empirically robust, potentially mitigating the risks associated with LLM hallucinations in legally and economically critical settings[9].
Furthermore, future research may focus on the legal and regulatory frameworks necessary to support AI-driven policy design. As LLMs become more capable of interpreting and applying complex statutes, such as the U.S. federal tax code, there is a growing need for reliable methods to translate natural-language laws into executable logic[9]. This includes developing agentic approaches that can handle the ambiguity inherent in legal texts and generate test cases to verify the correctness of the resulting software[9]. Additionally, the legal status of AI entities themselves remains an open question; research into the international legal personality of artificial intelligence may influence how AI agents are held accountable for policy decisions or economic actions[26].
Finally, addressing the regulatory uncertainty surrounding AI innovation is crucial for the widespread adoption of tools like TaxAgent. The current legal liability system is often ill-equipped to handle unforeseeable harms caused by AI, creating risks that may stifle innovation[12]. Future directions include the development of AI guarantee schemes, drawing insights from financial regulatory best practices, to provide certainty in the regulatory environment[12]. Simultaneously, addressing the governance of AI training data, including copyright infringement and pre-training data filtering, is essential to ensure the sustainability and ethical integrity of the models used in policy design[16]. By tackling these legal, technical, and economic challenges, future work can enhance the credibility and applicability of LLM-driven fiscal policy tools.
