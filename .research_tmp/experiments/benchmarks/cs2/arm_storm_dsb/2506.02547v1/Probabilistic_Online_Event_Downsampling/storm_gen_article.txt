# Overview

Probabilistic online event downsampling is a technique used to manage the volume of data in streaming environments by selectively retaining a subset of events based on probabilistic criteria. This approach is particularly relevant in scenarios where maintaining the accuracy of supervised learning models is critical in the presence of evolving data streams. By employing temporally-biased sampling schemes, the method weights recent data most heavily, with the inclusion probabilities for a given data item decaying over time according to a specified "decay function"[1]. This strategy allows models to adapt to recent changes in the data while retaining some older data to ensure robustness against temporary fluctuations and periodicities, unlike sliding-window approaches that discard older information entirely[1].
The primary advantage of this sampling-based approach is that it speeds up the training process relative to training on all available data, while allowing existing analytic algorithms designed for static data to be applied to dynamic streaming data with minimal modification[1]. In the context of event detection, such as identifying social signals in audio recordings, probabilistic sampling is also utilized to address class imbalance, where only a small fraction of training data corresponds to the target events[2]. By seeking to balance the class distribution, this technique alleviates difficulties during the training of Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) that arise from strong imbalances in training labels[2].
Furthermore, the concept of disentangling event types is central to understanding complex systems. In fields such as seismology and time series analysis, researchers use stochastic declustering methods to distinguish between exogenous influences and endogenous organization, where future events are partially triggered by past ones[3]. However, studies have indicated that declustered catalogs can be unreliable for synthetic catalogs of realistic sizes, with estimated rates of exogenous events suffering from large errors[3]. Similarly, in predictive modeling, learning to predict future events from past patterns is challenging when the set of possible event types is large, as unrestricted neural models may overfit to spurious patterns[4]. To mitigate this, temporal deductive databases and neural probabilistic models derived from concise Datalog programs are used to encode domain-specific knowledge, improving prediction accuracy by tracking structured facts over time[4]. These methods highlight the broader utility of probabilistic frameworks in handling event data, from downsampling for efficiency to complex inference in dynamic environments.

# Theoretical Foundations

The theoretical underpinnings of probabilistic online event downsampling are rooted in information theory, stochastic processes, and the mathematical characterization of probability distributions. A central component of this framework is the use of probability density functions (PDFs) to model the distribution of data and event importance. In discrete contexts, such as the distribution of outcomes from unbiased dice, the PDF represents the frequency of specific integer values, where the probability of non-integer results is zero.[5] In the context of event cameras, this concept is extended through the event-importance probability density function (ePDF), which allows for the arbitrary definition and adaptation of event importance metrics to specific applications.[6]
The efficiency of generating random variables and processing stochastic streams is governed by fundamental limits in information theory. Theoretical work on generating random variables from entropy sources demonstrates that it is possible to achieve an amortized expected entropy cost within $\varepsilon > 0$ bits of the information-theoretically optimal Shannon lower bound.[7] This result holds both pointwise and in expectation, providing a rate of $\mathbb{E}[H(P_1) + \dots + H(P_n)]/n + \varepsilon$ bits per sample as $n \to \infty$, where $H$ denotes Shannon entropy.[7] These theoretical bounds are critical for designing downsampling algorithms that operate within strict computational and memory constraints while preserving the statistical integrity of the data stream.
Furthermore, the analysis of event streams often relies on renewal theory and temporal point processes to characterize the timing and correlation of events. Renewal theory has been applied to analyze traffic patterns, such as in Twitter message streams, to assess levels of correlation and detect periodic traffic or spam.[8] Similarly, temporal point process models are used to characterize information cascades in asynchronous environments, such as online discussion forums, by modeling the intensity functions of nested event streams.[9] These stochastic frameworks provide the mathematical basis for understanding the temporal dynamics of event data, which is essential for developing online algorithms that can estimate event importance on-the-fly from raw streams.[6]
In the domain of scientific machine learning, the challenge of modeling continuous phenomena represented discretely is addressed through machine-learned operators (MLOs). Theoretical evaluations of MLOs focus on their ability to perform zero-shot super-resolution and multi-resolution inference, decoupling this capability into extrapolation to varying frequency information and interpolation across varying resolutions.[10] This theoretical perspective is relevant to event downsampling, as it addresses the general problem of maintaining model performance when inference is required on data with different resolutions or densities than those encountered during training.[10] Additionally, the integration of high-temporal-resolution event data with low-temporal-resolution images, as seen in dynamic 3D reconstruction, relies on the theoretical ability to use event trajectories to provide deterministic constraints for inter-frame motion, thereby addressing the uncertainty inherent in low-framerate video data.[11]

# Core Mechanisms

The core mechanism of Probabilistic Online Event Downsampling (POLED) is a probabilistic framework designed to model event importance through an event-importance probability density function (ePDF). Unlike prior approaches that rely on fixed heuristics or threshold-based strategies, which limit adaptability, POLED allows the ePDF to be arbitrarily defined and adapted to different applications.[6] This framework operates in a purely online setting, estimating event importance on-the-fly from raw event streams to enable scene-specific adaptation.[6]
A key component of this mechanism is the introduction of zero-shot event downsampling. In this mode, the downsampled events are required to remain usable for models that were originally trained on the full, original event stream.[6] This capability addresses the high bandwidth, memory, and computational demands associated with the extremely high temporal resolution of event cameras, which capture scene changes asynchronously on a per-pixel basis.[6] By dynamically filtering events based on their estimated importance rather than static thresholds, the system aims to reduce data volume while preserving the critical information necessary for downstream tasks.[6]

# Implementation

The implementation of probabilistic online event downsampling relies on efficient sampling algorithms designed to handle high-volume data streams in a timely and responsive fashion. A key component of this architecture is the use of online stratified reservoir sampling, which allows systems to produce approximate outputs with rigorous error bounds while processing data in real-time [12]. This approach is particularly suited for stream analytics, where input data is continuously changing, unlike batch analytics systems that operate on static datasets [12]. By computing over a representative sample rather than the entire input dataset, the system can make a systematic trade-off between output accuracy and computation efficiency based on the chosen sample size [12].
To support these operations, distributed stream processing architectures are employed to manage the fundamental tasks of dynamically assigning processing tasks to available physical resources and routing streaming data between them [13]. The theoretical foundation for these systems involves solving task allocation problems, which have been proven to be NP-hard, necessitating the use of approximation algorithms to ensure efficient resource utilization [13]. These platforms enable the generation of heterogeneous processing capabilities, supporting applications such as distributed data compression and network management [14].
In terms of computational efficiency, implementation strategies often incorporate techniques to reduce storage and processing costs. For instance, methods involving the downsampling of high-dimensional data into lower-dimensional representations can significantly lower computational complexity to $O(N + M \log M)$ and storage costs to $O(N)$, where $N$ is the original dimension and $M$ is the downsampled dimension [15]. This is achieved by multiplying the downsampled data with a circulant matrix, a process that preserves similarity and discriminative power, particularly when the data exhibits sparsity [15]. Furthermore, the integration of machine learning models, such as those used in sub-grid-scale modeling for turbulence, demonstrates how retaining only the largest dynamics explicitly while approximating small-scale dynamics can reduce computational effort by orders of magnitude while retaining statistical physical properties [16]. These combined techniques allow for the robust and efficient implementation of probabilistic downsampling in complex, large-scale data environments.

# Applications

Probabilistic online event downsampling techniques are integral to the processing of data from event-based cameras, which are bio-inspired sensors that detect light changes asynchronously for each pixel.[17] These sensors offer significant advantages over traditional frame-based cameras, including high temporal resolution, low latency, and high dynamic range, making them particularly suited for scenarios involving high motion, challenging lighting conditions, and real-time responsiveness.[17][18] The ability to efficiently manage the high data rates generated by these devices through downsampling enables their application in various fields, including computer vision, robotics, and distributed stream processing.[14]
In the domain of computer vision, event cameras are increasingly utilized for tasks such as object detection, where the high temporal precision allows for the capture of fast-moving objects that might be blurred in conventional imaging.[18] However, the performance of event-based systems can be limited by the lower spatial resolution of the sensors and the lack of large-scale training datasets compared to frame-based solutions.[18] Probabilistic downsampling helps mitigate the data volume issues associated with high-frequency event streams, facilitating the training of deep learning architectures and the reconstruction of continuous irradiance dynamics.[18][19] For instance, frameworks like the Event Density Flow Filter (EDFilter) model event generation as threshold-crossing probability fluxes, allowing for nonparametric estimation and real-time processing of event density, which is crucial for maintaining signal integrity while reducing data load.[19]
The utility of event-based sensing extends to robotics and simulation, where the need for high-fidelity data without the cost of extensive hardware setups is paramount.[17][20] Simulating event streams from 3D scenes has become a common practice in research, enabling the generation of large-scale, high-temporal-frequency data for training and testing algorithms.[20] Efficient rendering pipelines, such as EventTracer, use path tracing and lightweight denoising networks to simulate realistic event sequences, a process that benefits from effective data management strategies to handle the high volume of simulated events.[20] Furthermore, the asynchronous nature of event data, which carries state information through polarities and process information through inter-event time intervals, requires specialized filtering and downsampling techniques to avoid producing outputs that are sparser than the raw input, thereby preserving the necessary information for downstream tasks.[19]
Beyond vision, the principles of processing high-volume data streams in a timely and responsive fashion are applicable to distributed stream processing systems.[14] These systems handle data sensed externally and pushed to the network for processing, supporting applications such as distributed data compression, speech recognition, and network management.[14] The theoretical challenges of allocating processing tasks to resources in such environments, which have been proven to be NP-hard, highlight the importance of efficient data handling and downsampling strategies to ensure reliable and timely processing.[13] Additionally, the data-driven approaches used in event processing share similarities with other machine learning applications, such as weather downscaling and predictive maintenance, where efficient processing of large datasets is critical for generating high-resolution outputs or predicting system failures.[21]

# Evaluation Metrics

Evaluating the performance of probabilistic online event downsampling requires metrics that balance data reduction efficiency with the preservation of statistical fidelity and predictive accuracy. In the context of survey data, where downsampling or mode switching (such as moving from face-to-face to online surveys) is common, representativeness is a primary concern. Researchers assess the effects of such methods by comparing marginal distributions against validated sociodemographic and electoral benchmarks. While differences in these marginal distributions may exist, the critical evaluation often focuses on inferential models, such as those for party identification and vote choice, to determine if the downsampled or alternative mode data yields substantially different conclusions compared to the full probabilistic dataset [22].
In scientific computing and machine learning, where data reduction is critical for managing computational intensity, evaluation metrics often focus on the ability of the reduced dataset to maintain the structural properties of the original phase space. Strategies that select data points to uniformly span the phase space are evaluated based on their ability to estimate probability maps accurately. The success of such downsampling is often measured by the accuracy of the constructed acceptance probabilities and the fidelity of the iterative estimation methods used to model the data distribution [23].
For approximate computing systems designed for stream analytics, rigorous error bounds are a standard evaluation metric. Systems like StreamApprox utilize online stratified reservoir sampling to produce approximate outputs, and their performance is evaluated by the systematic trade-off between output accuracy and computation efficiency. The generic nature of these algorithms allows for the application of standard error bound metrics to ensure that the approximate results remain within acceptable limits of the exact computation [12].
In the domain of high-dimensional data embedding, where downsampling is used to reduce dimensionality before further processing, evaluation metrics include computational complexity, storage costs, and similarity preservation. For instance, methods that downsample N-dimensional data into M-dimensional data are evaluated on their computational cost, often expressed as O(N + M log M), and storage requirements, typically O(N). The primary performance metric is the preservation of discriminative power and similarity, particularly when the data exhibits sparsity, ensuring that the downsampled representation remains effective for downstream tasks such as image applications [15].
Finally, in the context of machine-learned operators and time series foundation models, evaluation often involves testing the robustness of models trained on reduced or specific data subsets. Metrics may include the ability to perform zero-shot inference or super-resolution, where the model's capacity to extrapolate to varying frequency information and interpolate across resolutions is assessed. The efficiency of the model, measured by parameter count and computational expense, is also a key metric, with recent work demonstrating that smaller, efficient models can match the performance of larger, more resource-intensive architectures [24][10].

# Challenges and Limitations

The development of probabilistic online event downsampling frameworks, such as POLED, addresses critical bottlenecks in processing high-frequency asynchronous data streams, yet several significant challenges and limitations persist in the field. A primary constraint is the inherent trade-off between temporal resolution and resource efficiency. While event cameras and similar asynchronous sensors offer extremely high temporal resolution, this advantage comes at the cost of high bandwidth, memory, and computational demands [6]. Consequently, data reduction remains a critical component of data-driven methods, particularly in applications constrained by memory and computational intensity, where excessively large datasets can hinder scientific discovery [23].
Existing approaches to event downsampling often rely on fixed heuristics or threshold-based strategies, which limit their adaptability to varying scene conditions [6]. To overcome this, probabilistic frameworks model event importance through probability density functions, allowing for scene-specific adaptation in a purely online setting [6]. However, ensuring that downsampled events remain usable for models trained on the original event stream—referred to as zero-shot event downsampling—presents a significant methodological challenge [6]. The system must estimate event importance on-the-fly from raw streams without prior knowledge of the specific application context, requiring robust generalization capabilities [6].
Furthermore, the integration of such downsampling techniques into broader predictive and analytical systems introduces additional complexities. In domains such as automotive diagnostics, where electronic control units generate large numbers of asynchronous events (diagnostic trouble codes), the manual grouping of these codes into higher-level error patterns is increasingly costly and error-prone as vehicle complexity grows [25]. The volume of unique events can reach the order of magnitude of natural language vocabulary, necessitating automated, scalable modeling approaches [25]. Similarly, in paleoseismology, constraining the timing of past events to build accurate chronologies is difficult due to uncertainties in dating and the need to integrate multi-site records [26]. Probabilistic modeling helps reduce these uncertainties, but the reliance on unconditioned correlations and the handling of poorly dated events remain ongoing methodological hurdles [26].
Finally, the dynamic nature of event streams poses challenges for maintaining model accuracy over time. Supervised learning models must adapt to evolving data, and while temporally-biased sampling schemes can help by weighting recent data more heavily, they must balance adaptation to recent changes with the retention of older data to ensure robustness against temporary fluctuations [1]. In the context of event-based vision, the performance gap between event-based systems and conventional frame-based solutions is also attributed to the lower spatial resolution of event sensors and the lack of large-scale training datasets and well-established deep learning architectures [18]. These factors collectively limit the current applicability and performance of probabilistic online event downsampling in high-stakes or high-precision domains.
