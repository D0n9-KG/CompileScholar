# Methodology

The methodology for enhancing claim verification via pseudo-subgraph generation with lightweight models integrates principles from graph-based reasoning, structured data serialization, and logical verification to address the challenges of fact-checking in large language model (LLM) outputs. A primary challenge in this domain is the high cost of transmitting real-time or extensive data to LLMs due to token-based pricing models. To mitigate this, the approach utilizes specialized data serialization formats such as TimeSeries Lean Notation (TSLN), which employs schema-first architecture, relative timestamps, and differential encoding to achieve 68–73% token reduction compared to standard JSON formats while maintaining lossless fidelity [1]. This efficiency is critical for processing large volumes of claims and source documents without prohibitive API costs.
Central to the verification process is the decomposition of generated claims and source documents into atomic claims, which are minimal, self-contained factual units. This decomposition allows for structured contrastive reasoning over each unit independently, a technique exemplified by frameworks like Atomic Contrastive Verification (ACV) [2]. By extracting knowledge graph triples from these atomic claims, the system can generate contrastive claim variants through a multi-type perturbation taxonomy that covers entity, relation, temporal, and quantitative dimensions [2]. This graph-grounded approach enables the detection of subtle inconsistencies, such as entity substitutions or relational misattributions, that traditional text-based methods may miss.
To ensure the robustness of the verification logic, the methodology incorporates formal verification techniques derived from workflow and process modeling. Similar to logic-based workflow verification approaches that apply propositional logic with constraints to detect anomalies in acyclic and cyclic models [3], the claim verification system treats the relationships between claims as a directed graph. This allows for the detection of structural incoherence within authorization or factual claim bundles. For instance, mechanisms like Claim Graph Coherence (CGC) build a directed graph of typed claims and permit execution or acceptance only if a structurally coherent subset of claims covers the proposed action, ensuring that individually valid claims compose into a coherent whole [4].
Furthermore, the methodology addresses the gap between unstructured textual claims and structured data required for verification. Systems such as Scrutinizer translate textual claims into structured queries (e.g., SQL) to facilitate automated fact-checking against database sources [5]. In the context of entity resolution within these claims, frameworks like Relation Extraction for Entity Linking (REEL) utilize automatically extracted relations to build disambiguation graphs, where nodes represent ontology candidates and edges represent relations established in the text [6]. This enhances the precision of entity linking, particularly in sparse disambiguation graphs where traditional Personalized PageRank algorithms may underperform [6]. By combining lightweight serialization, atomic graph decomposition, and formal logical coherence checks, the proposed methodology provides a scalable and accurate framework for verifying complex relational data in industrial and academic contexts [7].

# Experimental Setup

The experimental evaluation of the ClaimPKG framework focuses on assessing the efficacy of pseudo-subgraph generation in enhancing claim verification, particularly in scenarios involving complex relational data and large language model (LLM) interactions. The study employs a structured synthesis methodology, integrating findings from industrial implementations and peer-reviewed academic studies to validate the approach's performance across diverse domains [7].
A primary component of the experimental setup involves the integration of LLMs with real-time data streams, addressing the economic challenges associated with token-based pricing models. To optimize this integration, the experiments utilize TSLN (TimeSeries Lean Notation), a specialized data serialization format that achieves 68–73% token reduction compared to standard JSON formats through schema-first architecture, relative timestamps, and differential encoding [1]. Benchmarks conducted across cryptocurrency, stock market, and IoT datasets demonstrate that this compression strategy maintains lossless fidelity while enabling significant cost savings, with annual LLM API cost reductions exceeding $18,000 for applications processing one million records daily [1].
The verification process itself relies on graph-grounded fact-checking mechanisms. The setup incorporates Atomic Contrastive Verification (ACV), a training-free framework that decomposes generated claims and source documents into atomic claims—minimal, self-contained factual units [2]. For each atomic claim, the system extracts knowledge graph triples and generates contrastive claim variants using a multi-type perturbation taxonomy that covers entity, relation, temporal, and quantitative dimensions [2]. This structured contrastive reasoning allows for the detection of subtle inconsistencies, such as entity substitutions or temporal distortions, that are often embedded within lengthy LLM outputs [2].
Furthermore, the experimental design addresses the challenge of bridging the gap between unstructured textual claims and structured verification data. Drawing from methodologies such as Scrutinizer, the setup includes components that translate textual claims into structured queries, such as SQL, to facilitate interaction with underlying data sources [5]. This approach is critical for verifying claims against structured databases, ensuring that the verification process is not limited to surface-level text matching but extends to logical consistency within the data structure [5].
In the context of entity and relation extraction, which underpins the construction of the pseudo-subgraphs, the experiments evaluate the generalization capabilities of state-of-the-art models to unknown facts. Traditional benchmarks often suffer from lexical overlap, which can mask the true understanding capabilities of models; therefore, the setup includes tests designed to measure performance on non-lexicalized or novel entities and relations [8]. Additionally, the framework incorporates Named Entity Linking (NEL) techniques, specifically utilizing Relation Extraction for Entity Linking (REEL), to build disambiguation graphs where nodes represent ontology candidates and edges are defined by relations established in the text [6]. This method is particularly tested in scenarios where disambiguation graphs are sparse, a condition where traditional Personalized PageRank (PPR) based models typically exhibit low performance [6].
Finally, the safety and authorization aspects of the verification pipeline are evaluated using Claim Graph Coherence (CGC). This deterministic, fail-closed mechanism builds a directed graph of typed authorization claims to ensure that individually valid claims compose into a coherent authorization for the specific action requested [4]. The experimental setup verifies that CGC can detect structural incoherence in authorization claim bundles prior to execution, ensuring that the verification process adheres to strict safety protocols without relying on implicit intent detection [4].

# Results and Analysis

The integration of graph-based methodologies into claim verification and related natural language processing tasks has yielded significant improvements in accuracy, efficiency, and structural coherence. Recent studies demonstrate that leveraging graph structures allows for the precise decomposition of complex information, addressing limitations inherent in traditional machine learning approaches that struggle with relational dependencies.
In the domain of fact-checking, the Atomic Contrastive Verification (ACV) framework has shown that decomposing generated claims and source documents into atomic claims—minimal, self-contained factual units—enables structured contrastive reasoning. By extracting knowledge graph triples and generating contrastive variants through a multi-type perturbation taxonomy covering entity, relation, temporal, and quantitative dimensions, ACV effectively detects subtle inconsistencies such as entity substitutions and temporal distortions that are often missed by standard models [2]. This graph-grounded approach highlights the importance of structural analysis in identifying factual errors within lengthy outputs.
Furthermore, the application of graph structures to Named Entity Linking (NEL) has addressed performance bottlenecks in sparse disambiguation graphs. The Relation Extraction for Entity Linking (REEL) framework utilizes automatically extracted relations to build disambiguation graphs where edges are defined by textual relations rather than relying solely on ontology candidates. This method overcomes the low performance of Personalized PageRank (PPR) models in sparse environments, thereby enhancing the curation of digital libraries and improving the resolution of entity ambiguity [6]. Similarly, research into the generalization of pre-trained language models in Named Entity and Relation Extraction reveals that while these models achieve high benchmark scores, their capacity to generalize to unknown facts remains limited, indicating that structural understanding is a critical factor in performance that standard datasets may not fully capture [8].
Efficiency gains are also evident in the serialization of data for Large Language Models (LLMs). The TimeSeries Lean Notation (TSLN) format, which employs schema-first architecture and differential encoding, achieves a 68–73% reduction in token usage compared to JSON. This compression maintains lossless fidelity while significantly reducing API costs, with annual savings exceeding $18,000 for applications processing one million records daily [1]. This demonstrates that optimized data representation is crucial for the economic viability of LLM-based verification systems.
In the context of autonomous AI agents, the Claim Graph Coherence (CGC) mechanism introduces a deterministic, fail-closed pre-execution check for authorization. By building a directed graph of typed authorization claims, CGC ensures that only structurally coherent subsets of claims cover the proposed action, thereby detecting structural incoherence before execution [4]. This approach complements broader efforts to bridge the gap between unstructured text and structured data, such as the Scrutinizer system, which translates textual claims into SQL queries to facilitate verification against structured databases [5].
Finally, the accessibility of knowledge graphs for non-technical users has been improved through the development of natural language interfaces for property-graph databases. These systems address the challenges of morphological complexity and semantic mapping, particularly in mid-resource languages like Greek, by providing a unified theoretical framework for querying complex domain information without the need for specialized query languages like Cypher or SPARQL [9]. Collectively, these results underscore the role of graph neural networks and graph-based reasoning in enhancing operational efficiency, prediction accuracy, and decision-making processes across various industrial and academic applications [7].

# Discussion

The integration of graph-based methodologies into natural language processing and verification tasks has emerged as a critical area of research, addressing the limitations of traditional machine learning approaches in capturing structural dependencies within complex data. Recent studies highlight that as modern systems increasingly rely on interconnected entities, such as suppliers, machines, or infrastructure components, the ability to model these relational structures is essential for enhancing operational efficiency and decision-making processes [7]. This structural focus is particularly relevant in the domain of fact-checking, where large language models (LLMs) frequently produce text that is fluent yet factually inconsistent with source documents [2]. Detecting such inconsistencies remains challenging, especially when errors involve subtle entity substitutions, temporal distortions, or relational misattributions embedded within lengthy outputs [2].
To address these challenges, various frameworks have been proposed that leverage graph structures to improve verification accuracy. For instance, Atomic Contrastive Verification (ACV) introduces a training-free, graph-grounded fact-checking framework that decomposes generated claims and source documents into atomic claims, which are minimal, self-contained factual units [2]. By extracting knowledge graph triples and performing structured contrastive reasoning over each unit, ACV aims to identify inconsistencies that might be overlooked by linear processing methods [2]. Similarly, in the context of authorization and safety-critical systems, the Claim Graph Coherence (CGC) mechanism builds a directed graph of typed authorization claims to ensure that a structurally coherent subset of claims covers a proposed action before execution [4]. This approach underscores the utility of graph coherence in verifying the validity of complex, multi-part assertions, a principle that can be extended to broader claim verification tasks.
The application of graph neural networks (GNNs) further supports the argument for structural modeling in verification tasks. GNNs have demonstrated consistent performance gains in industrial implementations, including improved prediction accuracy and enhanced anomaly detection, by effectively modeling complex relational data [7]. These capabilities are particularly advantageous in scenarios where disambiguation graphs may be sparse, a limitation that has affected other state-of-the-art approaches like those based on the Personalized PageRank algorithm [6]. By utilizing automatically extracted relations to build robust disambiguation graphs, frameworks such as Relation Extraction for Entity Linking (REEL) overcome these sparsity issues, suggesting that similar strategies could enhance the robustness of claim verification systems [6].
However, the reliance on pre-trained models and standard benchmarks presents its own set of challenges. While the transfer of pre-trained language models has achieved unprecedented performance on standard benchmarks, their underlying comprehension capabilities remain limited, revealing the constraints of current reference datasets in measuring true understanding [8]. This gap suggests that while graph-based methods offer significant improvements in handling relational data and verifying coherence, further research is needed to ensure that these systems can generalize to unknown facts and maintain high fidelity in diverse contexts. The economic implications of these technologies are also notable; for example, specialized data serialization formats like TSLN have shown that optimizing data transmission for LLMs can result in substantial cost savings, highlighting the importance of efficiency in large-scale verification and processing pipelines [1]. In summary, the convergence of graph theory, neural networks, and structured reasoning provides a promising pathway for enhancing claim verification, though continued refinement is required to address issues of generalization and computational efficiency.
