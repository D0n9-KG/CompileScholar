# summary

**COSMIC Generalized Refusal Direction Identification in LLM Activations** is a framework designed to identify the internal activation directions responsible for refusal behavior in Large Language Models (LLMs) without relying on predefined refusal templates or specific output tokens.[1] As LLMs are increasingly deployed in real-world applications, ensuring alignment with human intentions and safety regulations has become a critical priority, yet models remain susceptible to adversarial "jailbreak" attacks that exploit failure modes in safety training.[2][3] Traditional methods for identifying behavioral features often assume linear encoding or rely on computationally expensive subspace extraction techniques, which are prohibitive for reasoning models that generate long traces.[4] COSMIC addresses these limitations by utilizing cosine similarity metrics independent of model outputs to locate viable steering directions and target layers, enabling robust behavioral control through activation steering.[1]
The methodology behind COSMIC represents a shift from early assumptions that refusal behaviors are encoded along single linear directions to the recognition that such behaviors reside in multi-dimensional subspaces.[4] While recent advancements like the Recursive Feature Machine (RFM) have improved the efficiency of identifying these subspaces, COSMIC distinguishes itself by eliminating the need for assumptions about a model’s specific linguistic markers of refusal.[4][1] This generalizable approach allows for the reliable identification of refusal directions in both adversarial settings and weakly aligned models, where traditional methods may struggle due to the absence of clear refusal signals in the output space.[1] By decoupling direction identification from specific output tokens, the framework facilitates faster and more accurate subspace identification across various model architectures, including reasoning models like Qwen 3 and non-reasoning models like Qwen 2.5.[4]
Experimental results demonstrate that COSMIC achieves steering performance comparable to prior methods while significantly reducing dependency on manual analysis or specific linguistic markers.[1] The framework is capable of steering models toward safer behavior with a minimal increase in false refusals, highlighting its robustness in scenarios where safety training has failed to generalize to domains where capabilities exist.[3][1] This capability is particularly notable because it allows for targeted interventions that can reopen attack success or suppress refusal behavior for research purposes, without the coherency degradation often associated with indiscriminate intervention on the residual stream.[5][6] Furthermore, the identification of these directions supports the development of more precise control techniques, such as manipulating sparse subsets of attention heads known as Style Modulation Heads, which independently govern persona and style formation.[6]
The identification of generalized refusal directions has significant implications for the development of "Glass Box" AI systems that are observable, secure, and commercially viable.[7] By isolating the specific internal representations responsible for refusal, researchers can better understand the mechanisms of safety training and develop robust control techniques that mitigate vulnerabilities such as mismatched generalization.[3][7] However, challenges remain in extending these techniques to Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs), where the reliance on unsafe multimodal data for alignment is difficult to collect and cross-modal alignment can introduce failure modes that spuriously steer safe inputs toward refusal.[8] Additionally, the evaluation of these safety mechanisms is complicated by structurally flawed metrics that often fail to distinguish between appropriate safety refusals and degenerate policies that refuse all inputs, underscoring the need for reference-free, severity-weighted scoring systems like the Weighted Safety Refusal (WSR) framework.[9]

# Background

The deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) in real-world applications has necessitated a critical focus on alignment, ensuring that model outputs adhere to human intentions, social norms, and safety regulations.[2] A central component of this alignment is the model's ability to refuse harmful requests while maintaining the capacity to answer benign ones.[5] Despite extensive efforts, including the iterative alignment processes employed by major developers, LLMs remain susceptible to adversarial misuse, such as "jailbreak" attacks that elicit undesired behavior.[3] These vulnerabilities often stem from failure modes in safety training, including competing objectives between capabilities and safety goals, and mismatched generalization where safety training fails to extend to domains where capabilities exist.[3]
To address these challenges, researchers have increasingly turned to white-box control techniques, particularly activation steering, which modifies internal model activations to elicit abstract changes in behavior.[10] This approach serves as a standard tool in both interpretability research, such as probing truthfulness, and safety research, including the assessment of jailbreakability.[10] Early assumptions in this field posited that behaviors were encoded along single linear directions; however, recent findings indicate that complex behaviors, such as the refusal to answer harmful queries, reside in multi-dimensional subspaces.[4] Identifying and manipulating these subspaces is crucial for robust behavioral control, yet existing methods for extracting them are often computationally expensive, a limitation that becomes prohibitive for reasoning models that generate long traces.[4]
Recent advancements have sought to optimize the identification of these behavioral features. For instance, the Recursive Feature Machine (RFM) algorithm, when adapted with probe-informed initialization, allows for the identification of multi-dimensional refusal subspaces in seconds, outperforming alternative methods in ablation tasks.[4] Similarly, activation steering offers a computationally efficient mechanism for controlling LLMs without fine-tuning, though it faces obstacles such as coherency degradation caused by indiscriminate intervention on the residual stream.[6] To mitigate this, researchers have identified sparse subsets of attention heads, termed Style Modulation Heads, that independently govern persona and style formation, enabling more targeted and robust behavioral control.[6]
Furthermore, the interpretation of these steering mechanisms remains an active area of investigation. Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) have been proposed as a method to uncover structured, human-interpretable representations within LLMs, achieving high performance in safety-critical classification tasks and demonstrating cross-model transferability.[11] However, directly applying SAEs to steering vectors can yield misleading decompositions because steering vectors often fall outside the input distribution for which SAEs are designed and may contain meaningful negative projections in feature directions.[12] Consequently, understanding the precise geometric and structural nature of refusal directions in LLM activations is essential for developing reliable, observable, and secure AI systems.[7]

# Methodology

The identification of refusal behaviors in Large Language Models (LLMs) has evolved from early assumptions of linear encoding to more complex, multi-dimensional subspace models. Initial research posited that specific behaviors, such as the refusal to answer harmful queries, were encoded along single linear directions within the model's activation space [4]. However, recent findings indicate that these behaviors reside in multi-dimensional subspaces, necessitating more sophisticated extraction methods [4]. A primary challenge in this domain is the computational expense of existing subspace extraction techniques, which becomes prohibitive when applied to reasoning models that generate long reasoning traces [4].
To address these computational constraints, researchers have adapted the Recursive Feature Machine (RFM) algorithm, which allows for efficient computation, combined with a probe-informed initialization [4]. This approach enables the identification of the multi-dimensional refusal subspace in seconds, demonstrating efficacy on both reasoning models (such as Qwen 3) and non-reasoning models (such as Qwen 2.5) [4]. The RFM-based method has shown superior performance in ablation tasks compared to alternative methods, facilitating faster and more accurate subspace identification [4].
Complementary methodologies focus on the generation of contrastive data pairs required for activation steering and interpretability. Traditional approaches often rely on simple binary pairs of samples exhibiting desired or undesired behaviors [13]. However, to improve the disentanglement of features, iterative data generation pipelines have been developed to isolate cascading linear features responsible for specific behaviors [13]. By moving beyond binary pairs to isolate samples that exhibit degrees of features scaling linearly with behavior, these methods enhance the reliability of interpretability frameworks in detecting model features [13]. This technique has been specifically applied to detect and steer away from sycophancy, the tendency of language models to prioritize user validation [13].
Furthermore, the evaluation of refusal behaviors requires robust metrics that avoid structural biases present in traditional safety scores. Existing metrics often suffer from issues such as flat averaging, which treats severe data-exfiltration bypasses identically to minor rudeness, or relative scoring that drifts with reference cohort changes [9]. To mitigate these issues, the Weighted Safety Refusal (WSR) framework has been introduced, offering a reference-free, severity-weighted, and dual-axis scoring system [9]. This framework jointly scores adversarial refusal and benign compliance, providing a gaming-resistance guarantee that ensures degenerate policies, such as refusing all queries, receive a score of zero [9]. These methodological advancements collectively aim to provide a more accurate and computationally efficient understanding of refusal mechanisms in LLM activations.

# Experimental Results

Experimental evaluations of the COSMIC framework demonstrate its efficacy in identifying viable steering directions and target layers for refusal behavior in Large Language Models (LLMs) without relying on predefined refusal templates or specific output tokens.[1] By utilizing cosine similarity metrics entirely independent of model outputs, COSMIC achieves steering performance comparable to prior methods while eliminating the need for assumptions about a model's specific refusal behavior, such as the presence of specific refusal tokens.[1]
The framework has been shown to reliably identify refusal directions in adversarial settings and within weakly aligned models.[1] In these contexts, COSMIC is capable of steering models toward safer behavior with a minimal increase in false refusals, highlighting its robustness in scenarios where traditional methods may struggle due to the absence of clear refusal signals in the output space.[1] These results suggest that COSMIC provides a generalizable approach to direction selection that maintains high steering accuracy while reducing the dependency on manual analysis or specific linguistic markers of refusal.[1]

# Applications

The identification of generalized refusal directions in Large Language Model (LLM) activations has significant implications for safety alignment, interpretability, and the development of controllable AI systems. By isolating the specific internal representations responsible for refusal behavior, researchers can better understand the mechanisms of safety training and develop more robust control techniques.

## Safety Alignment and Robustness

Understanding the geometry of refusal mechanisms is critical for enhancing the robustness of safety-aligned models. Recent studies utilizing dual safety-geometry protocols have demonstrated that refusal carriers and harmfulness carriers are nearly orthogonal, indicating that models learn to recognize harmfulness and activate refusal policies as distinct processes.[5] This separation allows for targeted interventions; for instance, refusal-side interventions have been shown to reopen attack success more strongly than harmfulness-only interventions, highlighting the specific vulnerability of the refusal mechanism.[5] Furthermore, analyzing these directions helps identify failure modes in safety training, such as "mismatched generalization," where safety training fails to generalize to domains where capabilities exist, leading to persistent vulnerabilities against jailbreak attacks.[3]

## Activation Steering and Control

The identification of refusal directions facilitates the use of activation steering, a white-box control technique that modifies model activations to elicit specific behavioral changes without fine-tuning.[10] Steering vectors can be applied to dense LLMs during inference to suppress refusal behavior, effectively inducing responses to harmful requests for research purposes.[14] This technique has been extended to Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, where it was found that steering performance is not inhibited by complex routing patterns.[14] Notably, a single expert in an MoE model can recover a significant portion of the full steering effect, suggesting that refusal mechanisms may be localized within specific components of the model architecture.[14] However, the application of steering vectors raises questions about the realizability of steered behaviors; theoretical and empirical evidence suggests that activation steering pushes the residual stream off the manifold of states reachable from discrete prompts, meaning that the induced behavior may not be reproducible by any natural textual prompt.[10]

## Interpretability and Feature Extraction

Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) are increasingly used to uncover structured, human-interpretable representations in LLMs, which is crucial for transparent AI systems.[11] SAE-derived features have been shown to achieve high performance in safety-critical classification tasks, outperforming hidden-state and bag-of-words baselines.[11] These features demonstrate cross-model transferability and can generalize in a zero-shot manner to tasks such as cross-lingual toxicity detection.[11] However, the direct application of SAEs to interpret steering vectors presents challenges. Research indicates that SAE-reconstructed vectors often lack the steering properties of the original vectors because steering vectors fall outside the input distribution for which SAEs are designed and may have meaningful negative projections in feature directions.[12] Despite these limitations, SAEs remain a vital tool for analyzing the sparse subsets of attention heads, such as "Style Modulation Heads," that govern specific behavioral traits, offering a pathway to more precise and coherent model control.[6]

## Deterministic and Auditable Systems

The ability to identify and manipulate specific activation directions contributes to the development of "Glass Box" AI systems that are observable, secure, and commercially viable.[7] In high-stakes enterprise environments, the inherent stochasticity of LLMs limits their utility; therefore, transforming probabilistic natural language intent into deterministic software artifacts is a key application of advanced interpretability techniques.[7] Frameworks such as the MFOUR Vibe Framework (MVF) utilize quantitative metrics like the Vibe Integrity Score (VIS) to evaluate the structural adherence of generative outputs, ensuring that AI systems meet the determinism and auditability requirements of critical applications.[7]

# Limitations

While methods for identifying and utilizing refusal directions in Large Language Model (LLM) activations offer a promising avenue for enhancing safety, several significant limitations persist across different approaches. A primary challenge in extending these techniques to Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) is the reliance on unsafe multimodal data for post-training alignment or direct extraction of refusal directions, which is significantly harder to collect than unimodal text data.[8] Even when attempting to generalize textual refusal directions from the LLM backbone to other modalities such as image and video, the effectiveness of this transfer is conditioned on specific factors including layer selection, steering strength, and cross-modal alignment.[8] Notably, cross-modal alignment can introduce a critical failure mode where safe multimodal inputs are spuriously steered toward refusal, thereby degrading model utility for benign queries.[8]
Furthermore, the robustness of safety training remains vulnerable to adversarial misuse, as evidenced by the prevalence of "jailbreak" attacks that elicit undesired behavior from models like GPT-4 and Claude v1.3.[3] These vulnerabilities stem from two hypothesized failure modes: competing objectives, where the model's capabilities conflict with its safety goals, and mismatched generalization, where safety training fails to generalize to domains for which the model possesses capabilities.[3] This indicates that simply identifying refusal directions may not be sufficient to prevent sophisticated attacks that exploit these structural weaknesses in safety alignment.
Finally, the evaluation of these safety mechanisms is hindered by structurally flawed metrics. Existing safety scores often rely on flat averaging, which treats severe breaches like data-exfiltration bypasses identically to minor issues like rude sentences, or on relative scoring that drifts when the reference cohort changes.[9] Additionally, refusal-only metrics can reward degenerate policies where the model refuses all inputs, failing to distinguish between appropriate safety refusals and a lack of compliance with benign requests.[9] These metric limitations complicate the accurate assessment of how effectively generalized refusal directions improve safety without compromising model functionality.
