# Related Work

### In-Context Learning and the Role of Demonstrations

In-context learning (ICL) — the ability of large language models (LLMs) to adapt to new tasks from a handful of exemplars placed in the prompt — was first characterized in GPT-3 [Brown et al., 2020] and has since become a dominant paradigm for few-shot NLP. A growing body of work shows that ICL performance is highly sensitive not only to *how many* demonstrations are supplied but to *which* ones are used: early studies found that label balance among demonstrations improves accuracy [Liu et al., 2021], logit calibration compensates for imbalanced demonstration labels [Zhao et al., 2021], and a large-scale analysis concluded that task format and label quality often matter more than the specific examples selected [Min et al., 2022]. Subsequent demonstration-selection methods pick examples by input similarity to the query [Dong et al., 2023], by learned latent representations [Cheng et al., 2023], or by explicitly accounting for the strong recency bias [Chen et al., 2023] and positional effects in long contexts [Liu et al., 2024]. Mechanistically, ICL has been explained as implicit Bayesian inference over label–input associations [Li et al., 2023] and as in-context optimization by gradient descent [von Oswald et al., 2023]; both views imply that a single mislabeled demonstration directly biases the posterior the model recovers from the prompt, which motivates treating noisy demonstrations as a first-order problem. Most of this line of work, however, assumes demonstrations are correctly labeled and addresses *selection* rather than *verification*.

### Noisy Label Learning and Dataset Quality Estimation

The study of label noise predates LLMs. Classical results show that standard cross-entropy training is fragile to corrupted labels [Natarajan et al., 2013; Goldstein et al., 2016], motivating robust loss functions [Zhang et al., 2019] and divide-and-conquer strategies that remain stable under high noise ratios [Li et al., 2020]; a survey consolidates this line of work [Han et al., 2020]. A complementary, dataset-level perspective asks *which* examples are mislabeled rather than how to train through noise: confident learning estimates per-sample flip probabilities from the disagreement between model predictions and observed labels [Northcutt et al., 2021], and the Cleanlab toolkit operationalizes this for large-scale dataset auditing [Jethani et al., 2023]. In NLP, annotation error has been studied through estimation of human labeler accuracy [Kiritchenko et al., 2014], audits of benchmark annotation quality [Röttger et al., 2020], and dataset cartography, which maps each sample by training confidence and variability to expose low-reliability regions [Swayamdipta et al., 2020]. These efforts belong to the broader data-centric AI movement, which treats the dataset itself as a first-class object of optimization [Zha et al., 2023]. While effective, such methods typically rely on model *predictions* (or training dynamics) rather than the language-model likelihood that naturally arises when an LLM processes raw text, and they are not designed to yield an absolute, per-sample quality score that is invariant to the overall noise level of the corpus.

### Perplexity-Based Data Selection and Its Biases

Because language models are trained to maximize text likelihood, the (local) perplexity of a sample under a model is a natural data-quality signal — a practice with roots in the original definition of perplexity as a measure of predictive uncertainty [Jelinek et al., 1977]. In pretraining data selection, perplexity is used to rank candidate tasks for pretraining [Mahabadi et al., 2020]; neural data pruning compares perplexities of a large and a small model to isolate high-value documents, while documenting that the choice of the scoring model strongly affects what gets pruned [AlKhamissi et al., 2023]; DataComp-LM incorporates perplexity-based filters in a large-scale data benchmark and shows that the behavior of model-based filters depends on the scoring model and its domain alignment [Li et al., 2024]; related heuristics appear in data-constrained scaling [Muennighoff et al., 2023] and instruction-data curation [Wettig et al., 2024]. The shared implicit assumption is that low perplexity under a reference model indicates a clean, high-quality sample. Two confounders threaten this assumption, and both are specific to *annotation* noise detection. First, **the annotation itself**: when the demonstrated label is wrong, text and label form an inconsistent pair, and the model's likelihood reflects that inconsistency rather than the intrinsic quality of the text. Second, **domain-specific knowledge**: a strong LLM assigns uniformly low perplexity to text from domains it has internalized and high perplexity to unfamiliar text, so perplexity conflates annotation quality with the model's prior familiarity — consistent with evidence that LLMs possess calibrated self-knowledge about what they know [Kadavath et al., 2022]. Data-valuation work offers a partial remedy by estimating per-sample utility *relative to a target* via influence functions and gradient-based attribution [Koh & Liang, 2017; Pruthi et al., 2020; Chen, 2021], but such scores are task-relative, expensive to compute, and are not typically used for annotation-noise detection in demonstration sets.

### Synthetic Data and Neighbor-Based Quality Assessment

A separate line of work uses model-generated data to improve the quality of training and demonstration sets. GPT-4's pretraining relied heavily on synthetic, textbook-quality text [Achiam et al., 2023]; Self-Instruct bootstraps instruction data from a seed model and filters the generated triples with a quality classifier [Wang et al., 2023]; and Quality Tuning generates synthetic instructions with a cheaper model, retaining only those judged high quality by a stronger model before instruction tuning [Liang et al., 2023]. The common pattern is to use synthetic, controlled inputs as a reference frame against which the quality of real samples is judged; pseudo-labeling [Lee et al., 2013] shares the same spirit at the instance level. Our work brings this neighbor-based comparison to annotation-noise detection: rather than filtering synthetic data, we synthesize *neighbors around each real demonstration* in order to explicitly remove the two confounders above from its perplexity estimate.

### In-Context Learning under Noisy Demonstrations

Recent analyses document the degradation of ICL when demonstrations contain errors, typically as one factor in the demonstration-selection process [Min et al., 2022; Dong et al., 2023]. These studies, however, are conducted mostly at low noise rates, and their signals — including heuristics that rank samples by local perplexity on the premise that *noisy samples yield higher perplexity than clean ones* — are relative to the corpus and therefore drift as the overall noise level changes. This premise inherits the "low perplexity ⇒ high quality" assumption from data-pruning pipelines [Mahabadi et al., 2020; AlKhamissi et al., 2023; Li et al., 2024], where it was never established for annotation-noise detection, and it is precisely the assumption that breaks down when a large fraction of demonstrations are flawed. We address this gap directly: we identify the two sources of perplexity bias (the annotation itself and the LLM's domain-specific knowledge), propose a dual debiasing framework that uses synthesized neighbors to correct perplexity estimates, and derive a Sample Cleanliness Score that measures *absolute* sample cleanliness regardless of the corpus-level noise ratio. Using this score to curate noisy demonstration sets yields ICL performance comparable to a fully clean corpus, including under extremely high noise levels.
