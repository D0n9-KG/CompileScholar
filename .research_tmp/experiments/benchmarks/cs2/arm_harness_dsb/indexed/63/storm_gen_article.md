# Related Works

## Probability Calibration

Accurate probabilistic predictions have long been a first-class concern in statistical learning, because many downstream tasks depend not merely on correct orderings but on reliable absolute scores. Early work introduced logistic (Platt) scaling to convert classifier margins into probabilities [1], and a subsequent study surveyed a broad family of post-hoc estimators—including isotonic regression, histogram binning, and temperature-like transformations—and compared their behavior [2]. Calibration later became an explicit diagnostic for modern deep networks: Guo et al. showed that large, high-accuracy neural models are frequently miscalibrated, and proposed temperature scaling as a simple one-parameter post-hoc remedy [3]. Parameterized calibrators such as beta calibration [4] and its multi-class Dirichlet extension [5] fit flexible density families directly to the score distribution, while generalized temperature scaling relaxes the single-temperature assumption to a more expressive, sample-dependent mapping [6]. Binning-based approaches such as Bayesian binning [7] and a wide empirical analysis of how different architectures and training objectives affect calibration [8] further map this design space. A recurring theme across these methods is the use of *predefined functional forms*—a sigmoid, a temperature, a beta/Dirichlet density, or a piecewise map. Such forms are attractive because they preserve the order of the original predictions and are easy to fit, but they also pin the calibrator to a fixed parametric shape. Isotonic regression fits the most general non-decreasing (hence order-preserving) mapping [9], yet it is realized as a non-smooth, piecewise monotone step function and can overfit in the tails, producing a discontinuous and poorly conditioned transform. Our work departs from this fixed-form view by learning the monotonic calibrator with a neural network that can represent *arbitrary* monotone mappings, rather than choosing a particular closed form in advance.

## Order-Based Ranking and the Limits of Ranking Objectives

A large body of work evaluates and optimizes ranking quality through order-based criteria rather than absolute scores. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) is the canonical rank-based measure, with its interpretation and analysis established in [10, 11]. Learning-to-rank methods make the order objective explicit: pairwise ranking losses [12], listwise formulations such as LambdaRank [13], and clickthrough-driven optimization [14] all aim to get the *relative* ordering of items right. This emphasis on order is well justified for retrieval and re-ranking, but it comes at a cost: ranking losses are generally indifferent to the absolute values of the scores as long as the order is preserved, so a model can approach perfect AUC while emitting badly miscalibrated probabilities. The distinction is formalized by proper scoring rules such as the Brier score [15] and the theory of strictly proper scoring rules [16], which reward both sharpness and calibration and, unlike ranking losses, penalize miscalibration even when the order is correct. The central tension our paper addresses is precisely this: ranking models optimize the relative order, whereas downstream tasks require calibrated absolute values. We treat the ranker's output as a fixed, order-rich signal and focus exclusively on restoring absolute accuracy through a monotonic calibration layer, thereby decoupling the ranking objective from the calibration objective.

## Calibration in Ranking and Recommendation Systems

In industrial ranking and recommendation systems, the absolute calibration of predicted scores often matters directly for business objectives. A prominent example is sponsored search and advertising, where items are scored by a product of predicted click-through rate (CTR) and bid and the auction is run under a generalized second-price (GSP) rule [17, 18]; there, a miscalibrated CTR systematically distorts expected revenue and thus both the allocation and the payments of the auction. More broadly, modern industrial rankers are deep models such as Wide & Deep [19], DeepFM [20], and the Deep Interest Network [21], whose raw outputs are order-optimized but not directly interpretable as probabilities. Production pipelines therefore typically apply a post-hoc calibration stage—most commonly Platt scaling or isotonic regression—to convert these scores into usable probabilities for downstream decisions such as bidding, expected-value scoring, and thresholding. The fixed forms of these standard calibrators, however, are a poor fit for the complex and often multi-modal score distributions produced by deep rankers: a single sigmoid or temperature can underfit, while isotonic regression can introduce spurious steps. Our monotonic neural calibrator targets this gap: it is expressive enough to fit the true score-to-probability relationship of a deep ranker, yet monotonic so that it never inverts the ranker's ordering, making it a drop-in calibration layer for production ranking stacks. We validate this both offline and through deployment in a large-scale online video ranking system, where calibration improvements translate into business-metric gains.

## Monotonic Neural Networks

Monotonicity can be enforced in neural networks in two contrasting ways. The first, *constrained* monotonicity, restricts the parameters—e.g., to non-negative values along monotonic paths—so that the network is monotone by construction; this line of work dates back to early monotonic networks [22] and was later developed as a general module for monotonic and shape-constrained deep models [23]. Such parameter constraints are simple, but they can reduce expressiveness and complicate optimization, because the network cannot freely use negative interactions to fit the data. The second, *unconstrained* monotonicity, leaves the parameters free and instead guarantees monotonicity through the network's structure—most notably by representing the output as a cumulative distribution function (CDF) over the input, which is non-decreasing by definition regardless of the learned parameters [24]. This structural approach yields arbitrary monotone functions while retaining the full modeling power of an unconstrained network. Our Unconstrained Monotonic Neural Network (UMNN) follows this structural philosophy: it learns an arbitrary monotone mapping for calibration without imposing weight constraints, which simultaneously increases expressiveness and preserves the order of the underlying ranker. Because this high flexibility can also lead to overfitting and to non-smooth transforms that distort the original scores, we introduce the Smooth Calibration Loss (SCLoss), a regularizer that steers the UMNN toward the ideal calibrated state and keeps the learned mapping well behaved.
