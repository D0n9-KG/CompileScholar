# Non-reciprocal energy transfer through the Casimir effect

Zhujing Xu $^{1}$ , Xingyu Gao $^{1}$ , Jaehoon Bang $^{2}$ , Zubin Jacob $^{2,3}$ and Tongcang Li $^{1,2,3,4}$

One of the fundamental predictions of quantum mechanics is the occurrence of random fluctuations in a vacuum caused by the zero-point energy. Remarkably, quantum electromagnetic fluctuations can induce a measurable force between neutral objects, known as the Casimir effect<sup>1</sup>, and it has been studied both theoretically<sup>2,3</sup> and experimentally<sup>4-9</sup>. The Casimir effect can dominate the interaction between microstructures at small separations and is essential for micro- and nanotechnologies<sup>10,11</sup>.

It has been utilized to realize nonlinear oscillation<sup>12</sup>, quantum trapping<sup>13</sup>, phonon transfer<sup>14,15</sup> and dissipation dilution<sup>16</sup>. However, a non-reciprocal device based on quantum vacuum fluctuations remains an unexplored frontier. Here we report quantum-vacuum-mediated non-reciprocal energy transfer between two micromechanical oscillators. We parametrically modulate the Casimir interaction to realize a strong coupling between the two oscillators with different resonant frequencies.

We engineer the system's spectrum such that it possesses an exceptional point<sup>17-20</sup> in the parameter space and explore the asymmetric topological structure in its vicinity. By dynamically changing the parameters near the exceptional point and utilizing the non-adiabaticity of the process, we achieve non-reciprocal energy transfer between the two oscillators with high contrast. Our work demonstrates a scheme that employs quantum vacuum fluctuations to regulate energy transfer at the nanoscale and may enable functional Casimir devices in the future.

Many essential devices such as diodes, isolators and circulators require non-reciprocity. In this work, we realize non-reciprocity by the dynamic modulation of the nonlinear Casimir interaction near an exceptional point $^{17-20}$ . An exceptional point is a branch singularity of a non-Hermitian system such that the eigenvalues of the system Hamiltonian collapse with each other for both real and imaginary parts in the parameter space $^{18,21}$ .

The exceptional point has attracted broad interests in optics, optomechanics and acoustics $^{22-27}$ since it exhibits a unique topological structure. In particular, non-reciprocal energy transfer between two optically coupled mechanical modes of a membrane inside an optical cavity has been carried out by dynamically controlling the parameters in a loop that encloses an exceptional point $^{24}$ . Such non-reciprocal operations open new directions for controlling optomechanical systems. However, this has not been realized with Casimir interactions before.

We utilize the strong nonlinearity of the Casimir interaction and asymmetric structure near the exceptional point to break the time-reversal symmetry by modulating the separation between two micromechanical resonators at the desired frequency and amplitude (Fig. 1). In this way, we realize non-reciprocal energy transfer with the Casimir

interaction. The direction of energy transfer depends on the sequence of operations.

Our device consists of two cantilevers with resonant frequencies $\omega_{1}$ and $\omega_{2}$ , as shown in Fig. 1a. A microsphere is attached to the left cantilever. The oscillating amplitudes of the two cantilevers are denoted as $A_{1}$ and $A_{2}$ . Both cantilevers experience quantum vacuum fluctuations and attract each other by the Casimir force. We apply an additional modulation of separation to couple the two cantilevers with different frequencies (Extended Data Fig. 1).

For the separation considered in our experiment, quantum fluctuations (instead of thermal fluctuations) dominate the Casimir interaction (Extended Data Fig. 2). The schematic of the experimental setup is shown in Fig. 2a. The motion of the two cantilevers is independently monitored by two fibre interferometers. The natural frequency and damping of the two modified cantilevers are $\omega_{1} = 2\pi \times 4,826\mathrm{Hz}$ , $\omega_{2} = 2\pi \times 5,582\mathrm{Hz}$ , $\gamma_{1} = 2\pi \times 2.65\mathrm{Hz}$ and $\gamma_{2} = 2\pi \times 2.68\mathrm{Hz}$ when the cantilevers are far away.

The microsphere and both cantilevers are coated with gold. When the two surfaces are close to each other, they will experience the Casimir interaction. At small separations, the Casimir force between an ideal conductive sphere and an ideal conductive plate is[28]

$$
F _ {\mathrm {C}} ^ {0} (x) = - \frac {\pi^ {3} \hbar c}{3 6 0} \frac {R}{x ^ {3}}, \tag {1}
$$

where $R$ is the radius of the sphere; $x$ is the separation between the sphere and plate; and $h$ and $c$ are the reduced Planck constant and speed of light, respectively. The Casimir force $F_{\mathrm{C}}$ between the real materials can be calculated by the Lifshitz theory[29]; additional details of calculation are provided in the Supplementary Information and Extended Data Figs. 3 and 4. The measured Casimir force gradient divided by the radius of the microsphere is shown in Fig. 2b. The experimental data agree well with the theoretical prediction for the Casimir force between real gold films under the proximity-force approximation. More details about the measurement are shown in Extended Data Figs. 5-7.

We now discuss how we achieve strong coupling between the two cantilevers by quantum vacuum fluctuations. To couple the two cantilevers with different resonant frequencies, we modulate the separation between them at a slow rate $\omega_{\mathrm{mod}}$ . The effective coupling strength is controllable by changing the modulation amplitude $\delta_{\mathrm{d}}$ . Different from direct coupling that requires identical resonant frequencies, parametric coupling gives us more freedom to couple arbitrary resonators and control the coupling time, coupling strength and effective detuning of two oscillators[30,31]. In the experiment, non-reciprocal energy transfer is realized by dynamically controlling the parameters in a loop near the exceptional point. Parametric coupling gives us the freedom to control the coupling strength and detuning of the system as a function of time. In an interaction picture, the simplified Hamiltonian (Methods and Supplementary Information) is

$$
H _ {\text {i n t}} = \left( \begin{array}{c c} - \frac {\mathrm {i} \gamma_ {1}}{2} & \frac {g}{2} \\ \frac {g}{2} & - \frac {\mathrm {i} \gamma_ {2}}{2} - \delta \end{array} \right), \tag {3}
$$

where $\gamma_{1,2}$ denote the damping rates of the two cantilevers, $g$ is the coupling strength between the two cantilevers and $\delta$ is the detuning of the system. The coupling strength and detuning are directly related to modulation amplitude $\delta_{\mathrm{d}}$ and modulation frequency $f_{\mathrm{mod}} = \omega_{\mathrm{mod}} / 2\pi$ as $g = \frac{\mathrm{d}^2F_{\mathrm{C}}}{\mathrm{d}x^2}\delta_{\mathrm{d}} / 2\sqrt{m_1m_2\omega_1\omega_2}$ and $\delta = 2\pi (f_{\mathrm{mod}} - f_{21})$ .

Here $\frac{\mathrm{d}^2F_{\mathrm{C}}}{\mathrm{d}x^2}$ is the second derivative of Casimir force $F_{\mathrm{C}}$ and $m_{1,2}$ denote the masses of the two cantilevers. For this open system, the exceptional point is located at $g = \frac{|\gamma_1 - \gamma_2|}{2}$ and $\delta = 0$ . At this specific point, the two eigenvalues $\lambda_{\pm}$ of the Hamiltonian are degenerate for both real and imaginary parts, as shown in Fig. 1b,c. The eigenvalues (two surfaces) intersect each other and exhibit a non-trivial topological structure near the exceptional point.

The asymmetric structure near the exceptional point provides us the freedom to construct dynamical operations that break the time-reversal symmetry.

We first experimentally measure the spectrum of the system near the exceptional point. The exceptional point locates at the point when two eigenvalue surfaces intersect each other. Under the natural condition, the two cantilevers have comparable damping rates and the damping difference is close to zero. Thus, the exceptional point locates at $\delta_{\mathrm{d}} = 0$ which means no coupling between the two cantilevers. To break symmetry and achieve non-reciprocal energy transfer, we need to add extra gain or loss to the system to shift the exceptional point.

Here we add extra loss to one of the cantilevers for simplicity. In the experiment, we apply additional damping on cantilever 2 (right) such that $\gamma_{2} = 2\pi \times 13.82\mathrm{Hz}$ . Figure 3a,b shows the measured PSD of cantilever 2 and they show that the exceptional point in this double-cantilever system is approximately located at $\delta_{\mathrm{d}} = 5.5\mathrm{nm}$ and $f_{\mathrm{mod}} = 727\mathrm{Hz}$ . The separation is calculated to be $76\mathrm{nm}$ from the coupling strength.

Based on the measurement of the exceptional point, we design a dynamical clockwise (CW) control loop, as shown in Fig. 3c. The modulation amplitude is continuously controlled from $6.7\pm 0.6$ to $13.3\pm 0.6\mathrm{nm}$ and the modulation frequency is tuned from 680 to $785\mathrm{Hz}$ . The total loop time is $80\mathrm{ms}$ . When the modulation frequency $f_{\mathrm{mod}}$ is tuned from 680 to $785\mathrm{Hz}$ and $\delta_{\mathrm{d}} = 6.7\mathrm{nm}$ , the minimum energy gap between the two eigenstates is small compared with the operation speed and hence the process is non-adiabatic.

The energy prefers to be transferred to the cantilever with less damping after going through the control process when $\delta_{\mathrm{d}} = 6.7\mathrm{nm}$ . The process is adiabatic for other parts of the control loop since the energy gap between the two eigenstates is large compared with the operation speed. Therefore, time-reversal symmetry is broken by the control loop shown in Fig. 3c.

We now realize a Casimir diode by dynamically engineering the system. Figure 3d-g shows the experimental results of the non-reciprocal energy transfer process for the CW and anti-clockwise (ACW) loops with different cantilevers being excited. We use normalized energy, which is defined as $E_{1} / (E_{1} + E_{2})$ and $E_{2} / (E_{1} + E_{2})$ , to quantify the portion of energy for the two cantilevers in the transfer process. Here $E_{1}$ and $E_{2}$ are the energies of the two cantilevers and obtained by measuring their oscillating amplitudes.

In conclusion, we report non-reciprocal energy transfer with quantum vacuum fluctuations. Strong phonon coupling and energy transfer between two mechanical resonators are realized by parametric modulation. Under the careful design of the control loop, non-reciprocal energy transfer with high contrast is observed. Our work develops a flexible and robust method to regulate vacuum fluctuations and build functional Casimir devices. The Casimir coupling due to virtual photons can replace optomechanical coupling due to real photons in cavity optomechanics[24] for various functions. If the mechanical oscillators are cooled to the quantum regime at low temperature, the non-reciprocal Casimir effect can be used to regulate the flow of single phonons for quantum information processing.

# Methods

Experimental setup. We use two atomic force microscope (AFM) cantilevers to construct our Casimir interaction system. The left cantilever has a size of $450 \times 50 \times 2\mu \mathrm{m}$ . A polystyrene sphere with a diameter of $69.1 \pm 0.9\mu \mathrm{m}$ is attached on the free end of the left cantilever by a vacuum-compatible conductive epoxy. The right cantilever has a size of $500 \times 100 \times 1\mu \mathrm{m}$ .

Further, 70-nm-thick gold layers are coated on both sides of the cantilevers and the sphere to create metallic surfaces for the Casimir interaction and better reflectivity for detection. Films with nearly equal thicknesses are coated on both sides of the cantilevers to reduce strains on the cantilevers. Each cantilever is mounted on a piezoelectric stack to control its equilibrium position and vibration. One cantilever is connected to the electrical ground. A biased voltage is applied on the other cantilever to minimize the effects of the patch potential (Extended Data Figs. 5 and 6).

The motion of the two cantilevers is monitored by two fibre interferometers. A $50 - \mu \mathrm{W}$ laser with a wavelength of $1,310\mathrm{nm}$ is incident on the back side of the cantilever from a fibre and gets reflected. The reflected light is directed into the same optical fibre and interferes with the reflected light from the fibre-air interface. The interference signal gives information on separation between the cantilever and fibre, and hence, we can measure the motion of the cantilever.

The jacket and cladding of the fibres have been removed and the bare fibre has a diameter of $125\mu \mathrm{m}$ . The fibres are placed at a distance of more than $200\mu \mathrm{m}$ from the cantilevers to reduce the electrostatic effect. To minimize the environmental impact, the system is placed on top of optical tables by two stages of pneumatic vibration isolation (Extended Data Fig. 1). The experiment is conducted under a pressure of $10^{-4}$ torr at room temperature.

To measure the Casimir force, we monitor the frequency shift of the cantilever at each separation using a phase-locked loop (Extended Data Fig. 5).

Casimir force. We use the Lifshitz theory to calculate the Casimir force between real materials[29]. The Casimir energy per unit area of two parallel plates at zero temperature with a finite separation $x$ is[29]

$$
\begin{array}{l} E _ {0} (x) = \frac {\hbar}{4 \pi^ {2}} \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} k _ {\perp} d k _ {\perp} \int_ {0} ^ {\infty} d \xi \left\{\ln \left[ 1 - r _ {\mathrm {T M}} ^ {2} (\mathrm {i} \xi , k _ {\perp}) e ^ {- 2 x q} \right] \right. \tag {4} \\ \left. + \ln \left[ 1 - r _ {\mathrm {T E}} ^ {2} (\mathrm {i} \xi , k _ {\perp}) \mathrm {e} ^ {- 2 x q} \right] \right\}, \\ \end{array}
$$

where $\xi$ is the imaginary frequency and $k_{\perp}$ is the wavevector parallel to the surface; $r_{\mathrm{TE}}(\mathrm{i}\xi ,k_{\perp})$ and $r_{\mathrm{TM}}(\mathrm{i}\xi ,k_{\perp})$ are the reflection coefficients of the transverse-electric and transverse-magnetic modes[29]. At finite temperature $T$ , both quantum f
luctuations and thermal fluctuations contribute to the Casimir energy per unit area $E(x,T)$ . In our experiment, the radius of the microsphere and dimensions of the bare cantilever are far larger than the separation.

Therefore, we can use proximity-force approximation to evaluate the Casimir force between a sphere and a plate. The Casimir force for our system is $F_{\mathrm{C}}(x,T) = -2\pi RE(x,T)$ , where $R$ is the radius of the microsphere. As shown in Extended Data Fig. 2b, the contribution from thermal fluctuations at $T = 300\mathrm{K}$ is less than $6\%$ when the separation is less than $1,000\mathrm{nm}$ and it is only about $2\%$ when the separation is $200\mathrm{nm}$ . Thus, the effects of quantum vacuum fluctuations are dominant in our experiment. As shown in Extended Data Figs.

3 and 4, the Casimir pressure between the $70\mathrm{-nm}$ -thick gold films is different from the Casimir pressure between infinitely thick gold plates by less than $0.1\%$ , which is negligible for our experiment. The AFM image provided in Extended Data Fig. 7 shows that the root mean square roughness of the gold film is about $0.8\mathrm{nm}$ , which is small compared with the separation in our experiment.

Effective Hamiltonian. Under a slow modulation and Casimir interaction, the separation between the two cantilevers is time dependent such that $x(t) = d_0 + \delta_{\mathrm{d}}\cos (\omega_{\mathrm{mod}}t) + x_1(t) - x_2(t)$ . Here $d_0$ is the equilibrium separation when there is no modulation applied, $\delta_{\mathrm{d}}$ is the modulation amplitude and $\omega_{\mathrm{mod}} = 2\pi f_{\mathrm{mod}}$ , where $f_{\mathrm{mod}}$ is the modulation frequency. Further, $x_{1}(t)$ and $x_{2}(t)$ describe the vibrations of the cantilevers near their equilibrium positions. The motion of these cantilevers follows the below equations:

$$
\begin{array}{l} m _ {1} \ddot {x} _ {1} + m _ {1} \gamma_ {1} \dot {x} _ {1} + m _ {1} \omega_ {1} ^ {2} x _ {1} = F _ {\mathrm {C}} (x (t)), \\ m _ {2} \ddot {x} _ {2} + m _ {2} \gamma_ {2} \dot {x} _ {2} + m _ {2} \omega_ {2} ^ {2} x _ {2} = - F _ {\mathrm {C}} (x (t)). \tag {5} \\ \end{array}
$$

When the modulation and oscillation amplitudes of the two cantilevers are far smaller than the separation, that is, $\delta_{\mathrm{d}}, x_1, x_2 \ll d_0$ , we can expand the Casimir force term, namely, $F_{\mathrm{C}}(d_0 + \delta_{\mathrm{d}} \cos(\omega_{\mathrm{mod}} t) + x_1 - x_2)$ , to the second order. Since the two cantilevers have a frequency difference of over $700\mathrm{Hz}$ , direct coupling is neglected. The zero-order and first-order terms shift the frequency of the two cantilevers but have no contribution to energy transfer since they are off-resonant. The contribution comes from the $\frac{\mathrm{d}^2 F_{\mathrm{C}}}{\mathrm{d} x^2} |_{d_0} \delta_{\mathrm{d}} \cos(\omega_{\mathrm{mod}} t)(x_1 - x_2)$ term. Therefore, we can rewrite the equations as

$$
\begin{array}{l} \ddot {x _ {1}} + \gamma_ {1} \dot {x _ {1}} + \omega_ {1} ^ {2} x _ {1} = \frac {\Lambda}{m _ {1}} \cos (\omega_ {\mathrm {m o d}} t) (x _ {1} - x _ {2}), \\ \dot {x _ {2}} + \gamma_ {2} \dot {x _ {2}} + \omega_ {2} ^ {2} x _ {2} = \frac {A}{m _ {2}} \cos (\omega_ {\mathrm {m o d}} t) (x _ {2} - x _ {1}), \\ \end{array}
$$

where $\varLambda=\frac{\mathrm{d}^2F_{\mathrm{c}}}{\mathrm{d}x^2}\delta_{\mathrm{d}}$

Here we solve the second-order ordinary equations in equation (6) by generalizing the displacements $x_{1}(t)$ and $x_{2}(t)$ to complex values $z_{1}(t)$ and $z_{2}(t)$ such that $x_{1}(t) = \mathrm{Re}[z_{1}(t)]$ and $x_{2}(t) = \mathrm{Re}[z_{2}(t)]$ . We separate the fast-rotating term and slow-varying term for $z_{1}(t)$ and $z_{2}(t)$ such that

$$
\begin{array}{l} z _ {1} (t) = A _ {1} (t) \mathrm {e} ^ {- \mathrm {i} \omega_ {1} t}, \\ z _ {2} (t) = A _ {2} (t) \mathrm {e} ^ {- \mathrm {i} \omega_ {2} t}, \\ \end{array}
$$

(7)

where $A_{1}(t)$ and $A_{2}(t)$ are slow-varying amplitudes and we can neglect their second derivatives $\ddot{A}_{1}(t)$ and $\ddot{A}_{2}(t)$ in the equations of motion. Besides, we consider the condition that the damping rates of the two cantilevers are far smaller than their resonant frequencies, that is, $\gamma_{1} \ll \omega_{1}$ and $\gamma_{2} \ll \omega_{2}$ . Therefore, the equations of motion can be rewritten as

$$
\begin{array}{l} - \mathrm {i} \omega_ {1} \gamma_ {1} A _ {1} (t) \mathrm {e} ^ {- \mathrm {i} \omega_ {1} t} - 2 \mathrm {i} \omega_ {1} \dot {A} _ {1} (t) \mathrm {e} ^ {- \mathrm {i} \omega_ {1} t} \\ = \frac {A}{2 m _ {1}} \left(A _ {1} (t) e ^ {- i (\omega_ {1} + \omega_ {\mathrm {m o d}}) t} - A _ {2} (t) e ^ {- i (\omega_ {2} - \omega_ {\mathrm {m o d}}) t}\right), \\ - \mathrm {i} \omega_ {2} \gamma_ {2} A _ {2} (t) \mathrm {e} ^ {- \mathrm {i} \omega_ {2} t} - 2 \mathrm {i} \omega_ {2} \dot {A} _ {2} (t) \mathrm {e} ^ {- \mathrm {i} \omega_ {2} t} \tag {8} \\ = \frac {A}{2 m _ {2}} \left(A _ {2} (t) e ^ {- i (\omega_ {2} - \omega_ {\mathrm {m o d}}) t} - A _ {1} (t) e ^ {- i (\omega_ {1} + \omega_ {\mathrm {m o d}}) t}\right), \\ \end{array}
$$

where we have taken the rotating-frame approximation and neglected the fast-rotating term. Now, we apply the transformation such that $A_1'(t) = A_1(t)$ and $A_2'(t) = A_2(t)\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\delta t}$ , where $\delta = \omega_{1} + \omega_{\mathrm{mod}} - \omega_{2}$ . Then, the equation of motion becomes

$$
\mathrm {i} \binom {\dot {A} _ {1} ^ {\prime} (t)} {\dot {A} _ {2} ^ {\prime} (t)} = \binom {- \mathrm {i} \frac {\gamma_ {1}}{2}} {\frac {\Lambda}{4 m _ {2} \omega_ {2}}} - \mathrm {i} \frac {\gamma_ {2}}{2} - \delta \binom {A _ {1} ^ {\prime} (t)} {A _ {2} ^ {\prime} (t)}, \tag {9}
$$

where we have neglected the fast-rotating terms. The vibrations of the two cantilevers can be quantized as phonons. By introducing normalized amplitudes $c_{1} = \sqrt{\frac{m_{1}\omega_{1}}{\hbar}} A_{1}^{\prime}$ and $c_{2} = \sqrt{\frac{m_{2}\omega_{2}}{\hbar}} A_{2}^{\prime}$ , we obtain the equation of motion for the phonon modes as

$$
\mathrm {i} \binom {\dot {c _ {1}}} {\dot {c _ {2}}} = \left( \begin{array}{l l} - \mathrm {i} ^ {\frac {\gamma_ {1}}{2}} & \frac {g}{2} \\ \frac {g}{2} & - \mathrm {i} ^ {\frac {\gamma_ {2}}{2}} - \delta \end{array} \right) \binom {c _ {1}} {c _ {2}}, \tag {10}
$$

where $g = \frac{\Lambda}{2\sqrt{m_1m_2\omega_1\omega_2}} = \frac{\mathrm{d}^2F_{\mathrm{C}}}{\mathrm{d}x^2}\delta_{\mathrm{d}}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{m_1m_2\omega_1\omega_2}}$ and $\delta = \omega_{1} + \omega_{\mathrm{mod}} - \omega_{2}$ . Thus, the effective Hamiltonian of the system can be expressed as equation (3). The eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian are

$$
\begin{array}{l} \lambda_ {\pm} = - \frac {\delta}{2} - \mathrm {i} \frac {\gamma_ {1} + \gamma_ {2}}{4} \tag {11} \\ \pm \frac {1}{2} \sqrt {- \frac {\left(\gamma_ {1} - \gamma_ {2}\right) ^ {2}}{4} + \delta^ {2} + g ^ {2} - \mathrm {i} \left(\gamma_ {1} - \gamma_ {2}\right) \delta}. \\ \end{array}
$$

The eigenvalue depends on the modulation amplitude $\delta_{\mathrm{d}}$ and modulation frequency $f_{\mathrm{mod}} = \omega_{\mathrm{mod}} / 2\pi$ . We can also see that the exceptional point locates at $\delta = 0$ and $g = \frac{|\gamma_1 - \gamma_2|}{2}$ , which means that $\omega_{\mathrm{mod}} = \omega_2 - \omega_1$ and $\delta_{\mathrm{d}} = \frac{|\gamma_1 - \gamma_2| \sqrt{m_1 m_2 \omega_1 \omega_2}}{\mathrm{d}^2 F_{\mathrm{C}} / \mathrm{d}x^2}$ .

Experimentally, we can independently control the modulation frequency $f_{\mathrm{mod}}$ and modulation amplitude $\delta_{\mathrm{d}}$ as a function of time and realize non-reciprocal energy transfer by controlling the parameters along a loop near the exceptional point. An important striking feature is that the preferred direction of energy transfer depends on the direction of the control loop. Our simulations (Supplementary Information) of the energy transfer process take into account the nonlinearity inherent in this Casimir-force-
coupled system.
