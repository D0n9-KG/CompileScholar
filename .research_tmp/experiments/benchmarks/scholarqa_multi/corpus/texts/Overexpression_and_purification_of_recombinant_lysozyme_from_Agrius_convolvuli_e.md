# Overexpression and purification of recombinant lysozyme from Agrius convolvuli expressed as inclusion body in Escherichia coli

Soon-Ik Park and Sung Moon Yoe*

Department of Biological Sciences, Dankook University, Cheonan 330-714, Korea

(Received 5 March 2012; received in revised form 14 June 2012; accepted 19 June 2012)

Amongst the various antimicrobial peptides, lysozyme plays a central role in initiating and maintaining the antibacterial defense response of insect. Here we propose the biosynthesis and refolding of recombinant lysozyme in Escherichia coli expressed in inclusion body form. The Agrius lysozyme gene was amplified using gene specific primers and then ligated into the pGEX-4T-1 vector, which contained the glutathione S-transferase (GST) gene as a fusion partner. A recombinant lysozyme was expressed in $E.

$ coli Rosetta cells using a pGEX-4T-1 expression vector, and the fusion protein was induced by ioporpyl- $\beta$ -D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). The recombinant protein produced as an inclusion body was resolubilized in solubilization buffer, and the resultant solution was dialyzed in refolding buffer. After thrombin cleavage, the recombinant lysozyme was purified by ion exchange chromatography and reverse phase chromatography.

The recombinant lysozyme was subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis and immunoreactivity against the anti-Agrius lysozyme was observed by western blot analysis of this protein. The recombinant lysozyme displayed antibacterial activity against Bacillus megaterium and Micrococcus luteus, which was confirmed by the inhibition zone assay.

Keywords: lysozyme; Agrius convolvuli; inclusion body; expression; refolding

# Introduction

Lysozymes play an important role in protecting against exterior bacteria. It is also known as muramidase or N-acetylmuramide glycanhydrolase, attacking and damaging bacterial cell walls by catalyzing the hydrolysis of the 1,4- $\beta$ -linkages between N-acetylmuramic acid and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine residues in a peptidoglycan and between N-acetyl-D-glucosamine residues (Ganz 2002). It has been found in many kinds of vertebrates, invertebrates, plants, and micro-organisms since its initial discovery in the 1920s. Insect lysozymes have been cloned and overexpressed in Escherichia coli. Recently, the c-type lysozyme has been cloned from some lepidopterans, including Spodoptera litura (Kim and Yoe 2009).

Expression systems using prokaryotic cells are the most widely used systems for the production of recombinant protein (Hanning and Makrides 1998). Many recombinant proteins expressed in $E.$ coli tend to aggregate and form inclusion bodies (IB). IB formation is mainly due to limited solubility and the lack of appropriate chaperone systems in the prokaryotic host. As suggested by Thatcher and Hitchcock (1994), purifying IB may be advantageous in reducing the impurities load on subsequent protein purification. Many industrial processes make use of these protein aggregates, though this strategy requires solubilization and renaturation of the product.

In the present work, we reported the purification and refolding study of IB using an insect lysozyme

as a model protein. The recombinant Agrius convolvuli lysozyme (ACLyz), which was expressed as IB in $E.$ coli, was solubilized, refolded by a chaotropic reagent, dialyzed, and subjected to on-column refolding. Purification of the bioactive recombinant protein was accomplished by cation exchange and reverse-phase chromatography.

# Materials and methods

# Insect, bacterial strains, and plasmid

Agrius convolvuli were supplied from the Rural Development Administration, Suwon, Korea, and the total RNA was obtained from the fifth instar larvae to clone the lysozyme gene. Larvae were infected with $E.$ coli K12 D21 to induce an immune response. Fat bodies were dissected $24\mathrm{h}$ after immunization, and stored at $-80^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for RNA extraction. The $E.$ coli JM109 (Progmega) and pGEX-4T-1 vector (Pharmacia) were used for cloning and the $E.$ coli Rosetta singles strain (Novagen) was used for expression studies.

# Overexpression of recombinant lysozyme

Escherichia coli Rosetta singles strain was transformed with the recombinant plasmid and a single colony grown for $12\mathrm{h}$ in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium with $100~\mu \mathrm{g / ml}$ ampicillin at $37^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ with shaking at $200~\mathrm{rpm}$ was used for inoculation. When the optical density reached 0.3 at $600~\mathrm{nm}$ , the recombinant lysozyme overexpression was induced by adding ioporpyl- $\beta$ -D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) to a final concentration of $0.3\mathrm{mM}$ and the solution was incubated for $3\mathrm{h}$ at $25^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ .

# Disruption and preparation of IB

The cells were harvested by centrifugation at $4000g$ for $20\mathrm{min}$ at $4^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . The pellets were resuspended in 1/20 culture volume of phosphate-buffer saline (PBS, pH 7.4) containing $1\%$ Triton X-100 at $4^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ and sonicated three times for 15 sec each time. After centrifugation at $25,000g$ for $15\mathrm{min}$ at $4^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , the IB were washed twice with $2\mathrm{M}$ urea mixing with intermittent sonication at $4^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . The isolated IB were stored at $-20^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ .

# Solubilization, refolding, and thrombin cleavage of the recombinant protein

The IB were dissolved in a solubilization buffer containing $8\mathrm{M}$ urea for $12\mathrm{h}$ at room temperature with shaking and the insoluble material was removed by centrifugation at $25,000~\mathrm{g}$ for $15\mathrm{min}$ at $4^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . The supernatant was dialyzed overnight against $1\mathrm{M}$ urea. After dialysis, $100~\mu \mathrm{g}$ of GST-ACLyz (glutathione S transferase-recombinant ACLyz) fusion protein per 1 unit thrombin (Sigma) was added to cleave the glutathione S-transferase (GST) from the recombinant ACLyz and incubated for $8\mathrm{h}$ at room temperature.

# Purification of recombinant lysozyme

The cleaved proteins were denatured by adding six volumes of denaturing buffer containing $8\mathrm{M}$ urea with $100\mathrm{mM}\beta$ -mercaptoethanol ( $\beta$ -MET) and loaded onto Resource S (GE healthcare) column equilibrated with $8\mathrm{M}$ urea. The bound proteins were eluted using a linear gradient starting with an elution buffer containing $8\mathrm{M}$ urea and ending with $10\mathrm{mM}$ Tris-HCl, $1\mathrm{MNaCl}$ , $1\mathrm{mM}$ GSH, $0.1\mathrm{mM}$ GSSG in $1\mathrm{M}$ urea, $\mathrm{pH}9.0$ . The fractions containing the eluted proteins were analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then denatured by adding six volumes of the denaturing buffer. The denatured

proteins were applied to a Resource RPC (GE healthcare) column equilibrated with $8\mathrm{M}$ urea and, followed by three column volumes of $8\mathrm{M}$ urea. The column containing denatured proteins was eluted with a linear gradient of $10 - 90\%$ acetonitrile in $0.1\%$ aqueous trifluoroacetic acid (TFA).

# Electrophoresis and western blot analysis

Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was carried out in a $12.5\%$ polyacrylamide gel. After electrophoresis, the gel was stained with Coomassie brilliant blue R250. Western blot analysis was performed as previously described using a rat polyclonal anti-lysozyme antibody (Kim and Yoe 2008) and goat anti-GST antibody. The protein concentration at each purification step was determined by the bicinchonic acid (BCA) assay according to the manufacturer's specifications using bovine serum albumin as the standard (Stoscheck 1990).

# Assay of antibacterial activity

Antibacterial activities of recombinant ACLyz were measured using the inhibition zone assay (Kim, Park, et al. 2011). Each of the $1\%$ agarose plates containing $7\mu \mathrm{g}$ of Micrococcus luteus powder (Sigam) and $6\times 10^{4}$ cells/ml of Bacillus megaterium was prepared and wells that were $3\mathrm{mm}$ in diameter were punched out of the plates. The antimicrobial activity of an aliquot of each fast protein liquid chromatography [FPLC] fraction against Gram-positive strains (B. megaterium and M. luteus) was examined. After overnight incubation at $37^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , the diameter of the clear zones was recorded.

# Results

# Recombinant plasmid construction

The full-length nucleotide sequence was obtained by RACE-PCR using total RNA from the $A$ . convolvuli fat bodies. Deduced amino acids are indicated by single-letter code below the sequence. E. coli JM109 and BL21 were transformed with the recombinant plasmid for the library and overexpression as shown in Figure 1B.

# Discussion

The ACLyz is a single-chain polypeptide of 120 amino acids that is cross-linked through four disulfide bridges. The DNA fragment containing the mature ACLyz sequence was generated by PCR using A. convolvuli cDNA as template and cloned at the BamHI and XhoI sites of the pGEX-4T-1 vector. The recombinant plasmid was sequenced and proven to be correct. To improve the recombinant protein yield, the Rosetta singles strain was used for the expression of recombinant ACLyz (Figure 1). The Rosetta (DE3) singles strain, which provides tRNAs for six rare codons, was designed to enhance the expression of eukaryotic proteins containing codons rarely used in $E.$ coli (Fan et al. 2009).

Soluble recombinant proteins are often properly folded, functional, and easier to purify than aggregated proteins from IB (Galloway et al. 2003). A general way to reduce protein aggregation due to self-association of nascent proteins is to reduce the rate of protein synthesis in $E.$ coli by applying lower growth temperatures. Following sonication, the lysate was removed as the soluble proteins by centrifugation and washing with $2\mathrm{M}$ urea to increase the purity of the IB (Figure 2).

The washing steps were used to purify the IB prior to protein refolding, which is strongly affected by the presence of impurities (Batas et al. 1999). Once the IB were isolated and purified, the next step was solubilization. Eight molar urea was employed to solubilize IB. After dialysis, the remaining impurities consisted of only three or four bands (Figure 2C, lane 1). These bands were most likely outer membrane proteins, whose presence in IB has been previously observed (Rinas et al. 1993).

Before the on-column refolding of ACLyz, the solubilized IB were dialyzed against $1\mathrm{M}$ urea to ensure proper cleavage of GST from the fusion protein and to prevent denaturation of thrombin and aggregation of the proteins. As shown in Figure 2C, lanes 2 and 3, the dimeric or higher forms of the proteins were observed to the formation of incorrect disulfide bonds, which indicated that reduced a thiol reagent, such as $\beta$ -MET, should be used for chromatography.

In recent years, many novel high-throughput protein refolding methods have been developed for renaturation of inclusion body proteins, including dilution, dialysis, and solid-phase separation. Different dialysis and dilution methods along with the use of additives have been reported to improve the recovery of inclusion body proteins (Clark 1998; Tsumoto et al. 2003; Middelberg 2004; Vallejo and Rinas 2004).

Several techniques based on the interaction between protein and chromatography techniques have been developed to help correct refolding and increase the refolding yield and product purity (Li et al. 2002). In this study, we used a Resource S column for on-column refolding and Resource RPC column for final purification. The cleaved proteins were denatured by adding six volumes of denaturing buffer and applied onto a Resource S column equilibrated with $8\mathrm{M}$ urea.

The bound proteins were eluted using a linear g
radient of $8\mathrm{M}$ urea and $1\mathrm{M}$ urea with $10\mathrm{mM}$ Tris-HCl, $1\mathrm{MNaCl}$ , $1\mathrm{mM}$ GSH, and $0.1\mathrm{mM}$ GSSG, $\mathrm{pH}9.0$ (Figure 3A). Proteins containing multiple disulfide bonds, such as lysozyme, which contains six cysteines, require a more elaborative refolding process in the presence of optimal concentrations of both oxidizing and reducing agents for the formation of disulfide bonds (Fischer et al. 1993; Vallejo and Rinas 2004).

As shown in Figure 3B, recombinant ACLyz was not purified from the inclusion body. Therefore, final purification was performed

using the Resource RPC column, which involves hydrophobic interaction chromatography, to purify recombinant ACLyz (Figure 3C). It should be noted that the usage of a reduced thiol reagent before column injection was crucial so that the ACLyz, GST, and GST-AClyz could be bound to the RPC column separately. The recombinant ACLyz was not separated from GST and GST-AClyz without $\beta$ -MET in final purification (data not shown). The purified recombinant ACLyz, which had an apparent molecular weight of $15\mathrm{kDa}$ (Figure 3D), displayed antibacterial activity against M. lueus and B.

megaterium (Figure 4). However, the activity of recombinant ACLyz was weaker than wilde-type ACLyz. As Figure 3D shown, fractions of the eluted protein were analyzed by SDSPAGE, revealing minor bands below the major band of $15\mathrm{kDa}$ . It is likely that formation of these minor forms is due to codon bias problems that are highly prevalent in recombinant expression systems, when heterologous proteins containing rare codons accumulate in large quantities (Kim, Yoe, et al. 2011).

The full-length cDNA encoding ACLyz was cloned and the recombinant ACLyz was successfully produced as an inclusion body using a prokaryotic system. The solubilization, refolding, and purification procedures for recombinant ACLyz have several distinct advantages over other methods, such as simple on-column refolding and purification of IB using efficient reducing agents. In addition, the results and methods developed in this study will help to resolve several industrial challenges in regard to proteins that express in IB.
