ABSTRACT: Stem cell therapies are increasingly recognized as the future direction of regenerative medicine, but the biological fate of the administrated stem cells remains a major concern for clinical translation, which calls for an approach to efficiently monitoring the stem cell behaviors in vivo. Magnetic particle imaging (MPI) is an emerging technology for cell tracking; however, its utility has been largely restricted due to the lack of optimal magnetic nanoparticle tracers.

Herein, by controlled engineering of the size and shape of magnetic nanoparticles tailored to MPI physics theory, a specialized MPI tracer, based on cubic iron oxide nanoparticles with an edge length of $22\ \mathbf{nm}$ (CIONs-22), is developed. Due to the inherent lower proportion of disordered surface spins, CIONs-22 exhibit significantly larger saturation magnetization than that of spherical ones, while they possess similar saturation magnetization but smaller coercivity compared to larger-sized CIONs. These magnetic properties of CIONs-22 warrant high sensitivity and resolution of MPI.

With their efficient cellular uptake, CIONs-22 exhibit superior MPI performance for stem cell labeling and tracking compared to the commercialized tracer Vivotrax. By virtue of these advantages, CIONs-22 enable real-time and prolonged monitoring of the spatiotemporal trajectory of stem cells transplanted to hindlimb ischemia mice, which demonstrates the great potential of CIONs-22 as MPI tracers to advance stem cell therapies.

In this regard, it is of particular relevance to monitor stem cells after administration into the body, which would provide valuable information for predicting the therapeutic efficacy, evaluating the potential risk, and optimizing treatment strategies.3

Current technologies for in vivo stem cell tracking include optical imaging, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and radionuclide imaging.4−7 Optical imaging has single-cell resolution, but its clinical translation is hindered by the poor tissue penetration of the light photon.8 Radionuclide imaging, with excellent depth penetration, faces the disadvantage of narrow temporal window due to the short half-lives of radionuclide tracers that are also constantly questioned for toxicities.9,10 MRI offers high spatial resolution and unlimited depth penetration without using radiotracers.

However, the dark signal generated by T2-weighed MRI contrast agents (CAs) (e.g., iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs))11,12 can be easily confused with that of intrinsic hypointense regions such as hemorrhage, bones, and lungs,13 while the sensitivity of bright T1-weighted MRI for molecular imaging is limited by current T1 CAs,14 some of which are raising safety concerns due to leaching of toxic ions, such as gadolinium-based agents.15

Recently, magnetic particle imaging (MPI) is emerging as a promising imaging modality that attracts growing attention. It collects signals from the nonlinear magnetization of the magnetic nanoparticle tracers that, represented by IONPs, determine the imaging performance .16,17 The MPI is irradiation-free and characterized by high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), zero signal attenuation, and potential high sensitivity,18 which renders MPI an ideal platform for in vivo stem cell tracking.

Currently, the MPI-based cell tracking mainly employs the existing $\mathrm{IONPs},$ for instance, the commercialized Vivotrax and Resovist, which are initially designed for T2- weighted MRI and not necessarily suitable for MPI.19,20 In fact, the MPI signals of Vivotrax and Resovist are contributed by the small portion (less than $3\%$ ) of large-sized nanoclusters in their polydisperse formulations.21 Bulte et al. reported their pioneering work using monodispersed superparamagnetic IONPs for MPI-based cell tracking, which demonstrated a slightly better MPI performance than that of Resovist.

22 Conclusively, the current MPI-based cell tracking is far from satisfactory due to the suboptimal design of magnetic tracers, even though the potential detection sensitivity of MPI is estimated to be comparable with that of positron emission computed tomography.3,16 Hence, the development of uniformly sized high-performance magnetic tracers that are tailored to MPI physics is crucial for improving the sensitivity and resolution of MPI-based cell tracking.

X-space reconstruction is the principal theory of the MPI system, in which the imaging effect can be described by the point spread function (PSF).23 The PSF is determined by the gradient magnetic field (G) and the derived function of the tracer magnetization curve $\left({\mathrm{d}M}/{\mathrm{d}H}\right)$ .23 Since $G$ is a specified parameter for the MPI scanner, the imaging sensitivity and spatial resolution can be improved by increasing the peak height of the $\mathbf{d}M/\mathbf{d}H$ and/or decreasing its full width at halfmaximum (FWHM).24 Importantly, the peak height and

FWHM of $\mathrm{d}M/\mathrm{d}H$ are largely dependent on the inherent magnetic properties of the MPI tracer, including saturation magnetization $\left(M_{s}\right)$ , magnetic susceptibility $(\chi)$ , magnetic relaxation time (τ), and coercivity (Hc).21,25,26

Considering that the shape is closely related to the magnetic properties of $\mathrm{IONPs},$ it is rational to optimize the shapes of IONPs for MPI application.27 Up to now, the spherical iron oxide nanoparticles (SIONs) are the most commonly studied MPI tracers.14,28 Compared with SIONs, cubic iron oxide nanoparticles (CIONs) generally have smaller surface anisotropies and less disordered spins, which are conducive to obtain larger $M_{s}$ and higher $\chi.^{2\hat{7},29}$ Therefore, CIONs will have a higher peak height of dM/dH than SIONs (Figure 1a).

On the other hand, the $H_{\mathrm{c}}$ of CIONs will increase with size, leading to the prolonged $\tau$ that impedes the MPI performance.25,26,30 Consequently, larger-sized CIONs may exhibit wider FWHM of $\mathrm{d}M/\mathrm{d}H$ than the smaller one (Figure 1a). Collectively, we infer that the engineered CIONs of monodispersity, large $M_{s},$ high $\chi,$ and weak
$H_{\mathrm{c}}$ are ideal magnetic tracers for MPI. To test this hypothesis, we synthesized monodispersed CIONs and SIONs of different sizes and gauged their performance as MPI tracers.

Results show that CIONs with edge lengths of $22.4~\pm~1.3~\mathrm{{\nm}}$ (CIONs-22) outcompete other probes and enable the most sensitive and accurate tracking of MSCs both in vitro and in vivo.

# RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Characterization and Functional Validation of IONPs with Different Shapes and Sizes. Different sized CIONs and SIONs were prepared via a “heat-up” process. Transmission electron microscope (TEM) images show that CIONs with edge lengths of $22.4\pm1.3$ , $26.2\pm2.8,$ and $45.6\pm2.4\mathrm{nm}$ (CIONs-22, CIONs-26, CIONs-46) and SIONs with diameters of $16.7\pm1.5$ and $21.6\pm2.5\ \mathrm{nm}$ (SIONs-17, SIONs-22) are highly uniform in shape and size (Figure 1b, Figures S1 and S2), in stark contrast to the Vivotrax of polydispersity (Figure S3).

The powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns reveal that SIONs-17 and different sized CIONs have identical magnetite $\left(\mathrm{Fe}_{3}\mathrm{O}_{4}\right)$ crystal structures (JCPDS card file no. 19-0629) (Figure 1c), while SIONs-22 have a wustite (FeO) structure (JCPDS card file no. 46-1312) (Figure S2).

As shown in Figure 1d, CIONs-22 have similar $M_{s}$ but smaller $H_{\mathrm{c}}$ compared to larger-sized CIONs, since the proportion of surface disordered spins for different sized CIONs remains almost unchanged, but the $H_{\mathrm{c}}$ of CIONs will increase with size in the single-domain size range.30 As for SIONs, they have significantly smaller $M_{s}$ and lower static magnetic susceptibility $\left(\chi_{0}\right)$ than CIONs, presumably due to the inherent higher portion of disordered spins on their surface (Figure 1d).

We exclude SIONs-22 for further investigation since they exhibit even much lower magnetization than SIONs-17 (Figure S2), which tallies well with previous studies that once the size is above $14\ \mathrm{nm}$ , the magnetization of SIONs will decrease as size increases because of the crystalline change.31,32

The MPI signal of CIONs-22 is significantly stronger than that of SIONs or larger-sized CIONs at the same Fe concentration (Figure 2f), which is consistent with our previous theoretical speculation. Notably, the MPI signal intensity generated by CIONs-22 is 4.15-fold higher than that of commercialized Vivotrax (Figure 2g). We then interpret the superior sensitivity and resolution of CIONs-22 according to MPI physics theory.21,25 Compared with SIONs, CIONs-22 have a larger $M_{s}$ and higher $\chi_{0},$ conferring higher peak height of the PSF (Figure 2h). For larger-sized CIONs, they have slower magnetic relaxation that can asymmetrically blur the PSF in the scan direction, leading to a decrement of the MPI signal after X-space reconstruction (Figure 2h).21

In Vitro MPI of CIONs-22-Labeled BMSCs. Next, we investigated the cellular uptake of CIONs-22 by bone mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs). Prussian blue staining and TEM imaging demonstrate efficient cellular internalization of CIONs-22 (Figure 3a and Figure S6), which is in agreement with our previous study showing that a CION-based nonviral gene delivery system displayed enhanced endocytosis.

34 The intracellular content of iron after cellular uptake was quantified and found to be positively correlated with the concentrations of CIONs-22 added and can reach as high as $29\mathrm{\pg/cell}$ (Figure 3b). Importantly, CIONs-22 are highly biocompatible and have no evident cytotoxicity even at high concentrations (Figure 3c). Also, BMSCs remain pluripotent after treatment with CIONs-22, as the differentiation of BMSCs into osteocytes or adipocytes is unaffected (Figure S7).

The in vitro performance of CIONs-22 for labeling and visualizing BMSCs under MPI was further explored. Results show that the MPI signal of CIONs-22-labeled BMSCs is significantly higher than that of Vivotrax-labeled BMSCs of the same cell number (Figure 3d). MPI linear scanning spectra revealed that the SNRs of the acquired images were 27.91 for ${\sim}2000\mu\mathrm{L}^{-1}$ Vivotrax-labeled BMSCs and 2.44 for $\sim1300\mu\mathrm{L}^{-1}$ as-labeled cells (Figure 3d and e), which drastically dropped to 1.24 when the cell concentration decreased to ${\sim}250~\mu\mathrm{L}^{-1}$ (Figure 3f).

Compared with Vivotrax, CIONs-22 provided much higher SNRs, which were 93.53, 5.45, and 1.64 at the cell concentrations of ${\sim}2000\$ , ${\sim}500,$ , and $\sim60~\mu\mathrm{L}^{-1}$ , respectively (Figure 3d, $\mathbf{g},$ and h).

Particularly, with CIONs-22 as the cell tracer, MPI can track as few as ${\sim}30\mu\mathrm{L}^{-1}$ BMSCs, with an SNR of 1.56, which guarantees unambiguous visualization of the target cells (Figure 3i), demonstrating a much lower MPI detection threshold for CIONs-22-labeled BMSCs than that for previously reported monodispersed superparamagnetic IONPs-labeled ones.22 Moreover, a straightforward comparison of the magnetic particle spectroscopy (MPS) signal efficacy was conducted to evaluate the MPI performance.

The MPS signal of CIONs-22-labeled MSCs $(8.0\times10^{-3}\mathrm{~Am}^{2}/\mathrm{g}$ Fe) was about three times higher than that of Vivotrax-labeled ones $(2.4\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Am}^{2}/\mathrm{g}$ Fe) (Table S1). There is a linear relationship between MPI signals and the numbers of labeled cells (Figure 3j), which is not observed for the T2-weighted MRI signals (Figure 3k).

Notably, the slope value for CIONs22-labeled BMSCs $(2.9\times10^{-4}$ per cell) was four times that of Vivotrax-labeled ones $\mathit{7}\times10^{-5}$ per cell), further demonstrating superior MPI performance of CIONs-22 for cell tracking (Figure 3j).

In Vivo MPI Tracking of CIONs-22-Labeled BMSCs. To examine the performance of CIONs-22-based MPI for cell tracking in vivo, CIONs-22-labeled BMSCs were subcutaneously injected into nude mice for imaging. As expected, the BMSCs can be successfully detected in both the prone position and supine position due to the excellent tissue penetration of MPI (Figure 4a). In line with the in vitro results, we observe that MPI of BMSCs is far more sensitive with CIONs-22 as the tracer than with Vivotrax.

Specifically, no signal of Vivotraxlabeled BMSCS can be detected by MPI once the cell number decreases to $\sim5000$ (Figure 4b), while as few as $\sim2500$ cells can be clearly visualized when labeled with CIONs-22 (Figure 4c). To our knowledge, this is the highest sensitivity of MPIbased stem cell monitoring that thus far can be achieved in vivo. Furthermore, we evaluated the performance of MPI tracking of BMSCs in the context of systemic administration.

CIONs-22-labeled BMSCs, at the magnitude of ${\sim}100000$ , can be clearly detected to accumulate in the lung within $3\mathrm{~h~}$ after intravenous (i.v.) injection and then migrate to the liver (Figure 4d), whereas no signal is observed for Vivotrax-labeled BMSCs of the same cell number (Figure 4e). Since CIONs are also contrast agents for MRI,30 we managed to collect MR images of the mice. During the first $3\mathrm{~h~}$ post i.v. injection of CIONs-22-labeled BMSCs, no changes in MRI signals can be discerned due to the intrinsic dark MRI signals of the lung (Figure 4f).

As BMSCs migrate to the liver, the MRI signals in the liver gradually attenuate. After 3 days post i.v. injection, whether the stem cells are still retained in or eliminated from the body is no longer detectable by MRI because of the dilution of CIONs-22 during cell proliferation and biodistribution (Figure 4f).35 In contrast, MPI allows long-term accurate tracking of CIONs-22-labeled BMSCs for up to 7 days (Figure $\scriptstyle4\ g\prime$ , providing a broad time window for inspecting the whole dynamics of administrated stem cells in the body.

Moreover, representative three-dimensional (3D) projection MPI images can be overlaid with computed tomography images (MPI-CT) for precisely localizing the labeled stem cells in vivo, by mapping MPI signals to specific organs and tissues (Figure S8).

MPI Tracking of CIONs-22-Labeled BMSCs in Hindlimb Ischemia Mi
ce Models. Encouraged by the in vitro and in vivo results, we next investigated whether CIONs-22- based MPI is competent in real-time visualization of the stem cell movement in the context of clinically relevant disease models.

We examined the performance of this system in tracking stem cells given to hindlimb ischemia mice, to monitor the migration behaviors of transplanted stem cells in real time, which may help to determine whether there are sufficient therapeutic cells reaching the target area and what dose should be used in each individual patient.36 We employed MPI-CT to record the migration and distribution pattern of the MPI tracer-labeled BMSCs after intramuscular (i.m.) injection into the gastrocnemius (Figure 5a).

There is no evident translocation of the transplanted stem cells in the nonischemic mouse, which remain within the injection site through the whole experiment (Figure 5b), while in the ischemic mouse, it is clearly shown by MPI that BMSCs migrate toward the muscles that are closer to the ligation site, such as adductor, quadriceps, and gracilis (Figure 5b), consistent with previous reports that stem cells can respond to inflammatory factors secreted by damaged tissues and subsequently migrate there.

37 Notably, CIONs-22-labeled BMSCs generate fairly strong MPI signal even at the end of the monitoring, which ensures long-term and unambiguous tracking of the dynamics of BMSCs in vivo. In contrast, Vivotrax-based MPI collects much weaker signal of the labeled BMSCs, from which the migration direction and retention time of therapeutic cells are hardly distinguishable (Figure 5c).

These results demonstrate that the integration of CIONs-22- based MPI and CT imaging is sufficient to determine the distribution pattern of stem cells, which has a sensitivity comparable to fluorescence imaging and obviates the need for complex 3D reconstruction. To this end, we believe that CIONs-22-based MPI represents a plausible strategy to decipher the biological fate of therapeutic stem cells in vivo, which may help the field of stem cell therapy to step further toward clinical applications.

# CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, by tuning the shape and size of $\mathrm{IONPs}_{\mathrm{.}}$ , a specialized MPI tracer, CIONs-22, was developed and applied for MPI-based stem cell tracking. Due to the fewer surface disordered spins and the size effect, CIONs-22 on one hand have larger $M_{s}$ and higher $\chi$ than SIONs and on the other hand possess similar $M_{s}$ but smaller $H_{\mathrm{c}}$ compared to larger-sized CIONs.

According to MPI physics theory, these merits of CIONs-22 confer the high peak height and narrow FWHM of the PSF, which explains the reasons for the high sensitivity and resolution generated by CIONs-22 during MPI. Owing to the optimized magnetic properties and efficient cellular internalization of CIONs-22, an extremely low concentration (30 cells $\mathbf{\mu}^{\prime}\mu\mathbf{L}$ ) of CIONs-22-labeled BMSCs can be detected by MPI in vitro.

Most importantly, this modality is also competent under complex in vivo conditions, enabling monitoring of as few as $\sim2500$ cells regardless of the tissue depth. To the best of our knowledge, tracking such few BMSCs represents the highest sensitivity that can be achieved by current MPI tracers, which guarantees accurate, stable, and long-term tracking of therapeutic stem cells.

Moreover, owing to the high sensitivity of CIONs-22-based MPI, we reveal the migration and distribution pattern of transplanted BMSCs given to hindlimb ischemia mice in real time, which cannot be realized by other imaging modalities or commercialized tracer-based MPI. Future work will be necessary to further explore the potential of CIONs-22-based MPI for stem cell tracking in other disease models. Overall, our rationally designed CIONs-22 may inspire the future development of next-generation high-performance MPI tracers to further expand the usefulness of MPI technology.

Instrumentation. TEM images were taken by using a transmission electron microscope (Hitachi HT7700, Japan). Synthesis of Cubic Iron Oxide Nanoparticles with Different Sizes. CIONs were fabricated by the thermal decomposition of iron(III) acetylacetonate. In a typical synthesis of CIONs with an edge length of ${\sim}22~\mathrm{nm}$ (CIONs-22), iron(III) acetylacetonate (0.706 g), 4-biphenylcarboxylic acid $(0.4\ \ g)$ , and oleic acid $\left(1.27~\mathrm{g}\right)$ were added to benzyl ether $(10.4\:\mathrm{g})$ . The mixture solution was magnetically stirred and degassed for $^{\textrm{1h}}$ .

Then it was heated to $290^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ at a heating rate of $20\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\ \operatorname*{min}^{-1}$ under a nitrogen flow and refluxed at $290^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $30~\mathrm{min}$ . After cooling the mixture to room temperature, ethanol was added and the solution was then centrifuged to precipitate nanoparticles. The separated precipitate was dispersed in chloroform. Similarly, other sized CIONs were fabricated by varying the amount of solvent and reaction time.

Synthesis of Spherical Iron Oxide Nanoparticles with Different Sizes. In a typical synthesis of SIONs with a diameter of ${\sim}17~\mathrm{nm}$ (SIONs-17), the iron oleate complexes $(1.85~\mathrm{g})$ synthesized following the previous study were added to a mixture of eicosane (6.5 $\mathbf{g})$ and oleic acid $(0.57\:\mathrm{g})$ .38 The mixture solution was degassed at 100 $^{\circ}{\mathrm{C}}$ for $^{2\mathrm{h}}$ and then heated to $343^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ at a heating rate of $\mathsf{\bar{3}}.3\ \mathrm{{^circC}\ m i n^{-1}}$ with vigorous magnetic stirring under an argon atmosphere.

The reaction mixture was maintained at $343~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $30~\mathrm{min}$ . After cooling the mixture to room temperature, acetone was added and the solution was then centrifuged to precipitate nanoparticles. The separated precipitate was redispersed in chloroform $\mathrm{\Omega}_{\mathrm{\Omega}}\mathrm{10\mL})$ . Similarly, SIONs with a diameter of ${\sim}22~\mathrm{nm}$ were fabricated by using docosane as the solvent and raising the reaction temperature to $380~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ .

Preparation of DSPE- $\mathsf{P E G}_{2000}$ -Encapsulated Iron Oxide Nanoparticles. The resulting CIONs were transferred to aqueous medium following the modification with PEG-phospholipid. Typically, $2~\mathrm{mg}$ of CIONs dispersed in $\mathrm{CHCl}_{3}$ was mixed with $10~\mathrm{mL}$ of $\mathrm{CHCl}_{3}$ con
taining $10\ \mathrm{mg}$ of $\mathrm{DSPE-PEG}_{2000}$ . After sonicating for 5 min, the solvent was then removed by evaporating for $^\textrm{\scriptsize1h}$ at $60~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ Afterward, $4\mathrm{\mL}$ of distilled water was added and excess PEGphospholipid was removed by ultracentrifugation at $5000~\mathrm{rpm}$ for $^{\textrm{1h}}$

MPI Performance Evaluation. MPI performances of magnetic tracers were investigated using the magnetic particle imaging system (Magnetic Insight, Inc., USA). The frequency of MPI is $45\ \mathrm{kHz};$ the magnetic gradient strength of MPI is $\bar{5}.7\ \mathrm{T\m^{-1}}$ . The original data obtained from the MPI scanner were processed by VivoQuant software. The prepared magnetic tracers and commercial MPI tracer Vivotrax were placed in a PCR tube to evaluate their MPI performance.

BMSC Isolation. Animals were maintained under the guidelines of the National Institute Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and all of the animal studies were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhejiang University. Three-week-old male Sprague−Dawley rats (Shanghai SLAC

Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China) were used to isolate BMSCs according to a previously reported method.39

Cell Viability Assay. BMSCs were incubated in low-sugar Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Genom, Hangzhou, China), including $10\%$ fetal bovine serum (Gibco, ThermoFisher, MA, USA) and $1\%$ penicillin/streptomycin (Cienry, Zhejiang, China) at $37^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ in an incubator with a humid atmosphere containing $5\%$ $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ . For cytotoxicity testing, BMSCs were seeded in 96-well culture plates and incubated for $24\mathrm{h}$ . Then, the BMSCs were exposed to 0, 5, 10, 20, 40, 60, and $80\mu\mathrm{g}\mathrm{mL}^{-1}$ of CIONs-22. The proliferation ability of BMSCs labeled with CIONs-22 was evaluated by Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8; Boster, Wuhan, China) assays.

Cellular Internalization. The cellular internalization effect was studied using Prussian blue staining and ICP-MS. First, BMSCs were plated in six-well culture plates and incubated for $24\mathrm{~h~}$ . CIONs-22 with different Fe concentrations of 25, 40, and $60\ \mu\mathrm{g\mL^{-1}}$ were added, respectively. After incubating for $^{4\ \mathrm{h},}$ the cells were washed thrice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with $4\%$ paraformaldehyde for $20~\mathrm{min}$ .

The cells were then incubated in the mixture containing potassium ferrocyanide (5 wt $\%$ ) and hydrochloric acid $10\mathrm{\vol\\%}$ ) for $30~\mathrm{min},$ followed by incubation with nuclear fast red for $10~\mathrm{\min}$ to counterstain the nucleus.

The ICP-MS was performed as follows: after incubating BMSCs with CIONs-22 (0, 25, 40, $60~\mu\mathrm{g~mL}^{-1}$ of Fe) or Vivotrax $\mathrm{\dot{\ell}60~\mu g~m L^{-1}}$ of Fe) for $^{4\mathrm{~h,~}}$ the cells were washed thrice with PBS and centrifuged thrice to remove unbound particles, followed by the addition of $1~\mathrm{mL}$ of nitric acid $(65-68\%)$ to each tube and allowing to stand for $24\mathrm{h}$ . The solutions were then diluted to $10~\mathrm{mL}$ with distilled water.

BMSC Labeling and Imaging Performance in Vitro. BMSCs were seeded on $100~\mathrm{{mm}}$ culture dishes and incubated for $24\mathrm{h}$ . Then, CIONs-22 or Vivotrax was added into each dish at the same iron concentration $(60~\mu\mathrm{g}~\mathrm{mL}^{-1}$ of Fe), respectively. After co-incubation for ${4\mathrm{h}},$ the cells were washed thrice with PBS and centrifuged thrice to remove unbound particles. Afterward, the labeled BMSCs were divided into a series of cell numbers, and $50~\mu\mathrm{L}$ of $1\%$ agarose solution was added to stabilize the cells.

MPI images of labeled BMSCs were collected on the MPI scanner. MPS signal efficacy values of CIONs-22- and Vivotrax-labeled BMSCs ( $\mathrm{\bar{1}}\times10^{\mathrm{\bar{6}}}$ BMSCs in $50\mu\mathrm{L}$ of $1\%$ agarose solution) were determined by a physical property measurement system (PPMS-9T, Quantum Design, USA). For T2- weighted MR images, cells in $200\mu\mathrm{L}$ PCR tube were stabilized by150 $\mu\mathrm{L}$ of $1\%$ agarose. The cells were imaged using the $9.0\mathrm{T}$ MRI scanner as mentioned above: $\mathrm{TR}=2500~\mathrm{ms}$ , $\mathrm{\bar{TE}}=75\mathrm{\ms}.

$ , field of view (FOV) $\mathit{\Omega}=30\times18\mathrm{\mm}^{2}.$ , matrix $=256\times256,$ and slice thickness/ $\prime\mathrm{gap}=0.8$ $\mathrm{mm}/0.8~\mathrm{mm}$ .

BMSC Tracking in Vivo. Different numbers of Vivotrax-labeled BMSCs and CIONs-22-labeled BMSCs were subcutaneously injected onto the back of mice. The visualization of labeled BMSCs was performed on the MPI scanner. Vivotrax-labeled BMSCs (cell numbers of ${\sim}100000$ and ${\sim}300000\rangle$ and CIONs-22-labeled BMSCs (cell number of ${\sim}100000$ ) were i.v. injected into 6-weekold BALB/c mice, respectively. MPI images were collected $3\mathrm{~h~}$ after i.v. injection to track Vivotrax-labeled BMSCs with cell numbers of ${\sim}100000$ and ${\sim}300000$ .

Tracking CIONs-22-labeled BMSCs in mice after a series of times $(20\ \mathrm{min},\ \mathrm{i}\ \mathrm{h},\ 3\ \mathrm{h},\ 24\ \mathrm{h},\ 3\ \mathrm{d},\ 5\ \mathrm{d},\ 7\ \mathrm{d})$ was achieved using the MPI technique. To longitudinally track CIONs-22- labeled BMSCs via MRI, ${\sim}300000$ cells were i.v. injected into the mice because there are almost no signal changes after i.v. injection of ${\sim}100000$ cells.

T2-weighted MR images were acquired using the 9.0 T MR scanner before and after the injection: $\mathrm{TR}=2500~\mathrm{ms}$ , $\mathrm{TE}=15$ ms, $\mathrm{FOV}=45\times36~\mathrm{mm}^{2}$ , matrix $=256\times256,$ , slice thickness/ $\mathtt{\partial g a p}=$ $0.8~\mathrm{mm}/0.16~\mathrm{mm}$ .

BMSC Tracking in Hindlimb Ischemia Mice Models. Sevenweek-old male BALB/c mice purchased from Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China) were used for ischemia hindlimb model establishment. Briefly, the left femoral artery of the mouse was ligated proximal to the origin of the deep femoral artery branch through a small skin incision. After model establishment, Vivotrax-labeled BMSCs and CIONs-22-labeled BMSCs with cell numbers of $1\times10^{6}$ were intramuscularly injected into the gastrocnemius of ischemic or nonischemic mice, respectively. Then, the labeled BMSCs were tracked in mice after a series of times (0 d, 1 d, 3 d, 6 d, 9 d) using the MPI-CT technique.
