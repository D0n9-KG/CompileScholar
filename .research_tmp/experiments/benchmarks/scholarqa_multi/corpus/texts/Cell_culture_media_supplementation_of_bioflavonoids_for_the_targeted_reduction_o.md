# Cell Culture Media Supplementation of Bioflavonoids for the Targeted Reduction of Acidic Species Charge Variants on Recombinant Therapeutic Proteins

Patrick Hossler, Min Wang, Sean McDermott, Christopher Racicot, Kofi Chemfe, Yun Zhang, Christopher Chumsae, and Anton Manuilov

Process Sciences, Global Pharmaceutical Operations, AbbVie Bioresearch Center, Worcester, MA 01605

DOI 10.1002/btpr.2095

Published online May 27, 2015 in Wiley Online Library (wileyonlinelibrary.com)

Charge variants in recombinant proteins are an important series of protein modifications, whose potential role on protein stability, activity, immunogenicity, and pharmacokinetics continues to be studied. Monoclonal antibodies in particular have been shown to have a wide range of acidic species variants, including those associated with the addition of covalent modifications as well as the chemical degradation at specific peptide regions on the antibody. These variants play a significant role toward the overall heterogeneity of recombinant therapeutic proteins and are typically monitored during manufacturing to ensure they lie within proven acceptable ranges. In this work, it has been found that the supplementation of members of the bioflavonoid chemical family into mammalian cell culture media was effective toward the reduction of acidic species charge variants on recombinant monoclonal antibodies and dual variable domain immunoglobulins. The demonstrated reduction in acidic species through the use of bioflavonoids facilitates the manufacturing of a less heterogeneous product with potential improvements in antibody structure and function. © 2015 American Institute of Chemical Engineers Biotechnol. Keywords: charge heterogeneity, acidic species, mammalian cell culture, bioflavonoids, EGCG

# Introduction

Heterogeneity in recombinant protein therapeutics, especially antibodies, is a common occurrence. This heterogeneity is manifested through many different types of modifications, as has been reviewed elsewhere. $^{1,2}$ Charge heterogeneity in particular is typically observed on most proteins, and originates with various chemical degradation mechanisms (oxidation, deamidation, isomerization, and fragmentation) and addition reactions (glycation adducts or covalent additions) that can occur at various locations on the protein. The cumulative effect of these molecular events is structural and conformational changes on the molecule and an apparent impact on the molecule's isoelectric pH value, and a potential impact to the protein's function. Acidic species variants are those variants that tend to bestow a net negative charge on the protein, and/or the removal of additional positive charges. The various categories of acidic species variants have been reviewed elsewhere. $^{3}$

There have been numerous reports of acidic species variants having more than just a nominal impact on the resulting protein. It has been postulated that the degree of impact of a particular charge variant is dependent on where on the protein the alteration occurred, and the extent to which the protein has been modified. Changes to either the $F_{c}$ or $F_{ab}$ region of an antibody may be impactful depending on the physiochemical parameter of interest. $^{3}$ Charge variants have been shown to affect the in vitro and in vivo binding characteristics of antibodies. $^{4,5}$ It has been shown in recombinant antibodies for example, that asparagine deamidation can facilitate a significant decrease in antigen binding. $^{6,7}$ Glycation has been shown to increase the formation of aggregates. $^{8}$ In addition, it has been shown with an enriched pool of acidic species from an IgG1 antibody, there was a lower F $_{c}$ R $_{n}$ binding response, despite demonstrating similar in vivo pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters. $^{9}$ Thus, there is sufficient evidence in the literature to suggest that the presence of acidic species variants on therapeutic proteins has at least the potential to have an impact on the resulting protein's efficacy, and function. As a result, the control of their levels, typically at relatively low abundance, is an important goal during the manufacturing of these proteins.

There have been some published reports of how the acidic species profile on recombinant proteins can be controlled. Studies have shown how acidic species variants are dependent upon the local environment in which the proteins are produced, as well as the environment in which they are kept in solution after purification. Bioflavonoids and polyphenolic compounds are commonly found in nature such as in apples, $^{13}$ and especially in green tea. $^{14}$ However, they are typically not used as cell culture media supplements. They are however present in human consumption since they have low toxicity. Molecules in the flavonoid chemical family are associated with polyphenolic ring chemical structures, with ketone and hydroxyl groups. Some representative examples of bioflavonoids are highlighted in Figure 1A. Bioflavonoids have been extensively studied to have both pro-oxidant and antioxidant activity in vitro. It is this latter function that has been an ongoing interest in the investigation of this class of molecules ability to prevent cancer. $^{15}$ Indeed, they have demonstrated the ability to scavenge a wide range of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and reactive nitrogen species (RNS). $^{16}$ In addition, they have also been proven to chelate metal ions, thus decreasing the pro-oxidant behavior of these metals. $^{16}$

Bioflavonoids also have a well-studied and proven role towards being pro-oxidants as well, especially epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG). The formation of these pro-oxidant byproducts is principally important because they actually lead to more ROS. $^{13,17}$ The formation of ROS and RNS in typical industrial mammalian cell culture is to be expected because of the relatively high dissolved oxygen levels that are typically employed, essentially ensuring that the culture environment is always under oxidative stress. $^{16}$ The pro-oxidant behavior of bioflavonoids is principally mediated through $H_{2}O_{2}$ , which forms as an oxidation byproduct, and is highly dependent on a multitude of factors. These factors include whether or not cells are present in the media, $^{13}$ the particular media used, and the relative levels of trace metals in the media of choice. $^{16}$ It is also dependent on the particular bioflavonoid used, as has been shown with rutin, which causes a considerably lower amount of $H_{2}O_{2}$ . $^{13}$

Under elevated oxidative stress conditions, mammalian cells respond with an oxidative stress response. In humans, the induction of an antioxidant defense is designed to not remove all ROS, but to control their levels at manageable levels so as to manage the potential for oxidative damage. $^{16}$ In cultured mammalian cells, there is a similar defense mechanism, as has been shown through the induction of the glutathione pathway, which helps facilitate an overall reduction in the levels of ROS. $^{13}$

In this work, it has been found that the supplementation of various members of the bioflavonoid family of molecules facilitated a significant decrease in the overall levels of acidic species variants on recombinant therapeutic proteins. EGCG and rutin were especially effective toward the overall reduction in acidic species charge variants. Bioflavonoid supplementation into cell culture media provides an effective means with which to reduce acidic species charge variants, as well as to control an emerging critical product quality attribute of interest.

# Materials and Methods

# Cell culture

Four different recombinant Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cell lines expressing four different recombinant glycoproteins were evaluated in either shaker flask culture, or 3L laboratory-scale bioreactors. Cell Line 1 was genetically engineered to express Antibody 1, Cell Line 2 was genetically engineered to express Antibody 2, Cell Line 3 was genetically engineered to express dual variable domain immunoglobulin 1 (DVD1) and Cell Line 4 was genetically engineered to express dual variable domain immunoglobulin 2 (DVD2). DVD1 and DVD2 were immunoglobulins with two variable domains as documented previously. $^{[18]}$ Antibody 1 and Antibody 2 were both IgG1 glycoproteins. All four cell lines were of CHO DUX-B11 origin based on a dhfr (dihydrofolate reductase) expression system, and cultured in the same chemically defined basal media, and fed periodically with the same chemically defined feed media, but at different media enrichment levels. All media used throughout all of the experiments were chemically defined, and both animal-component free, and protein-free. Each of the respective feed media from each experiment were supplemented as needed with select bioflavonoids to evaluate for their potential impact on the resulting acidic species profile. In preparation of the cultures, the cell lines were serially expanded through separate seed train inoculums to generate enough cells for inoculation. Process conditions used during the cultures were slightly different depending on the culture scale, but were kept similar at each scale to the respective nonbioflavonoid supplemented control conditions (Table 1). All bioflavonoids used were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO).

Viable cell density (VCD) and cell viability values were measured through trypan blue exclusion via Cedex automated cell counters (Roche Applied Science, Indianapolis, IN), glucose and lactate values were measured with an ABL-805 (Radiometer Medical, Denmark) blood gas analyzer. Offline pH, dissolved oxygen (DO), and $pCO_{2}$ measurements were performed as needed with an ABL-805 (Radiometer Medical, Denmark) blood gas analyzer. Osmolality was measured as needed on a Multi-Osmette 2430 osmometer (Precision Systems, Natick, MA).

# Protein A affinity chromatography

Antibody titers were measured from crude cell culture harvests on a Poros A $^{TM}$ (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) affinity column using an HPLC system operating with a low pH, step elution gradient with detection at 280 nm. Absolute concentrations were assigned with respect to reference standard calibration curves.

Purified antibodies subjected to additional analytical characterization were purified using MabSelect $^{TM}$ Protein A (GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ) using a low pH, step elution gradient, followed by buffer exchange (when needed) using Corning Lifesciences (Tewksbury, MA) Spin Concentrator X UF columns, or equivalent, according to the manufacturers' recommended procedures.

# Charge heterogeneity via weak cation exchange chromatography

Samples were analyzed using an HPLC system equipped with a ProPac WCX-10 analytical column (Thermo Scientific, Sunnyvale, CA). Sufficient sample amounts were injected and eluted from the column using an elution gradient comprised of 10 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.5), and 10 mM sodium phosphate, 500 mM NaCl (pH 5.5) as mobile phases A and B, respectively. The column was run at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The protein peaks were measured using UV absorbance at 280 nm as the peaks eluted from the column. The column was regenerated using 100% buffer B followed by a re-equilibration period before the injection of the next sample. Peaks eluting before the main peak were termed “acidic peaks” and peaks eluting after the main peak were termed ‘basic peaks’ (Figure 1B). All chromatograms were generated with consistent resolution with a diverse range of overlapping acidic peaks as that, which has been shown elsewhere. $^{9}$ Unless specified otherwise, the acidic, main, and basic species of experimental samples were expressed as the absolute percentage change with respect to the unsupplemented control. In this work, these experimental conditions included the selective culture media supplementation of bioflavonoids, which led to significant decreases in the acidic species profile (Figure 1C).

# Tandem protein A-weak cation exchange chromatography with pH gradient

Clarified cell culture supernatants were acidified with 2% (v/v) acetic acid, incubated for 60 min at room temperature and neutralized with NaOH prior to the tandem Protein A – WCX analysis. An Agilent quaternary HPLC (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) equipped with POROS® A20 column (Life Technologies, Foster City, CA; 30 × 2.1 mm ID; PEEK); ProPac™ WCX-10 column (Thermo Fischer Scientific, Waltham,

MA; 250 × 4 mm ID; PEEK); and two Agilent 3/6 switch valves for directing the flow between the two columns were used to analyze samples. 200 μg of each sample was loaded onto the POROS column, washed with 100% mobile phase A (1× PBS, 1 mM NaN3, pH 6.8) for 3 min at 2 mL/min with the outflow directed to waste, and eluted with 100% mobile phase B (0.1 M acetic acid, pH 3.5) in 1 min at 1 mL/min onto the WCX column. Then, the POROS column was bypassed, while the sample-loaded WCX was subjected to pH gradient elution. First, the WCX column was washed with 100% mobile phase C (CryoBioPhysica, Silver Spring, MD; pISep buffer, pH 5.5) for 4 min and further washed with 55% mobile phase C and 45% mobile phase D (pISep buffer, pH 10.8) for 5 min. Next, the sample was eluted with a linear gradient from 55% C and 45% D to 15% C and 85% D. The column temperature of the POROS column was kept ambient, while the oven temperature for WCX column was set to 40°C. UV detection at 280 nm was performed.

When needed, an Agilent fraction collector was used to isolate the protein from the AR and main peak of WCX for the following LC/MS analysis. The collected samples were concentrated using centrifugal filter units (EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA) and subjected to PNGase F (ProZyme, Hayward, CA) digestion for 16 h at 37°C to remove N-glycans. The samples were additionally alkylated with iodoacetamide (12.5 mM final concentration) for 2 h at 37°C when a comparison of disulfide bond stability and free thiols was required.

# Liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry

An Agilent HPLC (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) equipped with a Protein MicroTrap $^{TM}$ desalting column (Bruker, Billerica, MA; 8 × 1 mm ID) and Agilent Q-TOF mass spectrometer (Agilent) were used to analyze samples; 1–2 $\mu$ g of each sample was loaded at 95% mobile phase A (0.08% FA and 0.02% TFA in Milli-Q water) and 5% mobile phase B (0.08% FA and 0.02% TFA in acetonitrile) and then eluted using a linear gradient from 5 to 95% mobile phase B in 10 min. The flow rate was set at 50 $\mu$ L/min and column oven temperature was set to 40°C. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive mode with a scan range of m/z 600 to 3200. Ion spray voltage was set at 4750 volts, and the source temperature was set at 350°C. The data was processed using the Agilent MassHunter software.

# Hydrophilic interaction chromatography

Samples were measured with an HPLC system equipped with a 3 $\mu$ m, TSK gel Amide-80 column (Tosoh Bioscience, King of Prussia, PA). Sufficient sample amounts were injected and eluted from the column using an elution gradient comprised of acetonitrile and 100 mM ammonium formate (pH 4.4) as mobile phases A and B, respectively. The column was controlled at 35°C, and operated with a flowrate of 1 mL/min with UV absorbance at 280 nm. The elution peak corresponding to EGCG was identified through spiking in EGCG standards.

# Statistics

Experimental results are expressed as mean $\pm 1$ SD for those results generated from at least 3 independent cultures. Experimental results are expressed as the mean value for those results generated from <3 independent cultures. Results were evaluated for statistical significance (when needed) through 2-sided t-tests, with a requirement of P < 0.05 relative to the unsupplemented control conditions.

# Results

# Impact of EGCG supplementation on acidic species of various recombinant proteins

Three different recombinant proteins were expressed in three different CHO cell lines in the presence and absence of EGCG. Various concentrations were titrated in shake flask cultures to evaluate for its impact on cell culture performance, as well as the relative levels of acidic species. Cell Line 1 was cultured in chemically defined media and fed with feed media supplemented with 0.005, 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, and 0.2 g/L EGCG (Figure 2). Across the respective concentrations, there was no adverse impact to either cell growth or viability. In fact, in all of the EGCG supplemented cultures there was a statistically meaningful increase in cell growth. There was also no adverse impact on harvest titers, with the EGCG supplemented cultures demonstrating at most a 7% increase compared with the unsupplemented control. However, there was a very pronounced impact on the relative levels of acidic species variants. Figure 2D shows that EGCG had a concentration dependent impact on the AR levels, with an increasing level of overall AR level reduction across the respective EGCG concentrations. At the concentrations of 0.005, 0.02, and 0.05 g/L, respectively, EGCG reduced the total acidic species content 0.1, 1, and 3% compared with the unsupplemented control, respectively. Both the 0.1 and 0.2 g/L EGCG conditions facilitated the largest overall reduction in AR levels, with a 4% reduction. This drop in AR constituted a very large fraction of the absolute AR level typically observed on this particular antibody.

To further evaluate the impact of EGCG on acidic species variants, Antibody 2 expressed from CHO cell line 2 was evaluated in shaker flask cultures. This particular cell line was a much higher grower compared with CHO cell line 1 with peak VCD's at $\sim 25 \times 10^{6}$ cells/mL (Supporting Information Figure S1). Of particular interest in this study was whether or not with this particularly healthy amount of cell growth would the AR reduction behavior observed with cell line 1 be conserved. EGCG at 0.05 and $0.1\mathrm{g / L}$ did not have any adverse impact toward cell growth or cell viability. Harvest titers were also approximately the same. Upon measurement of the acidic species variants, EGCG reduced the total acidic species in a concentration-dependent manner. At the concentrations of 0.05 and $0.1\mathrm{g / L}$ , respectively, EGCG reduced the total acidic species content in absolute levels by 3 and $5\%$ , respectively, compared with the unsupplemented control. These results suggest that this particular bioflavonoid is effective at AR reduction regardless of whether the cell line is a high or average grower.

To assess the impact of EGCG on AR variants on proteins different than monoclonal antibodies, dual variable domain immunoglobulin 1 (DVD1) was examined. EGCG was added at the concentrations of 0.02, 0.05, 0.1, and $0.2\mathrm{g / L}$ into the feed media in a shaker flask experiment (Supporting Information Figure S2). With the exception of the $0.2\mathrm{g / L}$ condition, all EGCG supplemented cultures grew similarly to the unsupplemented culture. Species that differ in higher order structure may also migrate in the AR. Although LC/MS is a sensitive analytical tool capable of showing AR differences qualitatively, it was not sufficiently quantitative to demonstrate a predominant AR subspecies cause for a $<5\%$ decrease in the collective AR which is further distributed amongst all the possible acidic species. It is possible that some of these particular acidic species may not survive the LC/MS analysis, for example, the aldimine form of glycation (Schiff base). Secondly, the AR species of interest may be associated with cysteine residues, but the sample preparation of the LC/MS and the peptide map may mask it. Lastly, it is plausible that some of the AR species of interest may be a noncovalent association with the antibody, which is reduced when EGCG is added to the culture media. What is clear, however, is that the entire AR is going down in the EGCG treated cultures suggesting that there is likely to be a mixture of different effects, including perhaps higher order structure, and no one analytical method will be able to discern all of them.

# Impact of rutin supplementation on acidic species of various recombinant proteins

In the aforementioned studies, the bioflavonoid EGCG was found to be very effective towards the reduction of acidic species variants on recombinant proteins (Antibody 1, Antibody 2, and DVD1). However, it was uncertain whether or not additional bioflavonoids would also be effective in this regard. To evaluate the impact of alternative bioflavonoids, fed-batch shake flask cultures of Cell Line 1 were initiated with chemically defined feed media supplemented with multiple concentrations of rutin. Cell growth, viability, as well as titers and acidic species variants were measured and shown in Figure 4.

From the cell growth curves of Cell Line 1 one can see that over a range of concentrations there was not a significantly adverse impact on culture performance. However, at rutin concentrations above 0.05 g/L in the feed media, cell growth was adversely impacted. Peak VCD values decreased from $10.3 \times 10^{6}$ cells/mL for the unsupplemented control down to $9.0 \times 10^{6}$ cells/mL and $6.9 \times 10^{6}$ cells/mL for the 0.1 and 1.0 g/L supplemented cultures, respectively. Cell viability results were also comparable across the duration of each of the cultures. Only the 1 g/L rutin condition demonstrated a reduction in viability, with harvest values dropping to 76% compared with 79% for the unsupplemented control; only a nominal decrease. Harvest titers also demonstrated a nominal decrease across the range of tested concentrations. From 0.01 to 0.1 g/L rutin there was only a drop in relative titer from 0.98 to 0.93, respectively. The 1 g/L condition demonstrated the largest drop in relative titer to 0.78, which was statistically significant. Upon measurement of the acidic species variants on Antibody 1 from the various cultures, it was found that in each of the evaluated cases, there was a significant drop in acidic species variants. The magnitude of the drop was directly proportional to the amount of rutin in the feed media. The absolute percentage drop in acidic species variants from each of these cultures was measured to be 0.6% with the 0.1 g/L rutin culture to 6.2% for the 1 g/L rutin supplemented culture. The decrease in AR was concomitant with a corresponding increase in the levels of the main and basic species.

Cell Line 4 was also evaluated for its process performance impact upon exposure to rutin (Figure 5). From the cell growth curves, one can see that over a wide range of concentrations there was not a significantly adverse impact on culture performance. However, at rutin concentrations above 0.1 g/L in the chemically defined feed media, cell growth was adversely impacted. Peak VCD values decreased from $12.4 \times 10^{6}$ cells/mL for the unsupplemented control down to $11.0 \times 10^{6}$ cells/mL for the 1.0 g/L supplemented cultures, a nominal, but significant decrease. Cell viability results were also very comparable across the duration of each of the cultures, with the measured values being essentially superimposable against each other. In contrast to Cell Line 1, harvest titers actually increased across the range of tested concentrations. From 0.01 to 1 g/L rutin there was a statistically significant increase in relative titer from 1.03 to 1.09. Upon measurement of the acidic species variants on DVD2 from the various cultures, it was found that in each of the evaluated cases, there was a significant drop in acidic species variants. This decrease ranged from 8.4% for the 0.1 g/L condition to 14.3% for the 0.05 g/L condition. The decrease in total AR was concomitant with an increase in the levels of the main and basic species. Interestingly, the reduction in AR across the rutin concentrations evaluated did not have as clear of a trend as compared to the other cell line. This was most likely due to the particular nature of the protein structure of DVD2, as well as the fact that this protein had the largest starting levels of AR of any of the four recombinant proteins evaluated in this work. The fact that there was less of a trend in the AR results, and with more variability, suggests that the evaluated rutin concentrations may also be supporting a decrease in AR that is close to the point of maximum AR decrease that can be expected based on this approach.

# Bioflavonoids attenuate the formation of acidic species charge variants by attenuating process influences

In the previous section, it was highlighted how process conditions may have a contributory role towards helping to shape the relative acidic species profile at harvest. Principal among these include the harvest timing. 3L laboratory-scale bioreactors were utilized to produce Antibody 1. Purified sample retains were then measured for the resulting AR levels across time, up to 15 days postinoculation for both EGCG and rutin supplemented cultures, and compared to the unsupplemented control (Figure 8). These results were expressed as the absolute percentage change compared to the AR level measured on Day 8 for the unsupplemented control. For the control culture, there was not much impact on the total acidic species up to process day 12. After this day, the AR level started creeping upwards, until the culture was harvested on day 15, with the final AR level reaching +6% compared with the earlier point in the culture. These results were also consistent with a drop in cell viability over this same time duration. Intuitively, this is certainly in line with what one would anticipate regarding cell viability impacts towards the AR profile. When cells die, and their intracellular contents are released into the culture milieu, those various proteins, enzymes, and redox-influencing compounds certainly have the potential to impact the various chemical species that comprise the acidic species charge variants of the expressed protein. Since the antibody in this case was at g/L level quantities in the culture media over this entire duration, and is the most abundant protein in the culture milieu, the lysed intracellular contents certainly would not have a hard time finding a great target which which to react upon.

To further investigate the potential interaction of bioflavonoids with culture media components, a series of cell culture experiments were undertaken to ascertain if media compounds known by the authors to be complicit towards increasing AR levels on Antibody 1 could be mitigated upon exposure to EGCG and rutin. A confirmatory result for AR reduction would reinforce the notion that these bioflavonoids can in fact mitigate the media specific contribution towards overall AR levels; in this case, brought upon by these pro-AR forming compounds. Transition metals such as iron have been shown in the literature to be implicated in the formation of hydrogen peroxide and subsequently hydroxyl free radicals, an important source of ROS. In one published report it was found that the formation of ROS through the impact of iron can cause cleavage of proteins. $^{21}$ Ferric citrate is commonly added to cell culture media in relatively abundant quantities as a critical source of iron, and was previously known by the authors to also be implicated in the formation of acidic species variants on the particular recombinant protein evaluated (Antibody 1). This direct relationship of iron and the subsequent levels of acidic species has also been highlighted elsewhere. $^{22}$ Ascorbic acid has also been shown in the literature to have a dramatic effect on proteins. In one published, report it was found in transferrin, that ascorbic acid supplementation led to an increase in the formation of hydrogen peroxide, as well as the subsequent breaking of peptide chains, and deamination of the resulting peptides. $^{23}$ Ascorbic acid is commonly added to cell culture media, and it too was previously known by the authors to be implicated in the formation of acidic species on Antibody 1. Since both ascorbate and ferric citrate have been previously shown to be implicated in the formation of hydrogen peroxide, an important compound in the ROS reaction pathways, and bioflavonoids have been implicated as potent in vitro antioxidants, it would stand to reason that setting them against each other would provide some confirmatory behavioral clues for the bioflavonoid mechanism towards mitigating the contribution of AR promoting cell culture media compounds.

# Conclusions

In this work, it has been found that supplementation of bioflavonoids into mammalian cell culture feed media is capable of significantly decreasing the overall levels of acidic species charge variants on multiple recombinant proteins expressed by multiple cell lines. Shake flask studies evaluating the feed media supplementation of EGCG and rutin were all effective in this regard, suggesting that this behavior may be a feature of this class of molecules in general. These results were consistently observed at multiple

concentrations, across different cell lines and cell culture scales, and in a statistically meaningful manner. The resulting decrease in acidic species variants facilitated a concomitant increase in the main species to a larger degree, and the basic species to a lesser degree.

The bioflavonoids did not have an adverse impact on overall cell culture performance over a wide range of concentrations, but only up to a particular concentration. Once the tested concentrations exceeded a critical level, observations of lower cell growth were observed. For EGCG, a concentration of 0.5 g/L was found in 3L bioreactors to be the point at which the peak VCD of Cell Line 1 dropped lower than the unsupplemented control conditions. In Cell Line 3, 0.2 g/L was found to be the concentration, which adversely impacted cell growth. In the rutin supplementation studies, a concentration of 1 g/L was found to be the point at which the peak viable cell densities dropped lower than the unsupplemented control conditions for Cell Lines 1 and 2, respectively. Cell viability results for the most part remained similar throughout the duration of the bioflavonoid supplemented cultures compared to the unsupplemented control cultures. In those instances in which the bioflavonoid concentrations were at the highest tested, cell viability results were nominally lower at harvest compared to the control cultures.

Recombinant protein productivity was also only nominally impacted through the culture supplementation of these bioflavonoids. For Cell Line 3, harvest titers dropped by at most 8% for EGCG concentrations up to 0.1 g/L. At higher concentrations, including 0.2 g/L, EGCG dropped harvest titers by $\sim$ 28%. For the rutin supplemented cultures, harvest titers dropped by at most 22% for Cell Line 1 fed with media supplemented to 1 g/L. At this same rutin concentration, harvest titers actually increased by 9% for Cell Line 4. Thus, it would appear that there are also cell line differences in their responses towards these bioflavonoids, with some cell lines responding more strongly than others.

In each of the aforementioned studies it was demonstrated that regardless of the CHO cell line, protein expressed, culture scale, or media supplementation strategy, both EGCG and rutin were able to significantly decrease the relative levels of acidic species variants. In shake flask culture, Cell Lines 1, 2, and 3 were able to demonstrate maximum AR reductions of 7, 5, and 7%, respectively, across the evaluated EGCG concentrations. With rutin supplementation, Cell Lines 1 and 4 were able to demonstrate maximum AR reductions of 6 and 14%, respectively. These changes were significant and constitute very large overall changes in the total population of the AR profile.

It is not known with certainty the exact molecular mechanism these bioflavonoids are utilizing to decrease total AR levels. However, it is well known that the presence of ROS may impact the physiochemical attributes of expressed proteins. $^{24}$ In addition, it has also been shown that the presence of ROS can have a direct impact on the levels of acidic species variants on a monoclonal antibody. $^{12}$ It is plausible that bioflavonoids, and their well-documented antioxidant activities, are affecting overall ROS levels, and preventing their late-stage culture increase. By preventing ROS levels from reaching significantly high levels as the cells are dying in late-stage culture, bioflavonoids may be potentially impacting the subsequent levels of acidic species charge variants. It is also quite plausible that the evaluated bioflavonoids are also interacting, directly or indirectly, with AR promoting compounds in the cell culture media, thereby mitigating their positive contribution towards final AR levels. Upon inspection of the weak cation exchange chromatograms of purified Antibody 1, and in particular, the acidic species region, it was readily apparent that the entire chromatogram region of the AR decreased upon the cultures supplemented with a bioflavonoid. Since acidic species variants have been shown by previous researchers to be comprised of a mixture of various modifications, it can be reasonably concluded that these bioflavonoids are impacting a diverse mixture of different charge variants, and no particular predominant species. As a result, no one particular analytical method can be used to discern all of them. LC/MS analysis of purified samples were however performed with the results indicating that there were no predominant differences in AR subspecies that could account for the cumulative AR difference between EGCG supplemented, and control cultures. These results suggest that the decreased levels of acidic species variants facilitated through the use of bioflavonoids may be partially due to species that LC/MS cannot discern, and/or through changes in higher order structure.

The effective media concentration range of EGCG for reducing acidic species charge variants could be cell line-dependent and/or perhaps dependent on the total amount of AR promoting components in the culture media, or produced by the cells in culture. There are multiple means with which cells can be exposed to these bioflavonoids, including media supplementation to either the basal or feed media. In addition, since these compounds are typically found in nature, other compounds, commodities, or mixtures, which have these bioflavonoids as constituent components, may be equally effective at reducing total AR compared with the purified compounds evaluated in these studies. In each of these scenarios, the supplementation concentration could vary due to the highly reactive nature of these bioflavonoids in solution. $^{20}$

In summary, in this work, we claim for the first time an effective and efficient approach towards the reduction of acidic species charge variants through the use of bioflavonoids as media supplements in the context of industrial bioprocessing. Although 2 bioflavonoids were specifically demonstrated, other members of this chemical family may also support a reduction in acidic species in some instances. It is quite possible a combination of multiple bioflavonoid molecules would have a more effective, and synergistic impact in reducing acidic species charge variants. It also stands to reason that with such a pronounced impact on the AR profile of recombinant proteins expressed from mammalian cells in vitro, there may also be an impact on cells in vivo.
