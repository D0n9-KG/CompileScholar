# What Kind of Social Preparations Are Needed for Xenotransplantation? A Survey Study of the Japanese General Public

Ayako Kamisato | Satoshi Hosoya

National Center for Child Health and Development, Setagaya-ku, Tokyo, Japan

# ABSTRACT

Introduction: With the advent of genome editing technology, xenotransplantation has been attracting attention in recent years as a potential solution to the shortage of organs for transplantation. In Japan, several Japanese universities have reportedly been preparing for the first Japanese clinical trial. However, xenotransplantation poses social issues. Therefore, it is necessary to understand the public's awareness and opinions of it, and policymakers and researchers of xenotransplantation must prepare for social issues.

Materials and Methods: In December 2024, an online survey was conducted on Japanese aged 20 to 79, asking questions about their awareness, acceptability, and preferred transplant method. IBM SPSS was used for statistical analysis, and correlations with attributes were also examined.

Results: Valid responses were obtained from 3209 people (response rate: 10.8%). Only 34.6% knew the meaning of xenotransplantation, and 53.8% had a favorable opinion about xenotransplantation being performed as a medical treatment in the future. However, 77.0% predicted that they would feel uncomfortable if a doctor suggested xenotransplantation to them, and 60.9% predicted that they would decide not to undergo xenotransplantation. Fifty-eight percent responded that they would be anxious about discrimination, and 88.2% responded that they would be anxious about being infected with animal-derived pathogens if they received a xenotransplant. In addition, only 1.7% ranked xenotransplantation as their first preference.

Conclusion: The results imply the Japanese public is not ready enough to accept xenotransplantation. This study proposes four points that policymakers and researchers should prepare for social issues before the clinical trial begins in Japan.

# 1 | Introduction

Increasing life expectancy has resulted in a higher number of patients with severe organ failure. Organ transplantation is an effective treatment for those patients, but a serious global shortage of organs for transplantation prevents many patients from receiving transplant care. In the United States, more than 48 000 transplant procedures were performed in 2024. However, the transplant waiting list still exceeds 100 000 patients, and an average of 13 patients die each day while waiting [1]. In Japan, almost 580 transplant surgeries were performed in 2024, but the number of patients on the waiting list exceeded 16 800 at the end of that year [2].

One patient survived for nine months after transplantation, but most patients died within one month because of issues with the size of the donor animal's organs and immune rejection $[3–5]$ . However, with the advent of genome editing technology, progress has been made in developing pigs that are less likely to cause immune rejection and that do not contain pig-specific pathogens, and the implementation and research of xenotransplantation have accelerated again in recent years $[6]$ . Even looking just at cases performed from 2021 to 2024 in the United States, many significant developments have occurred. For example, trials of pig kidney transplants were conducted on two brain-dead patients at New York University Langone Medical Center in September and November 2021 $[7–8]$ and on one brain-dead patient at the University of Alabama in January 2022 $[9]$ . A pig heart transplant was performed on a patient at the University of Maryland Medical Center in January 2022 $[10]$ , with a second transplant at the same university in September 2023 $[11]$ . In March 2024, a pig kidney transplant was performed on a patient at Massachusetts General Hospital $[12]$ . The following month, a simultaneous pig kidney and thymus transplant was performed at New York University Langone Medical Center $[13]$ , and in November, a pig kidney was transplanted at the same center $[14]$ . Soon after 2025, a pig kidney transplant was also performed at Massachusetts General Hospital. It was hoped that this case would surpass the previous longest record of nine months for a xenotransplant, but just before breaking the record, the transplanted kidney failed and was removed $[15]$ . All transplants to non-brain-dead patients were performed in the US Single Patient Expanded Access system. However, in February 2025, the US Food and Drug Administration approved the first clinical trial to transplant a pig kidney into 30 patients, so the number of cases performed will likely increase in the near future $[16]$ . If the clinical trial demonstrates clinical efficacy, xenotransplantation might be a clinical option for patients with severe organ failure, contributing to resolving the imbalance between supply and demand for organ transplants.

On the other hand, as an animal organ is transplanted into a human body, there is a risk that the patient could become infected with a pathogen of animal origin, or that the pathogen could be transmitted from the patient to someone close to them, or even to the public $[17]$ . In other words, it is not sufficient for the patient alone to decide whether to accept the risk, as is usually the case in medical care or clinical trials $[18–20]$ . To minimize these risks, the transplanted patient must be placed under long-term public health surveillance $[21–23]$ . However, there is also the question of whether the patient's freedom to withdraw consent should be guaranteed $[24–26]$ .

Thus, because xenotransplantation involves different social issues than allogeneic transplantation, public opinion on whether xenotransplantation should be performed, as well as the conditions under which it should occur, is also important. In this context, the Changsha Communiqué, which was developed at the first WHO Global Consultation on the Regulatory Requirements for Xenotransplantation Clinical Trials held in 2008, also required public involvement in establishing xenotransplantation regulations $[27]$ . We have recently experienced the COVID-19 pandemic, which remains fresh in our memories. In a survey conducted in Japan in 2023, after the pandemic, more than 50% of respondents reported greater awareness of the danger of infectious diseases compared to before the spread of COVID-19 [28]. This finding suggests the importance of incorporating the public's post-pandemic opinions into regulation. However, because xenotransplantation has advanced rapidly in recent years, public engagement has not kept pace [29–32], and only a few previous studies on public awareness surveys have been conducted worldwide [33–38].

In Japan, there have been no cases of xenotransplantation to date, but in recent years, researchers have expressed the need for its implementation in Japan $[39]$ . In addition, several universities are reportedly preparing for clinical trials. Thus, the situation is becoming more realistic for clinical trials to be applied in Japan $[40–41]$ . Japanese public attitude surveys on xenotransplantation were conducted by one research team in 1997 and 2000 but have not been implemented for the past 25 years $[42]$ . In 2024, the government (the Administration for Children and Families and the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare) conducted an attitude survey and collected opinions from 113 people $[43]$ . The survey received many valuable free-text responses. However, it was not representative of the Japanese population because it targeted individuals between the ages of 12 and 30 who were registered with the government's “Children and Youth Opinion Reflection Promotion Project” and did not sample according to the demographic composition of Japan.

Therefore, we conducted a web-based survey of the Japanese public to investigate awareness of and opinions toward xenotransplantation. This survey is the largest of its kind in Japan. In this study, we analyze the public's awareness of and opinions on xenotransplantation based on the survey results, and we discuss the social preparations that policymakers and researchers of xenotransplantation must make before the clinical trials begin.

# 2 | Materials and Methods

# 2.1 | Survey Methodology and Subjects

From December 9 to December 11, 2024, we conducted an Internet survey using monitors from an Internet research company (Intage Inc.). We set the target number of valid responses at more than 3000. A total of 29 700 male and female monitors, aged 20 to 79, were asked to participate in the survey. We considered gender and age to match the distribution of demographic statistics. To understand the general public's awareness and opinions of xenotransplantation, we excluded people who have received organ transplants and their family members, people who have donated organs and their family members, and medical professionals from the target population.

# 2.2 | Survey Items

We surveyed the following items: awareness of xenotransplantation and related topics, acceptance of xenotransplantation being performed in Japan in the future and the reasons for this acceptance, feelings of comfort and the reasons for these feelings if respondents were patients and a doctor proposed

xenotransplantation for their treatment, the possibility of undergoing a xenotransplant and related concerns, and the type of transplantation treatment they would prefer to receive. As background information, we also asked respondents about their attributes, experiences of sickness or injury that resulted in surgery or hospitalization for more than two weeks, experiences of keeping pets, and their willingness to donate organs.

# 2.3 | Analysis Methods

In addition to simple tabulation, we cross-tabulated valid responses using the statistical software IBM SPSS Statistics 30.0 to examine the relationship between basic attributes and awareness or opinions of xenotransplantation, as well as the relationship between awareness and opinions of xenotransplantation. We performed a Chi-square test, and the statistical significance level was set at 5%.

# 2.4 | Ethical Considerations

This survey was conducted with the approval of the Ethics Review Committee and the permission of the head of the institution (National Center for Child Health and Development Ethics Review Committee Approval No. 2024-168). Respondents received an overview of the survey on an Internet screen and were asked to click the “Start” button if they agreed to cooperate, which ensured that participation in the study was voluntary. We did not obtain respondents’ personal information from the Internet research company.

# 3 | Results

# 3.1 | Respondents' Attributes and Background Information

Responses were received from 3209 respondents (response rate: $10.8\%$ ). The attributes and background information of the respondents are presented in Table 1. The average age of respondents was 50.9 years, and the gender and age distributions were similar to those of the Japanese Population Estimates [44]. Regarding educational backgrounds, while the national survey reported $11.2\%$ of respondents were junior high school graduates and $19.7\%$ were university graduates [45], this survey shows a higher rate of respondents with higher education. Additionally, the rate of respondents who had expressed their willingness regarding organ donation is significantly higher than the $10.2\%$ reported in the government poll conducted in 2021 [46].

# 3.3 | General Public's Opinions of the Implementation of Xenotransplantation

We then provided the information that xenotransplantation had never been conducted in Japan and asked, “Currently, research is being conducted to implement xenotransplantation. What do you think about the future of xenotransplantation’s medical use in Japan?” The results showed that 53.8% of respondents had favorable opinions, and 40.6% had unfavorable opinions. The “other” option was chosen by 5.7% (183) of respondents, most of whom stated that they could not decide at that time.

We analyzed the association between favorable/unfavorable opinions and attributes and background information, as shown in “Opinions on the implementation of xenotransplantation in Japan” in Table 2. Statistically significant differences appeared only in three items: gender, experience with keeping pets, and willingness to donate organs. We also analyzed whether relationships existed between awareness of the meaning and related topics of xenotransplantation and opinions regarding its implementation. We found that respondents who “knew” tended to show a statistically significant difference toward a more favorable opinion, as shown in Table 3.

# 3.4 | Opinions for Xenotransplantation When Proposed by a Physician

Next, we instructed the respondents to “Imagine a situation in which xenotransplantation has become a medical treatment in Japan. You have a medical condition that requires an organ transplant, and your doctor proposes that you undergo an animal organ transplant because it takes a long time for a human organ transplant to be performed in your turn. The effectiveness of the treatment and the cost are comparable to transplanting human organs.” We then asked, “Do you think you would feel uncomfortable with xenotransplantation?” The responses were as follows: 34.8% (1118) answered “I think I would feel uncomfortable,” and 42.2% (1355) answered “I think I would feel somewhat uncomfortable,” resulting in a total of 77.0% (2473) who indicated “uncomfortable.” In contrast, 7.4% (239) selected “I think I would feel comfortable,” and 13.8% (443) selected “I think I would feel somewhat comfortable,” totaling 21.2% (682) who indicated “comfortable.” “Other” was selected by 1.7% (54), with many respondents indicating “I don’t know.”

# 4 | Discussion

The first heart transplant in Japan was performed in 1968. This transplant, later known as the “Wada transplant,” raised questions about its validity, including whether the donor was truly brain-dead and whether the recipient truly needed a transplant [47]. This led to growing distrust in transplant medicine in Japan, and transplant of organs from brain-dead donors had to wait until the Organ Transplant Law was enacted in 1997. However, in the process of enacting this law, there was intense debate in the Diet over whether brain death should be considered human death. As a result, the law reached a compromise, stating that a brain-dead person could be treated as a dead person in the exceptional case of donating an organ $[48]$ . The law was revised in 2010 to allow organ donation with the consent of a family member instead of the individual's consent; however, the legal status of brain death remains unchanged. Furthermore, there have been no notable voices in society calling for brain death to be recognized as human death. Therefore, although efforts to increase the number of organ donors through public relations activities are necessary, it will be difficult to introduce the “opt-out system” adopted by countries such as Spain and France to dramatically increase the number of donors.

As mentioned in the introduction section, in Japan, there were more than 16 800 patients on the waiting list at the end of 2024. However, because the waiting period for a transplant is so long that many patients give up and do not even register, it is estimated that there are many more patients in need of organ transplants than this number [49]. In this situation, it is better to have more options available to patients who need a transplant, and xenotransplantation could be one of those options. Recently, several universities have been preparing for xenotransplantation clinical trials, as we mentioned above. And, in 2024, a Japanese venture succeeded in breeding genetically modified pigs for transplantation [50]. As such, it is expected that clinical trials of xenotransplantation will be applied for in Japan within the next few years. When xenotransplantation is performed in Japan, either the “Act on Securing Quality, Efficacy and Safety of Products Including Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices (the PMD Act)” or the “Act on the Safety of Regenerative Medicine” applies, depending on the purpose. Due to the increasing possibility of conducting xenotransplant clinical trials, the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare issued a notice on January 17, 2025, titled “Notice: Implementation of xenotransplantation under the Act on the Safety of Regenerative Medicine.” [51] This notice outlines the policy for conducting xenotransplantation, including the implementation structure and review system for xenotransplantation. In addition, the “Guideline for Risk Control of Xenograft-derived Infections Associated with the Practice of Xenotransplantation” was also published [52]. Guidelines for implementation under the PMD Act are currently being developed. Hence, administrative regulation preparations have been almost finished to respond to applications for xenotransplant clinical trials in Japan.

On the other hand, xenotransplantation involves different social issues compared to allogeneic transplantation. Therefore, policymakers and researchers of clinical trials must consider the opinions of the public as well as patients, their families, medical professionals, and other relevant parties. Our survey showed that the proportion of the public who had a favorable opinion that xenotransplantation will be performed as a medical treatment in Japan in the future was 53.8%, which was low compared to 72.9% in South Korea [34] and 65.2% in the United Kingdom [38]. This implies the Japanese public is not ready enough to accept the practice. If this is implemented, it could cause social distrust, as seen in the “Wada transplant.” Therefore, we will analyze our survey results and propose four points that policymakers and researchers of xenotransplantation should prepare society for before xenotransplantation clinical trials begin.

# 4.1 | Policymakers and Researchers Should Constantly Provide Easy-to-Understand, Up-to-Date Information to Help the Public Fully Understand Xenotransplantation

A favorable opinion about the implementation of xenotransplantation as a medical treatment in Japan in the future was expressed by 62.9% of those who knew about the meaning of xenotransplantation, compared to 53.8% of those who did not know about the meaning. Furthermore, among those who knew the meaning of xenotransplantation, individuals who also knew the topics “the purpose of xenotransplantation,” “genetic modification,” and “the risk of infection” had statistically significantly more favorable opinions. These findings indicate that understanding well about xenotransplantation, even when it comes to risks, affects positive opinion formation regarding its implementation. This trend can be seen in surveys on the general public in South Korea $[34]$ , the United Kingdom $[38]$ , and Germany $[53]$ . Although it was conducted among nursing students rather than the general public, a pilot study in the United Kingdom showed a similar trend $[54]$ . These results suggest that the public may be unable to make appropriate decisions if their understanding of xenotransplantation is not enough. Therefore, it is extremely important that the public has accurate information about xenotransplantation.

When we looked at the current situation in Japan, about two-thirds (65.4%) of the respondents had no idea what a xenotransplant was. In a previous Japanese survey, 51.9% answered “No” to the question “Have you heard of this application?” in the 1997 survey (N = 405), and 33.2% answered “No” in the 2000 survey (N = 297) [42]. This means that over the past 25 years, the Japanese public’s lack of awareness regarding xenotransplantation has increased significantly. The reason why public awareness was high in 1997 is probably because public interest in transplant medicine increased through coverage of a contentious debate in the Diet on the Organ Transplant Law, and for several years following this legislation, transplant cases received extensive media coverage. During this period, the public likely encountered information about xenotransplantation in discussions about organ shortages, as these topics appeared in the press. Currently, however, organ transplants from brain-dead bodies are so established in Japanese society that they are rarely reported in the news. This means that, at present, the Japanese public has few opportunities to learn and think about xenotransplantation.

On the other hand, the survey in the United States in 2023 showed 40.5% of respondents had never heard of xenotransplantation, which indicates a higher awareness rate than that of the Japanese public [36]. This difference is likely because, as noted at the beginning of this paper, there have been attempts to perform xenotransplants on patients in the United States since 2022, and these cases have received extensive media coverage. However, the survey in the United Kingdom, a country with no recent experience of xenotransplantation (the only case was in 1968) [5], showed almost two-thirds (65.2%) of the British public were “aware of the possibility” of xenotransplantation [38]. The reasons for the difference in awareness rates between the United Kingdom and Japan might be that the United Kingdom has been addressing the safety and ethical issues of xenotransplantation since the 1990s [55–56], resulting in a high level of public interest in the topic. The survey in South Korea, which has also never attempted xenotransplantation, showed a high awareness rate of 71.2%. Its possible reason was explained as that South Koreans have been more exposed to the news and issues regarding xenotransplantation [34].

Xenotransplantation cases in the United States have not been constantly reported in Japan, and it is not easy for the Japanese public to access information about the cases in English. Therefore, Japanese policymakers and researchers should communicate

information about cases overseas to the general public in easy-to-understand Japanese and also take this opportunity to provide the following information related to xenotransplantation.

# 4.1.1 | The background and purpose of xenotransplantation

The background and purpose are very important factors in understanding xenotransplantation. However, only 18.0% of all respondents were aware of it. Hence, Japanese policymakers and researchers should communicate to the public the information of the current situation of human organ transplantation, such as the following information: number of patients who need organ transplantation; number of registered donors; number of patients who had a transplant in each year; and number of patients on the waiting list.

# 4.1.2 | Characteristics of xenotransplantation

In addition, the facts that there is a risk that patients may be infected with animal-specific viruses and that animals that donate organs to humans are genetically modified to make them suitable for human transplantation are also important information for understanding the characteristics of xenotransplantation. However, awareness rates for these topics were low, at 13.5% and 6.6% of all respondents, respectively. These results show that Japanese policymakers and researchers must provide up-to-date information to the public.

# 4.1.3 | Current status of development of other technologies to alleviate the organ shortage for transplantation

In our survey, many respondents selected “transplantation of an organ artificially created from one’s own cells” and “transplantation of an organ artificially created from another person’s cells” as their first or second preference if available to undergo. However, various challenges must be overcome before transplanting complex, life-sized organs into humans $[57–58]$ . Thus, it may take a long time until those organs can be used. Japanese policymakers and researchers should inform the public about the current status of developing other alternative technologies to alleviate the organ shortage, especially those mentioned above.

# 4.2 | Policymakers and Researchers Should Address the Public's Anxieties

In this survey, just under 90% of respondents expressed concern about contracting a virus from an animal if they received a xenotransplant. In the survey in South Korea, 97.2% answered that it was “important” to think about the risk of zoonotic disease transmission [34]. In the United States survey, 79.1% answered “concerned” for the question, “How concerned would you be about getting an organ from a pig if there was a risk of getting a new infection from the pig organ once it was in your body, even if this risk was thought to be very low?” [36] The result from the United Kingdom survey showed “my risk of getting an animal infection" was the second-highest concern out of 13 options [38]. The risk of contracting infectious diseases was a strong concern among the public in each country.

Japanese surveys in 1997 and 2000 did not include a question about the risk of infection [42]. Two 2019 surveys in the United States of xenotransplant attitudes among patients and parents of pediatric patients [59], as well as among pre- and post-kidney transplant patients [60], did not include a question about the risk of infection. These facts show that our perception of infectious diseases has changed substantially before and after the COVID-19 pandemic. Through our experience with the COVID-19 pandemic, we have learned about the threat of infecting ourselves or others with a pathogen and the difficulty of controlling it. This might be a significant reason many people have expressed concern about the risk of infection from xenotransplantation [61].

Being harassed or discriminated against by others if they received xenotransplantation was a concern for nearly 60% (58.0%) of respondents. The South Korean survey showed a much higher percentage of people concerned about “stigmatization and judgment from others” [34]. One reason for the Japanese result may be the discrimination and prejudice that arose against infected people in Japan during the COVID-19 pandemic [62–63].

Considering the above, it is appropriate for Japanese policymakers and researchers to conduct a rigorous assessment of the risk of transmission of zoonotic pathogens to the patient and to those close to the patient and to society. They must then inform society of the results and allow society to decide whether to accept this transplantation method. If society accepts xenotransplantation, it is also necessary, especially for policymakers, to proactively create a social environment in which harassment and discrimination against patients do not occur.

# 4.3 | Researchers Should Conduct Research to Reduce Risks, and Policymakers Should Provide Financial Support to Them

In the United States survey, the question was, “How comfortable would you feel about having a pig organ put into you if you needed an organ transplant?” The combined percentage of respondents who answered “very comfortable” or “comfortable” was 41.3%, while those who answered “uncomfortable” or “very uncomfortable” totaled 41.7%, showing that the responses were close. However, when asked, “Let us say that a pig organ transplant will work as well as a human organ transplant. If you needed an organ, how likely would you be to agree to take a pig organ instead of a human organ if you could get the pig organ much faster?” Respondents who were in favor of receiving xenotransplants (56.9%) were more than twice as many as those who were opposed (25.5%) [36]. In other words, even those who feel uncomfortable with xenotransplantation were willing to undergo xenotransplantation if they had a medical condition that required an organ transplant and if the organ could be obtained more quickly than a human organ. Although there are no long-term survivors so far in the United States, media images of temporarily recovered patients may have increased the public’s

hopes for this treatment option. Similarly, in the South Korean survey, 61.7% of respondents answered “yes” to the question, “Would you accept xenotransplantation as a treatment if you had a disease for which no other treatment was available?” [34] And also in the United Kingdom survey, 59.5% of respondents said that they accept xenotransplantation [38].

On the other hand, in Japan, nearly 80% (77.0%) of respondents reported feeling uncomfortable with a xenotransplantation if a doctor proposed it under similar conditions. And the proportion of those who answered, “I would not undergo” (60.9%) the xenotransplantation was much higher than those who answered, “I would undergo” (34.6%). These results suggest that xenotransplantation may not have as significant an effect on resolving organ shortage in Japan as is expected in other countries. There was a statistically significant difference between “uncomfortable” or “comfortable” with xenotransplantation and the decision to undergo xenotransplantation, and the reasons for the uncomfortable feeling also affected the decision to undergo xenotransplantation. That is, among respondents who cited “because I think the physical burden and risks are greater than those of human organ transplantation,” “because I think the mental burden and risks are greater than those of human organ transplantation,” or “because I think the social risks, such as prejudice, are greater than those of human organ transplantation” as the most important reasons for their discomfort, the proportion who answered “I think I would undergo the xenotransplant” was relatively high, at just under 30% to 50%. This result may indicate that, although these individuals feel uncomfortable due to concerns about the burden and risks, they would still choose xenotransplantation for their own survival if they faced a medical condition that required transplantation. From this analysis, people who currently feel uncomfortable with xenotransplantation due to various burdens and risks are likely to decide to undergo xenotransplantation if these issues are improved.

Therefore, researchers should conduct research continuously to improve knowledge and technology related to xenotransplantation in order to reduce the physical and mental burdens and risks. Policymakers should provide financial support to the researchers to carry out the research and also address the creation of a social environment that does not cause prejudice as mentioned above so that the wishes of these individuals who want to undergo xenotransplantation can be fulfilled.

# 4.4 | Policymakers Should Simultaneously Promote Transplantation Beyond Xenotransplantation

The percentage of respondents who would accept xenotransplantation was low, at around 10 percent, among those who selected “because sacrificing an animal for myself does not fit with my beliefs,” “it is unethical,” or “because it is not allowed in my religion” as the most important reasons for their discomfort. These results suggest that when discomfort with xenotransplantation is based on personal beliefs, these beliefs strongly affect the decision whether to undergo xenotransplantation or not. Among the respondents who answered, “because animals’ organs are in my body,” which was the most common reason for feeling uncomfortable, only 23.5% of respondents indicated that they would accept this option. Although this reason is not a belief, it is based on an individual's view of their own body and is therefore considered more likely to affect the decision to undergo xenotransplantation than reasons based on burden or risk. It is expected that people who feel uncomfortable with xenotransplantation for reasons based on their beliefs or views of the human body are unlikely to accept xenotransplantation even if the burdens and risks are reduced.

Furthermore, one-third of respondents ranked xenotransplantation as the lowest preference, and 37.6% of respondents would not choose xenotransplantation. These results suggest that xenotransplantation is not a transplant method that the public prefers. In comparison, many respondents selected “transplantation of an organ artificially created from one’s own cells” and “transplantation of an organ artificially created from another person’s cells” as their first or second choices. In addition, some respondents selected transplantation of organs from deceased or living donors, which has already become a common medical practice, as their first or second choice.

Based on these results, even if xenotransplantation becomes available as a medical treatment, policymakers should consider xenotransplantation as one option within transplantation medicine and promote research and development beyond xenotransplantation simultaneously, as well as continue public relations activities for the transplantation of organs from deceased persons $[64]$ .

# 5 | Limitations of This Study

This survey has three significant limitations. First, the respondents were not a randomly selected probability sample, as the survey was conducted among registered respondents of an Internet research company. Compared to the Census, fewer respondents in this survey reported secondary school as their highest level of education, while more reported college as their highest level of education. Additionally, more respondents indicated a willingness to donate organs compared to the results of other polls. Therefore, it should be noted that the actual recognition rate of xenotransplantation may be lower than the results of this survey suggest. Second, as we learned from this survey, the Japanese public's awareness of xenotransplantation is low. As a result, their opinions about xenotransplantation are also based on limited awareness. It is expected that opinions will change as awareness increases. However, it is significant that we were able to learn about the general public's opinions of xenotransplantation in a context where awareness is low. Third, because this was the first survey conducted in Japan in 25 years, it was necessary to begin with basic questions, and we could not ask about the conditions under which xenotransplantation would be performed, such as what measures against infectious diseases were considered necessary. We plan to conduct a separate survey to clarify the public's awareness further.

# 6 | Conclusion

The survey results imply that the Japanese public is not ready enough to accept xenotransplantation. If xenotransplantation is implemented in this situation, it could cause social distrust.

Therefore, policymakers and researchers on xenotransplantation must prepare for social issues before the clinical trial begins in Japan. There isn't much time left before clinical trials begin. We proposed four points that policymakers and researchers should work on. We hope these proposals will be referred to by policymakers and researchers so that social acceptance of xenotransplantation clinical trials in Japan will increase.
