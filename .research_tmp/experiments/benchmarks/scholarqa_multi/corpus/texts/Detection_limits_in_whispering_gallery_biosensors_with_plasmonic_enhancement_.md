# Detection limits in whispering gallery biosensors with plasmonic enhancement

(Received 17 June 2011; accepted 17 November 2011; published online 14 December 2011)

We perform numerical modeling of a gold nanorod bound to the surface of a microtoroid-based biosensor. Localized surface plasmon resonances in the nanorod give rise to strong enhancements in the electric field when excited near resonance, increasing the frequency shift for a single bovine serum albumin molecule by a factor of 870, with even larger enhancements predicted for smaller proteins. On resonance, the frequency shift is predicted to be on the order of MHz, more than an order of magnitude larger than measurement noise arising from time-averaged frequency and thermal fluctuations. © 2011 American Institute of Physics. [doi:10.1063/1.3669398]

Whispering gallery mode (WGM) resonators such as silica $(\mathrm{SiO}_2)$ microspheres and microtoroids have unprecedented sensitivity as biological sensors due to their small optical mode volume and ultra-high quality factor $(Q > 10^{8})$ in water. The interaction between the resonator's evanescent field and its environment shifts the resonance frequency of the optical mode, such that when a molecule binds to the resonator surface it induces a shift given by

$$
\frac {\delta \omega_ {m}}{\omega} \simeq - \frac {\alpha_ {m} \left| E _ {0} \left(\vec {r} _ {m}\right) \right| ^ {2}}{2 V \left| E _ {0 , \max } \right| ^ {2}}, \tag {1}
$$

where $\alpha_{m}$ and $\vec{r}_m$ are the polarizability and position of the molecule, $E_0(\vec{r})$ is the WGM electric field, and $V$ is the optical mode volume of the resonator. For typical WGM resonators and proteins, Eq. (1) predicts single molecule optical frequency shifts in the Hz to kHz range. As an example, a bovine serum albumin (BSA; $\alpha_{m} = 54800\AA^{3})^{7}$ protein bound to a microtoroidal resonator with $V = 760\mu \mathrm{m}^3$ produces a frequency shift of $\delta \omega_{m} = 5\mathrm{kHz}$ .

Presently, however, measurement noise arising primarily from thermal fluctuations within the resonator and fluctuations in laser frequency often limits the minimum detectable frequency shift to above $5\mathrm{kHz},^{6,8}$ precluding the observation of single molecule binding events.

Recently, it was shown experimentally that a localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) in a large metallic nanoshell can enhance single nanoparticle optical frequency shifts in a microsphere resonator by a factor of four. The enhancement was predicted to be as much as 200 for BSA. A similar effect was shown in Ref. 10. In this letter we predict that a gold (Au) nanorod with a length-to-diameter aspect ratio of $R = 4$ can enhance the frequency shift of a single BSA molecule by as much as 870, with even larger enhancements possible for proteins smaller than BSA. We compare the enhanced frequency shift for BSA with that of a bare microtoroid resonator and show that the enhancement can allow

single molecule detection below the limits set by thermal and laser frequency fluctuations.

We present theoretical modeling of the LSPR generated in Au nanorods using a boundary element method (BEM) as described in Refs. 11-13. The model consists of a single Au nanorod bound to the equator of a microtoroid so that it experiences the maximum possible resonator evanescent field $E_0(\vec{r})$ (Fig. 1(a)), and orientated such that its longitudinal axis is aligned with the polarization of the WGM.

The nanorods' dimensions (diameter of $10\mathrm{nm}$ , $2\leq R\leq 4$ ) are small in comparison with the incident wavelength $\lambda$ , so the evanescent field may be modeled as a plane wave of unit amplitude, neglecting the small evanescent decay across the nanorod. The background medium is water (with relative permittivity $\epsilon_{b} = 1.77$ ), and the experimental dielectric function for Au was taken from Johnson and Christy. The strong fields at each tip result from the curvature of the nanorod's hemispherical caps, with enhancements relative to the incident intensity in excess of $10^{3}$ . To account for the enhanced absorption near resonance, we consider an additional cavity loss rate $\Gamma$ which reduces the optical Q factor of the resonator from $Q_0$ to $Q = (Q_0^{-1} + \Gamma / \omega)^{-1}$ .

Absorption dominates over scattering in the near-field, $^{15}$ so the loss rate can be determined by $I_{\mathrm{inc}}\sigma = \hbar \omega \Gamma$ , where $I_{\mathrm{inc}} = \hbar \omega cf^2 (\vec{r}_s) / V$ is the intensity incident on the nanorod, $^{16}$ $c$ is the speed of light, and $f(\vec{r}_s)$ is the spatial variation of $E_0(\vec{r})$ in the optical mode. $^{17}$ The Q factor is then

$$
Q = \left(Q _ {0} ^ {- 1} + \frac {\sigma (R) \lambda}{2 \pi V} f ^ {2} (\vec {r} _ {s})\right) ^ {- 1}. \tag {2}
$$

The reduced WGM Q factors are shown in Fig. 2(b) for $\mathrm{Q_0} = 10^7$ , $V = 760\mu \mathrm{m}^3$ , and $f^{2}(\vec{r}_{s}) = 0.3$ at the equator.

Now we can consider the enhancement in $\delta \omega$ due to a BSA molecule binding to the microtoroid-bound nanorod. We model the BSA molecule as a dielectric sphere with a radius of $3\mathrm{nm}$ and $\epsilon_{m} = 2.78$ (Ref. 18) that is bound to the tip of the nanorod. Because the field strength decays very rapidly near the tips (with a characteristic length on the order of nanometers), the field is inhomogeneous over the BSA molecule. For this reason rather than using a dipole approximation as in Eq. (1), the frequency shift is found by numerically integrating the field intensity associated with polarizing the BSA molecule. The enhancement in $\delta \omega$ is the ratio of the polarization energies with and without the nanorod

$$
\xi (R) = \frac {\int E _ {n r} ^ {*} (\vec {r}) \cdot E _ {m} (\vec {r}) d \vec {r}}{\int E _ {0} ^ {*} (\vec {r}) \cdot E _ {0 , m} (\vec {r}) d \vec {r}}, \tag {3}
$$

where $E_{nr}(\vec{r})$ is the field emerging from the nanorod, $E_{m}(\vec{r})$ is the induced field in the BSA molecule, and $E_0(\vec{r})$ is a plane wave which represents the WGM field prior to the enhancement. We consider the frequency shift due to a first-order perturbation and therefore take the integration over the volume of the BSA molecule only. We can now estimate the frequency shift $\delta \omega_{\mathrm{BSA}}$ expected for a single BSA molecule. We consider a measurement of $\delta \omega$ with some fraction due to the binding event $\delta \omega_{\mathrm{BSA}}$ and the rest due to noise from fluctuations in the laser frequency $\delta \omega_{\Delta \Omega}$ and thermal fluctuations within the resonator $\delta \omega_{\Delta T}$

$$
\delta \omega = \delta \omega_ {\mathrm {B S A}} + \delta \omega_ {\Delta \Omega} + \delta \omega_ {\Delta T}. \tag {4}
$$

Using Eqs. (1) and (3), the enhanced BSA frequency shift is $\delta \omega_{\mathrm{BSA}} = \xi (R)\times \delta \omega_{m}$ , where $\delta \omega_{m}$ is the frequency shift due to BSA prior to the enhancement. For a standard microtoroid resonator with $V = 760~\mu \mathrm{m}^3$ , $\delta \omega_{m}$ is about $5\mathrm{kHz}$ . For the remaining terms in Eq. (4), we take $\delta \omega_{\Delta \Omega}$ to be fluctuations on the order of a tunable diode laser's linewidth $(\delta \omega_{\Delta \Omega} = 100\mathrm{kHz}$ Ref.

20)) and $\delta \omega_{\Delta T}$ to be thermorefractive fluctuations in a standard microtoroid resonator. The thermal frequency shift is given by $\delta \omega_{\Delta T} / \omega = n^{-1}\frac{dn}{dT}\Delta T$ where for $\mathrm{SiO}_2n = 1.45$ is the refractive index, $\frac{dn}{dT} = 1.45\times 10^{-5}\mathrm{K}^{-1}$ is the thermorefractive coefficient, and $\Delta T$ is the temperature fluctuation over an averaging time $\tau$ .

By following the analytical work of Gorodetsky and Grudinin[21] and numerically calculating the power spectral density of thermorefractive noise in a microtoroid, we found that $\Delta T = 0.6\pm 0.1\mu \mathrm{K}$ for $\tau = 1$ ms (see supplemental material[22]). However, since temperature fluctuations scale with the effective mode volume, $\Delta T\propto V_{\mathrm{eff}}^{-1} = \int |E(\vec{r})|^{4}d\vec{r}$ , we also calculated the spectrum including the field from the nanorod $E_{nr}(\vec{r})$ .

For the enhancements reported in this letter, the nanorod's contribution to thermorefractive noise was negligible.

In summary, we have performed theoretical calculations of the LSPR excited in Au nanorods bound to a microtoroidal resonator. The frequency shift enhancement is likely to put single molecule detection within reach. We calculate a maximum resonant enhancement of $\xi = 870$ for BSA binding to a nanorod with $R = 4$ , which corresponds to a frequency shift on the order of $4\mathrm{MHz}$ in a microtoroid.
