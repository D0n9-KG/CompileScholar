## Related Works

Video super-resolution (VSR) aims to restore high-resolution videos from low-resolution inputs, a task that requires modeling complex spatial-temporal dependencies. Early approaches often relied on recurrent architectures to propagate information across frames, such as the Recurrent Structure-Detail Network, which separates input into structure and detail components to enhance robustness against error accumulation [1]. To address the limitations of recurrent models in capturing long-range dependencies, Transformer-based methods have gained prominence. The Video Super-Resolution Transformer (VSR-Transformer) adapted the standard Transformer architecture for VSR by introducing spatial-temporal convolutional self-attention and bidirectional optical flow-based feed-forward layers to exploit data locality and feature alignment [2]. Similarly, the Video Restoration Transformer (VRT) employs parallel frame prediction and long-range temporal dependency modeling through temporal mutual self-attention and parallel warping, achieving superior performance across multiple restoration tasks [3]. To balance the computational cost of parallel Transformers with the efficiency of recurrent models, the Recurrent Video Restoration Transformer (RVRT) processes local neighboring frames in parallel within a globally recurrent framework, utilizing guided deformable attention for clip-to-clip alignment [4].

Beyond Transformers, other architectures have focused on efficient temporal aggregation and alignment. BasicVSR established a succinct pipeline based on four essential functionalities: propagation, alignment, aggregation, and upsampling, serving as a strong baseline for subsequent research [5]. Its extension, BasicVSR++, further improved propagation and alignment mechanisms [6]. Alternative approaches include the use of temporal group attention to hierarchically incorporate temporal information [7] and multi-correspondence aggregation networks that leverage similar patches across frames to mitigate optical flow errors [8]. For real-world scenarios, where degradations are complex and unknown, specific challenges arise. RealBasicVSR introduced an image pre-cleaning stage to reduce noise and artifacts before propagation, addressing the issue of severe in-the-wild degradations being exaggerated through long-term propagation [9]. Furthermore, the FastRealVSR method proposed a Hidden State Attention module to mitigate artifacts in recurrent models by replacing hidden states with cleaner counterparts derived from various filters [10].

Recent advancements in sequence modeling have introduced State Space Models (SSMs) as efficient alternatives to Transformers for long-range dependencies. The Mamba architecture utilizes selective state spaces to achieve linear-time sequence modeling with content-based reasoning, offering significantly higher throughput than Transformers [11]. This efficiency has been extended to multi-dimensional data through Mamba-ND [12] and applied to vision tasks via Vision Mamba (Vim), which uses bidirectional state space models to overcome the position-sensitivity and global context requirements of visual data, achieving higher performance and efficiency compared to vision Transformers [13]. Additionally, simplified state space layers like S5 have demonstrated state-of-the-art performance in long-range sequence modeling tasks [14].

In the domain of self-supervised learning, which is critical for extracting degradation-insensitive features, contrastive learning methods have significantly reduced the gap with supervised pretraining. SimCLR simplified contrastive self-supervised learning by emphasizing the role of data augmentations and nonlinear transformations [15]. Momentum Contrast (MoCo) introduced a dynamic dictionary using a queue and moving-averaged encoder to facilitate large-scale contrastive learning [16]. SwAV proposed an online algorithm that clusters data while enforcing consistency between cluster assignments of different views, avoiding the need for pairwise feature comparisons and large memory banks [17]. These self-supervised techniques provide the foundation for extracting robust features from low-quality videos, a key component in modern noise-robust VSR frameworks.
