## Related Works

The application of machine learning to neuroimaging, particularly in Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), has been significantly advanced by transfer learning and synthetic data generation techniques. Transfer learning, which involves acquiring knowledge from related tasks to improve generalization in target tasks, is crucial for addressing variations in MR images and re-utilizing models trained on related domains \[[Valverde](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01530v2)\]. A systematic review of transfer learning in brain MRI identified 129 studies, with the most frequent applications being dementia-related classification and brain tumor segmentation, predominantly utilizing Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) \[[Valverde](http://arxiv.org/abs/2102.01530v2)\]. However, theoretical limits of transfer learning indicate that the degree of probabilistic change in an algorithm places an upper bound on possible improvements, necessitating careful selection of transferred information and ensuring dependence between transferred insights and target problems \[[Jake Williams and Abel Tadesse and Tyler Sam and Huey Sun and George D. Montanez](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.12694v1)\].

Addressing class imbalance remains a significant challenge in medical image analysis, where minority classes such as specific lesion types are rare. Traditional approaches to imbalanced data include loss function modifications and instance resampling, often relying on Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) \[[Damien Dablain and Bartosz Krawczyk and Nitesh V. Chawla](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.02340v1)\]. While GANs are powerful, they may suffer from mode collapse, prompting the development of alternatives like DeepSMOTE, which fuses deep learning with SMOTE-based oversampling to generate high-quality artificial images without requiring a discriminator \[[Damien Dablain and Bartosz Krawczyk and Nitesh V. Chawla](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.02340v1)\]. In the specific context of Multiple Sclerosis (MS), synthetic lesion generation has been proposed to mitigate the lack of ground truth data. One approach utilizes a two-input, two-output fully convolutional neural network to synthesize MS lesions on healthy MRI images, demonstrating that data augmentation with synthetic lesions can achieve performance comparable to fully trained models even with limited training data \[[Salem](http://arxiv.org/abs/1901.05733v1)\].

Generative Adversarial Networks themselves have evolved from the original framework, which trains a generator and discriminator in a minimax game, to conditional variants that allow for controlled generation based on specific labels or inputs \[[Ian J. Goodfellow and Jean Pouget-Abadie and Mehdi Mirza and Bing Xu and David Warde-Farley and Sherjil Ozair and Aaron Courville and Yoshua Bengio](http://arxiv.org/abs/1406.2661v1)\]\[[Mehdi Mirza and
                  Simon Osindero](http://arxiv.org/abs/1411.1784v1)\]. The robustness of conditional GANs to noisy labels has also been studied, with architectures like the Robust Conditional GAN (RCGAN) designed to handle corrupted labels by corrupting the label of generated samples before feeding them to the discriminator, thereby forcing the generator to produce samples with clean labels \[[Kiran Koshy Thekumparampil and Ashish Khetan and Zinan Lin and Sewoong Oh](http://arxiv.org/abs/1811.03205v1)\]. This is particularly relevant for clinical data where label noise is inevitable. Furthermore, post-correction methods for noisy predictions, such as Noisy Prediction Calibration (NPC), utilize generative models to estimate transition matrices and correct noisy predictions from pre-trained classifiers, providing a robust pathway to true labels without retraining the classifier \[[HeeSun Bae and Seungjae Shin and Byeonghu Na and JoonHo Jang and Kyungwoo Song and Il-Chul Moon](http://arxiv.org/abs/2205.00690v3)\]. Other approaches model label noise as stochastic shifts in the latent space of deep generative models to improve multilabel classification performance \[[Weipeng Huang and Qin Li and Yang Xiao and Cheng Qiao and Tie Cai and Junwei Liao and Neil J. Hurley and Guangyuan Piao](http://arxiv.org/abs/2502.14281v3)\].

In the domain of quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM), specific architectures like QSMRim-Net have been developed for the identification of chronic active multiple sclerosis lesions, employing imbalance-aware learning strategies \[[Zhang](https://ref.local/5)\]. The integration of synthetic data generation with these imbalance-aware techniques offers a promising avenue for improving the detection of rare biomarkers, such as paramagnetic rim lesions, by enhancing the minority class representation and enabling more robust training on ambiguous cases.
