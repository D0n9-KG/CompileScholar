## Related Works

Generative Information Retrieval (GenIR) represents a paradigm shift from traditional similarity-matching systems to models that directly generate relevant document identifiers or responses [1]. This approach leverages the parameters of pre-trained language models to memorize document corpora, enabling end-to-end retrieval without explicit indexing structures [1][2]. The Differentiable Search Index (DSI) was a foundational contribution, demonstrating that a single Transformer can encode corpus information within its parameters to map queries directly to document IDs [2]. Subsequent works have refined this paradigm by addressing specific challenges in identifier design, training optimization, and scalability.

A critical component of generative retrieval is the definition of document identifiers (docids). Early methods like GENRE proposed autoregressive generation of entity names to mitigate memory footprint issues associated with dense vector storage [3]. However, fixed rule-based identifiers often fail to capture complete semantic information [4]. To address this, GenRet introduced a discrete auto-encoding approach to learn semantic docids in an end-to-end manner [4]. Similarly, the Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI) employed a prefix-aware weight-adaptive decoder to optimize recall performance using semantic identifiers [5]. More recent approaches, such as RIPOR, argue that docids should be constructed based on relevance associations between queries and documents rather than syntactic or semantic document features alone, utilizing relevance-based quantization to improve performance on large-scale benchmarks [6]. Other strategies include using n-grams as identifiers to avoid forced hierarchical structures [7] and employing few-shot prompting of Large Language Models to generate docids without heavy training overhead [8].

The optimization and training of generative retrieval models have also evolved to bridge the gap between generation and ranking. LTRGR proposed a learning-to-rank framework that optimizes autoregressive models toward final passage ranking targets via rank loss, addressing the disconnect between generation objectives and ranking goals [9]. PAG introduced simultaneous decoding to guide autoregressive generation, achieving significant improvements in Mean Reciprocal Rank and query latency [10]. Furthermore, to handle dynamic corpora, methods like IncDSI and CLEVER have been developed to allow incremental indexing of new documents without full retraining, utilizing constrained optimization or memory-augmented learning mechanisms [11][12].

Theoretical analyses have sought to clarify the relationship between generative retrieval and traditional dense retrieval. It has been demonstrated that generative retrieval with atomic identifiers is equivalent to single-vector dense retrieval, while hierarchical identifiers exhibit behavior analogous to hierarchical search in tree indices [13]. Further analysis reveals that generative retrieval can be viewed as a special case of multi-vector dense retrieval, where relevance is computed as a sum of products of query and document vectors [13][14]. These insights have led to hybrid approaches like Generative Dense Retrieval (GDR), which combines the deep interaction of generative models with the scalability of dense retrieval for fine-grained matching [15].

Despite these advancements, the robustness and generalization capabilities of generative retrieval models remain areas of active investigation. Studies indicate that current models often struggle with out-of-distribution (OOD) scenarios, including query variations, unseen query types, and corpus expansion [16]. The reliance on parametric memory can lead to poor accuracy for fine-grained features and increased confusion as corpus size grows [15]. Additionally, the use of constrained decoding and beam search introduces specific limitations; for instance, the training-testing discrepancy in tree-based models and the potential suboptimality of marginal distributions in beam search have been identified as factors affecting retrieval performance [17]. This paper builds upon these findings by examining the inherent limitations of constrained auto-regressive generation, specifically deriving error bounds related to KL divergence and analyzing the impact of beam search on generalization.
