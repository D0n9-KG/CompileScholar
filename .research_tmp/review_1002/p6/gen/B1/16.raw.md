## Related Works

Open-vocabulary object detection (OvOD) addresses the limitation of traditional detectors, which are restricted to pre-defined categories due to the high cost of manual labeling \[[Zhu](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09220v2)\]. By leveraging natural language supervision, recent models can classify objects beyond closed vocabularies, enabling users to specify class vocabularies freely at test time \[[Zhu](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.09220v2)\]\[[Liu](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.10053v1)\]. However, the performance of these detectors is often sensitive to the semantic granularity and specificity of the user-provided vocabulary, posing challenges for real-world deployment \[[Liu](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.10053v1)\].

Early advancements in scene understanding relied on large-scale datasets with fixed categories. The Microsoft COCO dataset introduced complex everyday scenes with 91 object types, establishing baselines for detection and segmentation \[[Lin](http://arxiv.org/abs/1405.0312v3)\]. To address the long-tail distribution of object categories, the LVIS dataset was developed, containing over 1,000 categories and highlighting the poor performance of deep learning methods in low-sample regimes \[[Gupta](http://arxiv.org/abs/1908.03195v2)\]. ImageNet further expanded the scale of visual recognition with a hierarchical database of 21,000 classes, demonstrating the potential for large-vocabulary classification \[[Deng](https://ref.local/5)\]\[[Zhou](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.02605v3)\].

A pivotal shift occurred with the introduction of Contrastive Language-Image Pre-training (CLIP), which learns visual representations from 400 million image-text pairs \[[Radford](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020v1)\]. CLIP enables zero-shot transfer to downstream tasks by aligning images with natural language descriptions, matching fully supervised baselines on ImageNet without task-specific training \[[Radford](http://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020v1)\]. However, directly applying CLIP to object detection yields poor performance due to a domain shift, as CLIP aligns whole images with text rather than fine-grained image regions \[[Zhong](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09106v1)\]. To mitigate this, RegionCLIP extends CLIP to learn region-level visual representations, significantly improving open-vocabulary detection performance on COCO and LVIS \[[Zhong](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.09106v1)\].

Further methodologies for open-vocabulary detection include Detic, which expands detector vocabulary to tens of thousands of concepts by training classifiers on image-level supervision data, achieving state-of-the-art results on open-vocabulary benchmarks \[[Zhou](http://arxiv.org/abs/2201.02605v3)\]. CoDet reformulates region-word alignment as a co-occurring object discovery problem, leveraging visual similarities to align objects with shared concepts in captions, thereby surpassing previous state-of-the-art on OV-LVIS \[[Ma](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.16667v1)\]. Additionally, SHiNe introduces a semantic hierarchy nexus to enhance robustness across diverse vocabulary granularities, using class hierarchies to generate classifier vectors that improve zero-shot accuracy without additional computational overhead \[[Liu](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.10053v1)\].

These works collectively demonstrate the progression from fixed-vocabulary datasets to language-guided detection, highlighting the critical role of aligning visual regions with textual concepts and the need for robust handling of variable vocabulary specifications.
