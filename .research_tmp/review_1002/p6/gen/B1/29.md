## Related Works

Differential Privacy (DP) has established itself as the de facto standard for releasing aggregate statistics on sensitive data, typically implemented in either central or local models [1][2][3]. In the central model, a trusted server aggregates data and adds noise, whereas the local model requires individuals to randomize their data before submission, thereby eliminating the need for a trusted collector but often at the cost of reduced utility [2][3]. However, both models face significant vulnerabilities when the entities responsible for executing DP mechanisms are untrusted or malicious. In the central model, a malicious server can manipulate results, using the inherent randomness of DP as a smokescreen to mask nefarious activities, effectively providing a perfect alibi since revealing the noise would defeat the purpose of DP [1]. Similarly, in local DP, malicious data providers can manipulate their outputs before sending them, exploiting the fact that estimations are normalized by randomization probabilities to skew or control final results [4].

To address these issues, recent research has focused on verifiable mechanisms and cryptographic techniques. Verifiable Differential Privacy (VDP) introduces the requirement for publishing entities to output zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) that convince verifiers the output is both DP-compliant and reliable, resolving the paradox of validating randomness without learning it by mixing private and public randomness [1]. In the context of local DP, verifiable randomization mechanisms allow data collectors to verify that each provider has correctly executed the agreed-upon randomization protocol, protecting against general output manipulations without assuming specific attack vectors [4]. Cryptographic approaches have also been developed to guarantee unconditional privacy, ensuring that cheating respondents cannot affect the tally beyond their own vote, utilizing both traditional and quantum cryptographic techniques [5].

In distributed settings, Secure Multi-Party Computation (MPC) is increasingly used to achieve DP with utility comparable to the central model while preventing parties from accessing others' information [3]. Systems like Crypt$\epsilon$ employ two non-colluding untrusted servers to run DP programs on encrypted data, achieving the accuracy of the central model without a trusted collector [2]. However, a critical challenge in these distributed models is the efficient and secure sampling of noise. Various protocols have been proposed for securely sampling discrete Gaussian noise and other distributions, but they often suffer from isolated theoretical analyses and varying security assumptions [6][7][3]. Recent benchmarking efforts have highlighted the lack of comprehensive experimental evaluations for these sampling protocols, noting that while some methods are resilient to Byzantine attackers, their efficiency and utility trade-offs require further empirical study [3]. Our work builds upon these foundations by proposing Verifiable Distributed Differential Privacy (VDDP), which specifically addresses the risks of unfaithful noise execution in distributed client-server-verifier setups, offering efficient mechanisms that significantly improve upon previous state-of-the-art verifiable DP solutions in terms of proof generation and communication costs.
