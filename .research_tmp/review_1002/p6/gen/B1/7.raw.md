## Related Works

The alignment of Large Language Models (LLMs) with human values has become a critical area of research, primarily driven by the need to mitigate harmful outputs. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is a dominant paradigm for this alignment, where preference modeling and RL are used to finetune models to act as helpful and harmless assistants \[[Yuntao Bai and Andy Jones and Kamal Ndousse and Amanda Askell and Anna Chen and Nova DasSarma and Dawn Drain and Stanislav Fort and Deep Ganguli and Tom Henighan and Nicholas Joseph and Saurav Kadavath and Jackson Kernion and Tom Conerly and Sheer El-Showk and Nelson Elhage and Zac Hatfield-Dodds and Danny Hernandez and Tristan Hume and Scott Johnston and Shauna Kravec and Liane Lovitt and Neel Nanda and Catherine Olsson and Dario Amodei and Tom Brown and Jack Clark and Sam McCandlish and Chris Olah and Ben Mann and Jared Kaplan](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862v1)\]. This training improves performance on various NLP evaluations and is compatible with specialized skills \[[Yuntao Bai and Andy Jones and Kamal Ndousse and Amanda Askell and Anna Chen and Nova DasSarma and Dawn Drain and Stanislav Fort and Deep Ganguli and Tom Henighan and Nicholas Joseph and Saurav Kadavath and Jackson Kernion and Tom Conerly and Sheer El-Showk and Nelson Elhage and Zac Hatfield-Dodds and Danny Hernandez and Tristan Hume and Scott Johnston and Shauna Kravec and Liane Lovitt and Neel Nanda and Catherine Olsson and Dario Amodei and Tom Brown and Jack Clark and Sam McCandlish and Chris Olah and Ben Mann and Jared Kaplan](http://arxiv.org/abs/2204.05862v1)\]. However, the robustness of these safety measures is increasingly under scrutiny. Red teaming efforts have shown that while RLHF models become harder to red team as they scale, other model types exhibit flat trends in vulnerability \[[Deep Ganguli and Liane Lovitt and Jackson Kernion and Amanda Askell and Yuntao Bai and Saurav Kadavath and Ben Mann and Ethan Perez and Nicholas Schiefer and Kamal Ndousse and Andy Jones and Sam Bowman and Anna Chen and Tom Conerly and Nova DasSarma and Dawn Drain and Nelson Elhage and Sheer El-Showk and Stanislav Fort and Zac Hatfield-Dodds and Tom Henighan and Danny Hernandez and Tristan Hume and Josh Jacobson and Scott Johnston and Shauna Kravec and Catherine Olsson and Sam Ringer and Eli Tran-Johnson and Dario Amodei and Tom Brown and Nicholas Joseph and Sam McCandlish and Chris Olah and Jared Kaplan and Jack Clark](http://arxiv.org/abs/2209.07858v2)\]. Furthermore, subversive fine-tuning techniques, such as using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) or "Shadow Alignment," can easily undo safety training with minimal computational resources, demonstrating the brittleness of current alignment methods \[[Simon Lermen and Charlie Rogers-Smith and Jeffrey Ladish](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.20624v2)\]\[[Xianjun Yang and Xiao Wang and Qi Zhang and Linda Petzold and William Yang Wang and Xun Zhao and Dahua Lin](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.02949v1)\]. Even without malicious intent, fine-tuning aligned models can compromise safety \[[Xiangyu Qi and
Yi Zeng and
Tinghao Xie and
Pin{-}Yu Chen and
Ruoxi Jia and
Prateek Mittal and
Peter Henderson](https://ref.local/6)\]. Additionally, simple adaptive jailbreaking attacks leveraging logprobs or random search can achieve high success rates against leading safety-aligned LLMs \[[Andriushchenko](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.02151v4)\].

Understanding the internal mechanisms of these models is essential for developing robust safety controls. The "linear representation hypothesis" posits that high-level concepts are encoded as directions in the model's representation space \[[Kiho Park and
Yo Joong Choe and
Victor Veitch](http://arxiv.org/abs/2311.03658v2)\]. This hypothesis is supported by evidence that linguistic features and world model states are represented linearly, allowing for control via vector arithmetic \[[Nanda](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.00941v2)\]\[[Hernandez](http://arxiv.org/abs/2105.07109v2)\]. Recent work has specifically identified that refusal behavior in conversational LLMs is mediated by a single direction in the activation space; erasing this direction prevents refusal, while adding it elicits refusal \[[Andy Arditi and
Oscar Obeso and
Aaquib Syed and
Daniel Paleka and
Nina Panickssery and
Wes Gurnee and
Neel Nanda](http://arxiv.org/abs/2406.11717v3)\]. This insight has led to the development of activation steering methods, which modify internal activations during inference to control model behavior without weight updates.

Several techniques have been proposed for activation steering. Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) computes steering vectors by averaging the difference in residual stream activations between positive and negative examples, allowing for precise control over behaviors like factual accuracy \[[Nina Panickssery and Nick Gabrieli and Julian Schulz and Meg Tong and Evan Hubinger and Alexander Matt Turner](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.06681v4)\]. Similarly, Activation Addition (ActAdd) contrasts intermediate activations on prompt pairs to steer outputs toward desired properties, such as detoxification \[[Alexander Matt Turner and Lisa Thiergart and Gavin Leech and David Udell and Juan J. Vazquez and Ulisse Mini and Monte MacDiarmid](http://arxiv.org/abs/2308.10248v5)\]. More advanced methods include Conditional Activation Steering (CAST), which selectively applies steering based on input context to enable fine-grained control \[[Bruce W. Lee and Inkit Padhi and Karthikeyan Natesan Ramamurthy and Erik Miehling and Pierre Dognin and Manish Nagireddy and Amit Dhurandhar](http://arxiv.org/abs/2409.05907v3)\], and Spectral Editing of Activations (SEA), which projects inputs into directions with maximal covariance with positive demonstrations \[[Yifu Qiu and
Zheng Zhao and
Yftah Ziser and
Anna Korhonen and
Edoardo Maria Ponti and
Shay B. Cohen](http://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09719v3)\]. Affine Concept Editing (ACE) further refines this by combining affine subspace projection and activation addition to reliably control refusal responses \[[Thomas Marshall and Adam Scherlis and Nora Belrose](http://arxiv.org/abs/2411.09003v3)\]. These methods are grounded in the broader field of Representation Engineering (RepE), which focuses on manipulating high-level cognitive phenomena in deep neural networks to enhance transparency and safety \[[Andy Zou and Long Phan and Sarah Chen and James Campbell and Phillip Guo and Richard Ren and Alexander Pan and Xuwang Yin and Mantas Mazeika and Ann-Kathrin Dombrowski and Shashwat Goel and Nathaniel Li and Michael J. Byun and Zifan Wang and Alex Mallen and Steven Basart and Sanmi Koyejo and Dawn Song and Matt Fredrikson and J. Zico Kolter and Dan Hendrycks](http://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01405v4)\].

Despite the efficacy of these steering methods, existing approaches often rely on predefined refusal templates or manual analysis to identify viable steering directions. This dependency on specific output tokens or assumptions about refusal behavior limits their applicability, particularly in adversarial settings or with weakly aligned models. Our work, COSMIC, addresses this gap by introducing an automated framework for direction selection that uses cosine similarity to identify viable steering directions and target layers entirely independent of model outputs. By removing the need for assumptions about specific refusal tokens, COSMIC provides a robust mechanism for steering models toward safer behavior across a wide range of alignment conditions.
