## Background

Dense passage retrieval has emerged as a critical component in open-domain question answering systems, offering significant improvements over traditional sparse vector space models such as TF-IDF and BM25. Early work demonstrated that retrieval can be effectively implemented using dense representations learned via a dual-encoder framework, where embeddings are derived from a small number of questions and passages. This approach, exemplified by Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR), outperforms strong Lucene-BM25 systems by 9%–19% in top-20 passage retrieval accuracy and establishes state-of-the-art results on multiple open-domain QA benchmarks [1].

The foundation of dense retrieval relies on the ability to encode text into semantically meaningful vector representations that can be compared using shallow similarity operations, such as inner products or cosine similarity. A major challenge in utilizing pre-trained language models like BERT for this purpose is computational efficiency. Standard BERT architectures require both sentences to be fed into the network simultaneously for pair-wise comparison, resulting in massive computational overhead; for instance, finding the most similar pair in a collection of 10,000 sentences requires approximately 50 million inference computations, taking about 65 hours [2]. To address this, Sentence-BERT (SBERT) introduced siamese and triplet network structures to derive independent sentence embeddings, reducing the search time to approximately 5 seconds while maintaining accuracy [2].

Recent advancements have focused on improving the quality of these sentence embeddings through various training paradigms. SimCSE proposed a simple contrastive learning framework that significantly advances state-of-the-art sentence embeddings. By using standard dropout as a form of data augmentation in an unsupervised contrastive objective, SimCSE achieves performance on par with supervised counterparts, improving Spearman’s correlation on semantic textual similarity tasks by 4.2% for unsupervised and 2.2% for supervised models compared to previous best results [3]. This method regularizes the anisotropic space of pre-trained embeddings, making them more uniform and better aligned for retrieval tasks [3].

Various architectures and training strategies have been explored to enhance dense retrieval performance. Contriever introduced a fully unsupervised dense retriever, demonstrating that effective retrieval models can be trained without labeled data [4]. Additionally, decoder-based approaches have been investigated, such as RePLAMA, which utilizes a decoder-based dense retriever for open-domain question answering [5]. Other methods, like PromptReps, enhance dense retrieval by incorporating prompt-based representations to improve the encoding process [6].

The evaluation of these retrieval systems is often conducted on large-scale, human-generated datasets. The MS MARCO dataset comprises over one million anonymized questions sampled from Bing search logs, paired with human-generated answers and millions of web passages, providing a robust benchmark for machine reading comprehension and question answering [7]. Similarly, the Natural Questions benchmark serves as a standard for evaluating question answering systems, providing real-world user queries for assessment [8].

Recent research has questioned the relative contributions of pre-training versus fine-tuning in dense retrievers. It has been argued that retrieval knowledge is primarily acquired during the pre-training phase, with fine-tuning serving mainly to adjust neuron activations rather than reorganizing fundamental knowledge. This hypothesis was initially studied in the context of BERT-based encoders using DPR as the representative model. However, the universality of this claim across different representation approaches (e.g., CLS tokens vs. mean pooling) and backbone architectures (encoder-only vs. decoder-only) remains an area of active investigation, particularly when extending analyses to include diverse datasets like MS MARCO and Natural Questions [1][4][5][7][8].
