## Related Works

The evaluation of Large Language Models (LLMs) has expanded beyond accuracy to include broader desiderata such as robustness, toxicity, and fairness. The Holistic Evaluation of Language Models (HELM) framework introduced a multi-metric approach, measuring seven metrics including fairness and bias across 16 core scenarios for 30 prominent models [1]. Similarly, BIG-bench focuses on tasks believed to be beyond current capabilities, noting that social bias often increases with model scale in ambiguous contexts [2]. However, conventional fairness evaluations often rely on discrete accuracy-based metrics, which may fail to capture the nuanced impact of model uncertainty on decision-making reliability.

Research into bias in NLP has historically focused on identifying and mitigating social stereotypes. Early work introduced the WinoBias benchmark for coreference resolution, demonstrating that neural systems link gendered pronouns to stereotypical entities with higher accuracy than anti-stereotypical ones [3]. Subsequent studies confirmed systematic gender bias in coreference systems using Winograd schema-style minimal pairs [4] and expanded this to large-scale datasets of real-world sentences to evaluate bias in machine translation and coreference [5]. More recently, studies have shown that LLMs amplify gender biases beyond human perceptions and ground truth statistics, often providing factually inaccurate rationalizations for their biased choices [6]. The Bias Benchmark for Question Answering (BBQ) further highlighted that models rely on stereotypes when context is under-informative, with accuracy gaps widening when correct answers conflict with social biases [7].

Despite these advances, the relationship between model uncertainty and fairness remains underexplored. Uncertainty quantification (UQ) is critical for assessing the trustworthiness of LLMs, as erroneous generations and hallucinations pose risks in safety-sensitive applications [8]. Various methods have been proposed to estimate uncertainty, including semantic entropy, which accounts for linguistic invariances in natural language generation [9], and approaches that shift attention to relevant tokens to improve predictive uncertainty quantification in free-form LLMs [10]. Recent benchmarks have integrated UQ to reveal that larger models may display greater uncertainty and that instruction fine-tuning can increase this uncertainty [11]. However, evaluations of UQ methods themselves face challenges, such as spurious interactions with response length that can distort metrics like AUROC [12].

The intersection of uncertainty and fairness is an emerging area. While fairness is often defined as the absence of discrimination, it competes with utility in classification tasks [13]. Recent work suggests that aleatoric uncertainty, or data ambiguity, is a key factor in algorithmic fairness, with samples of low aleatoric uncertainty being modeled more fairly than those with high uncertainty [13]. This perspective opens avenues for using prediction uncertainty to improve the fairness-utility trade-off. Furthermore, the transfer of bias from pre-trained models to prompt-adapted models has been shown to be strongly correlated, highlighting the importance of ensuring fairness in the base models [14]. By monitoring fairness dynamics during training, researchers have found that biases can emerge suddenly and that larger models may exhibit more bias than smaller ones, even when gender is unspecified [15]. Our work builds on these foundations by proposing an uncertainty-aware fairness metric, UCerF, and a new gender-occupation dataset to address the limitations of conventional accuracy-based evaluations in capturing implicit biases related to model confidence.
