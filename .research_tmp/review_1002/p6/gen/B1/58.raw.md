## Related Works

Conversational Recommender Systems (CRSs) have emerged as a paradigm to overcome the limitations of static recommendation systems, which often struggle to capture fine-grained and dynamic user preferences due to reliance on past interaction history \[[Lei](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00194v1)\]. By enabling direct interaction, CRSs allow systems to query users about specific item attributes, thereby addressing the cold-start problem and enhancing personalization \[[Lei](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00194v1)\]\[[Li](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12979v5)\]. Early approaches, such as the Estimation-Action-Reflection (EAR) framework, focused on deep interaction between conversational and recommender components to refine user understanding \[[Lei](https://ref.local/1)\]. However, existing methods often utilize attribute feedback implicitly, such as by updating latent user representations, rather than leveraging the explicit structure of user preferences \[[Lei](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00194v1)\].

To address these limitations, recent research has explored explicit modeling of user preferences through graph-based reasoning. The Conversational Path Reasoning (CPR) framework models recommendation as an interactive path reasoning problem on a graph, walking through attribute vertices based on user feedback to prune irrelevant candidates \[[Lei](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00194v1)\]. This approach, instantiated as Simple CPR (SCPR), has demonstrated significant improvements over state-of-the-art methods like EAR and CRM in multi-round scenarios, particularly when dealing with a large number of attributes \[[Lei](http://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00194v1)\]. In parallel, efforts to unify attributes and items within a single decision space have been proposed to handle the exploration-exploitation trade-off more effectively. The Conversational Thompson Sampling (ConTS) model seamlessly integrates attributes and items into the same arm space, automatically managing the trade-off without relying on fragile, hand-crafted decision functions \[[Li](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12979v5)\]. ConTS has shown superior performance in success rate and conversation efficiency compared to methods like Conversational UCB (ConUCB) and EAR \[[Li](http://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12979v5)\].

Despite these advancements, CRSs face challenges related to data distribution and the quality of interaction. Existing datasets often suffer from a long-tail problem, where a large proportion of items are rarely mentioned, leading to reduced recommendation diversity and potential user boredom \[[Zhao](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.11650v1)\]. To mitigate this, the LOT-CRS framework simulates balanced datasets and employs pre-training tasks and retrieval-augmented fine-tuning to improve long-tail recommendation performance \[[Zhao](http://arxiv.org/abs/2307.11650v1)\]. Furthermore, the integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) has opened new avenues for zero-shot recommendation and retrieval-augmented generation, although specific methodologies for leveraging LLMs as conversational agents remain an active area of research \[[He](https://ref.local/8)\]\[[Yang](https://ref.local/9)\].

The concept of using generative models to simulate user behavior or evaluate outputs draws inspiration from advancements in reward modeling and verification. Generative Reward Models (GenRM) have been developed to unify Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and AI Feedback (RLAIF), training LLMs on self-generated reasoning traces to produce synthetic preference labels that align closely with human judgments \[[Mahan](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12832v1)\]. Similarly, Generative Verifiers utilize next-token prediction objectives to rank candidate solutions, outperforming discriminative classifiers in tasks requiring complex reasoning \[[Zhang](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15240v3)\]. These techniques, which involve generating multiple candidates and selecting the best based on a learned reward or verification signal, provide the foundational inspiration for the proposed GRSU framework. By adapting these generative reward mechanisms to simulate user feedback—specifically through coarse-grained item scoring and fine-grained attribute critique—our work bridges the gap between advanced LLM reasoning capabilities and the interactive requirements of conversational recommendation \[[Mahan](http://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12832v1)\]\[[Zhang](http://arxiv.org/abs/2408.15240v3)\].
