## Related Works

Speech-driven 3D facial animation aims to generate realistic, expressive facial motions synchronized with audio input. This task is inherently challenging due to the complex geometry of human faces, the ill-posed nature of the cross-modal mapping, and the scarcity of high-quality 3D audio-visual data \[[Yingruo Fan and
                  Zhaojiang Lin and
                  Jun Saito and
                  Wenping Wang and
                  Taku Komura](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329v4)\]\[[Jinbo Xing and
                  Menghan Xia and
                  Yuechen Zhang and
                  Xiaodong Cun and
                  Jue Wang and
                  Tien{-}Tsin Wong](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379v2)\]. Early approaches, such as VOCA, focused on factoring identity from facial motion using 4D datasets, enabling the animation of unseen subjects and providing controls for speaking style and pose \[[Daniel Cudeiro and
                  Timo Bolkart and
                  Cassidy Laidlaw and
                  Anurag Ranjan and
                  Michael J. Black](http://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03079v1)\]. However, these methods often struggled with the "regression-to-mean" problem, resulting in over-smoothed and less vivid facial motions \[[Jinbo Xing and
                  Menghan Xia and
                  Yuechen Zhang and
                  Xiaodong Cun and
                  Jue Wang and
                  Tien{-}Tsin Wong](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379v2)\].

To address the limitations of deterministic regression, recent research has explored stochastic and autoregressive models. FaceFormer introduced a Transformer-based autoregressive model that encodes long-term audio context to predict 3D face meshes, utilizing biased attention mechanisms to align audio and motion modalities \[[Yingruo Fan and
                  Zhaojiang Lin and
                  Jun Saito and
                  Wenping Wang and
                  Taku Komura](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329v4)\]. Similarly, CodeTalker cast the animation task as a code query problem within a discrete motion prior space, using a temporal autoregressive model to synthesize vivid motions while ensuring lip-sync \[[Jinbo Xing and
                  Menghan Xia and
                  Yuechen Zhang and
                  Xiaodong Cun and
                  Jue Wang and
                  Tien{-}Tsin Wong](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02379v2)\]. More recently, diffusion-based models have emerged to capture the non-deterministic nature of facial cues. FaceDiffuser was among the first to employ diffusion techniques for this task, trained on both 3D vertex and blendshape datasets to improve compatibility with existing animation pipelines \[[Stefan Stan and
                  Kazi Injamamul Haque and
                  Zerrin Yumak](http://arxiv.org/abs/2309.11306v1)\]. 3DiFACE further advanced this by proposing a lightweight audio-conditioned diffusion model that allows for motion editing and personalization via fine-tuning on short videos \[[Balamurugan Thambiraja and
                  Sadegh Aliakbarian and
                  Darren Cosker and
                  Justus Thies](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.00870v1)\]. Media2Face extended this paradigm by introducing a Generalized Neural Parametric Facial Asset (GNPFA) and a diffusion model in its latent space, accepting multi-modality guidance from audio, text, and images \[[Qingcheng Zhao and
                  Pengyu Long and
                  Qixuan Zhang and
                  Dafei Qin and
                  Han Liang and
                  Longwen Zhang and
                  Yingliang Zhang and
                  Jingyi Yu and
                  Lan Xu](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15687v2)\].

A significant focus in recent literature has been the integration of emotional expressivity and the disentanglement of speech content from emotional cues. Existing methods often produce satisfactory lip movements but lack dramatic emotional expressions \[[Cao](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02008v2)\]. To address this, EMOTE proposed decoupled losses for speech and emotion, leveraging the observation that speech deformations are spatially localized and high-frequency, whereas emotional expressions are global and low-frequency \[[Dan{\v{e}}{\v{c}}ek](http://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08990v2)\]. EmoTalk introduced an emotion disentangling encoder to separate emotion and content, contributing a large-scale 3D emotional talking face dataset (3D-ETF) to train the network \[[Peng](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11089v2)\]. Another approach, presented in \[[Cao](http://arxiv.org/abs/2301.02008v2)\], proposed an emotion controller module to learn the relationship between emotion variations and facial parameters, enabling continuous adjustment of expression type and intensity.

The use of self-supervised speech representations has become a standard practice to mitigate data scarcity. Models like HuBERT and wav2vec 2.0 have demonstrated that learning powerful representations from speech audio alone can outperform semi-supervised methods \[[Alexei Baevski and
                  Yuhao Zhou and
                  Abdelrahman Mohamed and
                  Michael Auli](http://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11477v3)\]\[[Wei{-}Ning Hsu and
                  Benjamin Bolte and
                  Yao{-}Hung Hubert Tsai and
                  Kushal Lakhotia and
                  Ruslan Salakhutdinov and
                  Abdelrahman Mohamed](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07447v1)\]. HuBERT, in particular, utilizes offline clustering to provide aligned target labels for a BERT-like prediction loss, effectively learning combined acoustic and language models \[[Wei{-}Ning Hsu and
                  Benjamin Bolte and
                  Yao{-}Hung Hubert Tsai and
                  Kushal Lakhotia and
                  Ruslan Salakhutdinov and
                  Abdelrahman Mohamed](http://arxiv.org/abs/2106.07447v1)\]. These pre-trained models are now widely integrated into facial animation pipelines. FaceFormer integrates self-supervised pre-trained speech representations to cope with data scarcity \[[Yingruo Fan and
                  Zhaojiang Lin and
                  Jun Saito and
                  Wenping Wang and
                  Taku Komura](http://arxiv.org/abs/2112.05329v4)\]. FaceXHuBERT employs the HuBERT model to capture personalized and subtle cues, such as identity and emotion, without requiring a large lexicon, achieving superior realism and faster inference compared to transformer-based sequential models \[[Haque](http://arxiv.org/abs/2303.05416v1)\]. Media2Face also utilizes audio as a primary modality for guidance within its diffusion framework \[[Qingcheng Zhao and
                  Pengyu Long and
                  Qixuan Zhang and
                  Dafei Qin and
                  Han Liang and
                  Longwen Zhang and
                  Yingliang Zhang and
                  Jingyi Yu and
                  Lan Xu](http://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15687v2)\].

Despite these advancements, a critical issue remains in the use of self-supervised audio encoders. Due to the prevalence of phonetically similar syllables with distinct lip shapes, near-homophone syllables often exhibit significant coupling in self-supervised feature spaces. This leads to an averaging effect in lip motion generation, reducing the precision and naturalness of the resulting animations. While methods like Wav2Lip focus on lip-sync accuracy in unconstrained videos \[[K. R. Prajwal and
                  Rudrabha Mukhopadhyay and
                  Vinay P. Namboodiri and
                  C. V. Jawahar](http://arxiv.org/abs/2008.10010v1)\] and Mimic explores speaking style disentanglement \[[Hui Fu and
                  Zeqing Wang and
                  Ke Gong and
                  Keze Wang and
                  Tianshui Chen and
                  Haojie Li and
                  Haifeng Zeng and
                  Wenxiong Kang](http://arxiv.org/abs/2312.10877v1)\], the specific challenge of semantic decorrelation within the audio feature space to alleviate this averaging effect remains an area for further improvement.
