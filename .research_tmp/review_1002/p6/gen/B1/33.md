## Related Works

Catastrophic forgetting, the significant degradation in performance on previously learned tasks when a neural network is trained sequentially on new tasks, remains a central challenge in continual learning (CL) [1][2][3]. To mitigate this, existing methodologies are broadly categorized into regularization-based, replay-based, and architectural approaches. Regularization-based methods constrain weight updates to preserve knowledge of past tasks, often formulated as second-order Taylor approximations of the loss function [3]. These approaches include Elastic Weight Consolidation and Kronecker factored Laplace approximation, which rely on accurate Hessian matrix approximations to balance optimization and generalization [3].

Replay-based methods address forgetting by storing a subset of past data in a memory buffer and interleaving it with new data during training [4][5]. Deep Generative Replay, for instance, utilizes a cooperative dual model architecture consisting of a generator and a solver to sample training data for previous tasks, thereby alleviating the need for large memory storage of raw data [4]. Recent theoretical analyses have characterized the performance of replay-based CL models, revealing that buffer size and model capacity significantly impact the forgetting rate [5]. While these methods have shown superior empirical performance, the optimality of the standard concurrent rehearsal strategy—where past and new data are trained simultaneously—has not been rigorously established theoretically [5].

Architectural approaches aim to prevent interference by modifying the network structure. Progressive Neural Networks, for example, use lateral connections to leverage prior knowledge while remaining immune to forgetting by expanding the network for new tasks [6]. Other methods, such as Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM) and its efficient variant A-GEM, constrain the optimization space by projecting gradients to ensure they do not conflict with the gradients of previous tasks [7][2]. Similarly, Gradient Projection Memory (GPM) and Trust Region Gradient Projection (TRGP) propose taking gradient steps in directions orthogonal to the subspaces important for past tasks, thereby minimizing interference and facilitating knowledge transfer [8][9].

Theoretical understanding of catastrophic forgetting has advanced significantly through the analysis of linear models and the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime. Studies have demonstrated that overparameterization can improve generalization performance and reduce forgetting in multilayer perceptrons, with effects comparable to state-of-the-art CL algorithms [10]. In the NTK regime, Orthogonal Gradient Descent (OGD) has been proven robust to catastrophic forgetting, with generalization bounds derived for both SGD and OGD [11]. Furthermore, the NTK overlap matrix has been introduced as a measure of task similarity, showing that the impact of forgetting increases as tasks align [12]. Recent work has provided explicit forms for expected forgetting and generalization error in overparameterized linear models, highlighting the critical roles of task similarity, task ordering, and overparameterization [13][14]. Specifically, it has been shown that training tasks with larger eigenvalues in their population data covariance matrices later in a sequence tends to increase forgetting, while appropriate step size selection can mitigate this effect [14]. Additionally, theoretical bounds on forgetting in linear regression settings have been established, connecting CL to alternating projections and the Kaczmarz method [15][16].

Despite these advances, a comprehensive theoretical comparison of concurrent versus sequential rehearsal strategies in overparameterized models remains lacking. While prior work has analyzed the impact of task ordering and similarity on forgetting [13][14], it has not explicitly characterized the benefits of sequential revisiting of past data compared to standard concurrent training. This gap motivates the present work, which provides the first comprehensive theoretical analysis of rehearsal-based CL, comparing concurrent and sequential strategies and proposing a hybrid approach that leverages insights from task similarity analysis [12][13].
