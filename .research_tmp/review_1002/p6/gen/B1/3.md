## Related Works

Recent advancements in Text-to-Speech (TTS) synthesis have shifted from traditional concatenative and statistical parametric approaches to neural network-based end-to-end models, significantly improving speech quality [1]. Early prominent methods, such as Tacotron 2, typically generate mel-spectrograms from text before synthesizing speech via vocoders, though these autoregressive models often suffer from slow inference speeds and limited robustness against word skipping or repetition [1]. To address these limitations, non-autoregressive architectures like FastSpeech were introduced, utilizing Transformer-based feed-forward networks to generate mel-spectrograms in parallel [1]. By extracting attention alignments from teacher models for duration prediction, FastSpeech achieves comparable speech quality to autoregressive models while accelerating mel-spectrogram generation by 270x and end-to-end synthesis by 38x [1].

Subsequent improvements in FastSpeech 2 addressed the complexities of the teacher-student distillation pipeline and information loss in distilled targets by directly training on ground-truth data and incorporating explicit variation information such as pitch, energy, and duration as conditional inputs [2]. This approach not only simplified the training process, achieving a 3x speed-up over FastSpeech, but also enhanced voice quality to surpass autoregressive models in some cases [2]. Furthermore, FastSpeech 2s introduced the capability to directly generate speech waveforms from text in parallel, enabling fully end-to-end inference with even faster speeds [2].

Despite these architectural advancements, precise control over prosodic attributes remains a critical challenge. Traditional methods for prosody manipulation often rely on specialized modules or additional training, which limits their flexibility for post-hoc adjustments. For instance, Style Tokens (GSTs) were proposed as a bank of embeddings jointly trained within Tacotron to model acoustic expressiveness without explicit labels, allowing for control over speed and speaking style independent of text content [3]. Similarly, hierarchical generative modeling using Variational Autoencoders (VAE) has been employed to control latent attributes such as speaking style, accent, and background noise through categorical and Gaussian latent variables [4]. Other approaches include learning latent embedding spaces of prosody from reference acoustic representations to achieve prosody transfer across different speakers [5].

More recent work has focused on specific prosodic controls, such as speaking rate. One approach conditions the speaking rate inside the duration predictor of non-autoregressive TTS models to achieve implicit control, demonstrating higher subjective scores and lower speaker rate errors compared to baselines [6]. Alternative methods explore temporal control of prosodic variation [7] or direct manipulation of encoder output embeddings to adjust speaking rate in end-to-end models [8]. While these methods offer significant control, they often require specific architectural modifications or additional training phases.

In parallel, mispronunciation correction has traditionally relied on grapheme-to-phoneme dictionaries, which are less practical in low-resource settings. Recent efforts have explored using speech from non-target speakers for one-off correction of mispronunciations in grapheme-input TTS systems [9]. However, existing approaches for both prosody manipulation and mispronunciation correction often depend on specialized modules or additional training, limiting their capacity for inference-time refinement. This gap highlights the need for model-agnostic methods that can manipulate internal representations in pre-trained TTS models to achieve post-hoc control of prosody and pronunciation without retraining.
