## Related Works

Generative Retrieval (GR) represents a paradigm shift in information retrieval by replacing traditional index-retrieve pipelines with autoregressive models that directly generate document identifiers (docids) for a given query. This approach unifies indexing and retrieval into a single end-to-end process, eliminating the need for separate sparse or dense indexes [1][2][3]. Early foundational work, such as GENRE, demonstrated that entities could be retrieved by generating their unique names token-by-token, significantly reducing memory footprints compared to dense vector approaches [1]. Similarly, the Differentiable Search Index (DSI) established that a single Transformer can encode corpus information within its parameters, mapping queries directly to document IDs [2]. Subsequent systems, including DynamicRetriever, Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI), and Ultron, further refined this model-based indexer paradigm, showing that embedding document-level information into model parameters allows for dynamic and updatable retrieval processes [3][4][5].

A critical component of GR is the design of document identifiers. Existing methods vary significantly in how they represent documents, ranging from numeric IDs to text-based strings. Numeric-based docids, often derived from clustering or quantization, offer high efficiency but may lack semantic richness [6][7]. In contrast, text-based docids, such as document titles or substrings, leverage the semantic alignment with language models' pretrained knowledge [8][9]. For instance, SEAL proposed using n-grams as identifiers to avoid forcing hierarchical structures on the search space, while Multiview Identifiers Enhanced Generative Retrieval argued that synthetic and text-based identifiers complement each other to capture holistic passage content [8][9]. Recent advancements like GenRet have explored learning to tokenize documents into discrete representations via auto-encoding, aiming to capture complete semantic information in a fully end-to-end manner [6]. Other frameworks, such as RIPOR, have focused on optimizing the ranking process by introducing prefix-oriented ranking and relevance-based docid construction to improve performance on large-scale benchmarks [10].

Despite these advancements, most GR research has been conducted on static corpora, raising questions about the models' adaptability to dynamic environments where documents are continuously added. Recent studies have begun to address this gap. CLEVER introduced a continual learning framework for GR, proposing Incremental Product Quantization to encode new documents with low computational cost and a memory-augmented mechanism to prevent catastrophic forgetting [11]. Similarly, DSI++ and CorpusBrain++ have explored updating transformer memory and continual pre-training for knowledge-intensive tasks, respectively [12][13]. Empirical evaluations on dynamic corpora, such as the StreamingQA benchmark, indicate that GR models are more adaptable to evolving knowledge and more efficient in terms of inference FLOPs and storage footprint compared to dual encoder baselines [14]. However, the specific impact of docid granularity and type (numeric vs. text-based) on generalization to unseen documents in dynamic settings remains an area of active investigation, with recent findings suggesting that fine-grained, text-based docids offer superior generalization due to their lexical diversity and semantic alignment [8][9][14].
