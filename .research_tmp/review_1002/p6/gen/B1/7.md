## Related Works

The alignment of Large Language Models (LLMs) with human values has become a critical area of research, primarily driven by the need to mitigate harmful outputs. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is a dominant paradigm for this alignment, where preference modeling and RL are used to finetune models to act as helpful and harmless assistants [1]. This training improves performance on various NLP evaluations and is compatible with specialized skills [1]. However, the robustness of these safety measures is increasingly under scrutiny. Red teaming efforts have shown that while RLHF models become harder to red team as they scale, other model types exhibit flat trends in vulnerability [2]. Furthermore, subversive fine-tuning techniques, such as using Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) or "Shadow Alignment," can easily undo safety training with minimal computational resources, demonstrating the brittleness of current alignment methods [3][4]. Even without malicious intent, fine-tuning aligned models can compromise safety [5]. Additionally, simple adaptive jailbreaking attacks leveraging logprobs or random search can achieve high success rates against leading safety-aligned LLMs [6].

Understanding the internal mechanisms of these models is essential for developing robust safety controls. The "linear representation hypothesis" posits that high-level concepts are encoded as directions in the model's representation space [7]. This hypothesis is supported by evidence that linguistic features and world model states are represented linearly, allowing for control via vector arithmetic [8][9]. Recent work has specifically identified that refusal behavior in conversational LLMs is mediated by a single direction in the activation space; erasing this direction prevents refusal, while adding it elicits refusal [10]. This insight has led to the development of activation steering methods, which modify internal activations during inference to control model behavior without weight updates.

Several techniques have been proposed for activation steering. Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) computes steering vectors by averaging the difference in residual stream activations between positive and negative examples, allowing for precise control over behaviors like factual accuracy [11]. Similarly, Activation Addition (ActAdd) contrasts intermediate activations on prompt pairs to steer outputs toward desired properties, such as detoxification [12]. More advanced methods include Conditional Activation Steering (CAST), which selectively applies steering based on input context to enable fine-grained control [13], and Spectral Editing of Activations (SEA), which projects inputs into directions with maximal covariance with positive demonstrations [14]. Affine Concept Editing (ACE) further refines this by combining affine subspace projection and activation addition to reliably control refusal responses [15]. These methods are grounded in the broader field of Representation Engineering (RepE), which focuses on manipulating high-level cognitive phenomena in deep neural networks to enhance transparency and safety [16].

Despite the efficacy of these steering methods, existing approaches often rely on predefined refusal templates or manual analysis to identify viable steering directions. This dependency on specific output tokens or assumptions about refusal behavior limits their applicability, particularly in adversarial settings or with weakly aligned models. Our work, COSMIC, addresses this gap by introducing an automated framework for direction selection that uses cosine similarity to identify viable steering directions and target layers entirely independent of model outputs. By removing the need for assumptions about specific refusal tokens, COSMIC provides a robust mechanism for steering models toward safer behavior across a wide range of alignment conditions.
