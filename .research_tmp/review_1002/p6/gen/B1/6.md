## Related Works

Summarizing long-form narratives, such as books, movies, and TV scripts, presents significant challenges due to the extreme length of the input and the need to capture intricate plotlines, character interactions, and thematic coherence. Traditional Transformer-based models face a fundamental limitation in this domain: their self-attention mechanism scales quadratically with sequence length, resulting in prohibitive memory and computational costs for documents exceeding standard context windows [1][2][3][4]. To address this, several efficient Transformer variants have been proposed. BigBird introduces a sparse attention mechanism that reduces complexity to linear, enabling the processing of sequences up to eight times longer than previous models [1]. Similarly, Longformer employs a combination of local windowed and global attention to handle documents of thousands of tokens, with its encoder-decoder variant (LED) demonstrating effectiveness on long-document summarization tasks [2]. The Reformer utilizes locality-sensitive hashing to achieve $O(L \log L)$ complexity, while Linformer approximates self-attention with low-rank matrices to reduce both time and space complexity to $O(n)$ [3][4]. More recent approaches, such as Unlimiformer, wrap existing pretrained encoder-decoder models to offload cross-attention to a k-nearest-neighbor index, allowing for the processing of inputs up to 500k tokens without truncation [5]. Additionally, Position Interpolation extends the context window of RoPE-based models like LLaMA to 32,768 tokens with minimal fine-tuning, preserving quality on long-context tasks [6].

Despite these architectural advancements, current language models often struggle to robustly utilize information across long contexts. Research indicates that performance degrades significantly when relevant information is located in the middle of the input, a phenomenon known as the "lost in the middle" effect [7]. To mitigate this, multi-agent frameworks have emerged as a promising strategy. Chain-of-Agents (CoA) harnesses multi-agent collaboration to process long contexts by assigning short segments to worker agents who sequentially communicate, followed by a manager agent that synthesizes the contributions [8]. This approach addresses the limitations of retrieval-augmented generation and simple window extension by ensuring focused reasoning over segmented portions of the text [8].

Specific to narrative summarization, recent work has focused on both extractive and abstractive methods. For novel chapters, extractive summarization requires aligning reference summary sentences with chapter sentences, a task complicated by extreme paraphrasing and generalization [9]. In the domain of movie scripts, scene saliency has been identified as a critical factor; a two-stage abstractive approach that first identifies salient scenes and then generates a summary from only those scenes outperforms methods that process the entire script [10]. This aligns with the observation that only a fraction of scenes in a movie script are essential for understanding the overall narrative [10].

Evaluation of long-form summarization remains a critical challenge. Traditional metrics like ROUGE and BERTScore often fail to capture factual accuracy and coherence in long narratives [11]. BooookScore was introduced to systematically evaluate the coherence of LLM-based book-length summarizers by measuring the proportion of sentences free from specific error types, revealing that closed-source LLMs generally outperform open-source models in coherence [12]. Furthermore, NarrativeFactScore proposes an "Agent-as-a-Judge" framework that leverages Character Knowledge Graphs to assess factual consistency and provide actionable guidance for refining summaries, addressing the limitations of lexical similarity metrics [11]. These developments underscore the necessity for frameworks that not only handle long contexts efficiently but also ensure factual and thematic integrity in the generated summaries.
