## Related Works

The design of optimal tax policies has long been a central topic in public economics, aiming to balance equity and efficiency. Traditional theoretical frameworks, such as the Saez Optimal Taxation model, provide dynamic adjustments to tax rates but often rely on simplified assumptions that fail to capture taxpayer heterogeneity and irrational behavior [1][2]. These models typically utilize elasticity estimates to derive optimal income tax rates, focusing on labor supply responses to determine the trade-off between revenue generation and welfare loss [2][3]. However, such approaches may not adequately address the complexities of real-world socio-economic challenges, including the specific impacts on top labor incomes and the nuances of wage responses in the presence of unemployment [3][4].

The Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) represents a significant policy instrument designed to reduce inequality by providing subsidies to low-income workers, effectively acting as a negative income tax [5][6]. While the EITC has been analyzed for its effectiveness in reducing income distribution disparities, it operates within static or semi-static policy frameworks that lack the adaptability required for dynamic economic environments [5]. Similarly, broader discussions on progressive taxation highlight the need for policies that move beyond basic research to actionable recommendations, yet these often struggle with the implementation of adaptive mechanisms that respond to changing economic conditions [1].

Recent advancements in computational economics have introduced agent-based modeling (ABM) and machine learning to address these limitations. ABM allows for the simulation of heterogeneous agents, providing a more realistic representation of market dynamics and individual behaviors than traditional equilibrium models [7]. Machine learning techniques have been increasingly applied to economics, enabling the discovery of complex patterns in data that traditional statistical methods might miss [8]. Specifically, the "AI Economist" framework utilizes deep reinforcement learning to train social planners that discover tax policies in dynamic economies, effectively trading off economic equality and productivity [9]. This approach demonstrates that AI-driven tax policies can improve the equality-productivity trade-off by 16% over baseline policies, including the Saez framework, by setting higher top tax rates and higher net subsidies for low incomes [9]. Furthermore, these AI-driven policies have shown robustness against emergent tax-gaming strategies and have performed well in experiments with human participants, suggesting their potential for real-world application [9].

Despite these advances, the integration of large language models (LLMs) with agent-based modeling for fiscal policy design remains an emerging area. LLMs have shown progress in financial applications, offering new avenues for processing complex economic data and generating policy recommendations [10]. The proposed TaxAgent framework builds upon these foundations by integrating LLMs with ABM to simulate heterogeneous household behaviors and iteratively optimize tax rates. This approach aims to overcome the rigidity of traditional systems like the U.S. federal income tax and the theoretical constraints of the Saez model, offering a scalable, data-driven framework for evaluating fiscal policies that better accounts for taxpayer heterogeneity and adaptive behavior [8][7][10].
