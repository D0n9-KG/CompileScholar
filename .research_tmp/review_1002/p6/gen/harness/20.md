## Related Works

### Single-Pixel Imaging and Its Solvers

Single-pixel imaging (SPI), rooted in computational ghost imaging [1], replaces an imaging array with a single bucket detector that integrates light modulated by a sequence of spatial patterns, so that image acquisition becomes a linear inverse problem $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{n}$ [2]–[4]. Early systems recovered images with compressed-sensing solvers such as greedy pursuit and $\ell_1$-minimization [2], [4], and the field was later consolidated in reviews of computational SPI and its extensions to nonlinear measurements [5], [6]. Because natural images admit sparsity and total-variation (TV) priors [7], iterative first-order methods — in particular ISTA [8] and its accelerated variant FISTA [9] — became the standard model-based solvers for SPI at low sampling budgets.

Deep end-to-end solvers subsequently mapped measurement vectors directly to images with discriminative networks, reporting clear gains over model-based baselines [10]–[12]. A practical limitation, however, is that such networks are typically trained for a *fixed* compression ratio (CR): a separate model is required for each CR, and the physical measurement operator does not explicitly enter the network. This motivates solvers that (i) retain the CR-flexibility of model-based splittings and (ii) inherit the accuracy and speed of learned networks.

### Plug-and-Play Solvers

The plug-and-play (PnP) framework [13], [14] exploits this flexibility directly. Under a problem splitting such as the alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) [15], [16] or half-quadratic splitting (HQS) [17], the proximal step associated with the regularizer is replaced by an off-the-shelf deep denoiser, so that the *same* algorithm — and the same denoiser — can be used at any CR and with any linear forward operator, since the measurement operator enters only through the data-fidelity step. The PnP-ADMM formulation demonstrated strong performance for sparse-regularized imaging problems [19], and denoiser-prior methods were comprehensively surveyed in [18]. The quality of the implicit prior is dictated by the denoiser: residual CNNs (DnCNN) [20] and nearest-neighbor priors [21] were among the first off-the-shelf denoisers validated in PnP settings, systematic comparative studies [22] and subsequent transformer- and lightweight-based restorers [23], [24] further raised the attainable quality. PnP methods are, however, iterative: they require many proximal/denoising passes, and their convergence can degrade at very low CRs or under high noise, which limits both inference speed and final accuracy.

### Deep Unrolling of Iterative Solvers

Deep unrolling (learned unfolding) takes the complementary route: a truncated iterative optimization is converted into a feed-forward network whose internal parameters — step sizes, regularization strengths, and possibly the proximal operator itself — are learned end-to-end [25], [26]. ADMM-Net [27] unrolled an ADMM splitting for compressed sensing and demonstrated large gains over classical ADMM; unrolling schemes were subsequently developed for MRI [28], [29] and for primal-dual and momentum-gradient methods [30]–[32]. Since only a few stages need to be evaluated at test time, unrolled networks typically combine higher reconstruction accuracy with much faster inference than PnP iterations. The price is rigidity: the unrolled architecture is tied to a specific forward operator, and for CS-type problems including SPI it is additionally tied to the CR at which it was trained — changing the CR requires fine-tuning or full retraining, the same inflexibility observed in end-to-end SPI networks [10]–[12].

### Learned Proximal Operators and Trajectory-Based Losses

A recent line of work closes the gap between the two families by making the proximal operator itself a learned object. Rather than fixing an off-the-shelf denoiser, these methods train a deep image restorer (DIR) to approximate the proximal operator of an implicit — or "ideal" explicit — regularizer, and supervise the training through the reconstruction *trajectory* generated by the solver: learned proximal gradients [33], learned proximal gradient descent [34], and the ProxGrad and ProxGrad++ algorithms, in which a trajectory-based loss trains the learned proximal operator so that successive PnP iterates drive the data-fidelity residual to zero [35], [36]. These works establish that a well-learned proximal operator substantially improves PnP convergence and accuracy, but they remain on the PnP side: the solver is still an iterative PnP loop rather than a truncated, end-to-end trained unrolled network.

### Positioning of the Present Work

We bridge the two families. We design a lightweight DIR and embed it into the unrolling of the HQS and ADMM splittings of the SPI problem, and we introduce a general proximal trajectory (PT) loss that trains the unrolled network so that its DIR acts as the proximal operator of an ideal explicit restoration regularizer — transplanting the ProxGrad-style trajectory objective [35] from PnP iterations into an unrolled architecture. The resulting proximal unrolling networks inherit the CR-flexibility of PnP solvers (a single model across CRs) while attaining the accuracy and speed of unrolled solvers, outperforming previous CR-specific unrolling networks on both counts.
