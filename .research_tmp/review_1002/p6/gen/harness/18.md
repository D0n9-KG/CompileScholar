## Related Work

### Industrial visual anomaly detection

Image-based defect detection for industrial products has been standardized by two widely used benchmarks: MVTec AD, which covers 15 object and texture categories with dense ground-truth defect masks [Bergmann et al., 2019], and ViSA (VisApp), which provides RGB–infrared image pairs across 12 object categories [Bergmann et al., 2018]; a 3D extension, MVTec 3D-AD, targets volumetric defect detection with structured-light scans [Bergmann et al., 2022]. Because defect samples are rare and costly to label, the dominant paradigm has been *unsupervised* anomaly detection, in which models are trained on normal images only. Early deep approaches frame anomaly as deviation from a learned normal data distribution: reconstruction-based methods train autoencoders or GANs on normal data and score anomalies by reconstruction error [Akcay et al., 2018; Schlegl et al., 2019]. A more influential line exploits memory banks of patch-level embeddings: PaDiM models the distribution of patch features and flags deviations [Roth et al., 2020], while PatchCore scales this idea to a multi-scale feature memory bank queried with k-nearest neighbors, reaching state-of-the-art performance on MVTec AD [Roth et al., 2022]. Student–teacher distillation with feature pyramid matching [Yu et al., 2022] and self-reconstruction networks [Jeong et al., 2021] further improve localization accuracy. More recently, the field has moved toward generative synthesis of anomalies during training [Li et al., 2024] and zero-/few-shot detection with CLIP-based vision–language models [Wang et al., 2024]; a recent survey [Zhou et al., 2024] consolidates this landscape. Two limitations of these methods are central to our work. First, leading approaches are built on heavy backbones and large memory banks, making them expensive to run on the compact embedded platforms that are the only realistic option in small and medium enterprise (SME) workshops. Second, most approaches ignore defect labels even when they are available: earlier supervised work trained convolutional classifiers directly on labeled defect images, e.g., for surface defect detection in manufacturing [Zhang et al., 2019], and the standard benchmarks themselves provide dense masks [Bergmann et al., 2019], yet comparatively few recent methods combine dense supervision with modern segmentation models.

### Segmentation foundation models

Dense segmentation for anomaly localization has a long history, from encoder–decoder architectures such as U-Net [Ronneberger et al., 2015] to instance-level models such as Mask R-CNN [He et al., 2017]. The Segment Anything Model (SAM) shifted the setting by learning a *promptable* segmentation system from the large-scale SA-1B dataset: given a point, box, or mask prompt, SAM predicts high-quality object masks with strong zero-shot generalization [Kirillov et al., 2023]. The full model, built on a ViT-H backbone, is too large for real-time edge inference; MobileSAM addresses this by substituting a ViT-T encoder and a one-to-many decoder, halving FLOPs at a modest accuracy cost [Cai et al., 2023]. SAM's promptable interface is a natural fit for industrial quality control, where a detector first hypothesizes a suspicious region and a segmentation model then delineates the defect boundary. A number of recent works have begun adapting SAM and its lightweight variants to industrial anomaly tasks such as defect segmentation and inpainting, but — to our knowledge — none combines promptable segmentation with a supervised anomaly-detection pipeline and validates the result end-to-end on embedded hardware.

### Efficient deployment on embedded platforms

Deploying perception on the production line rather than in the cloud is motivated by latency, bandwidth, privacy, and the low cost of commodity hardware; edge intelligence has been proposed as the missing layer that extends AI to the last mile of industrial systems [Li et al., 2020]. On the hardware side, compact GPU platforms such as the NVIDIA Jetson family are the de facto standard for embedded vision, trading peak throughput for small form factor and low power envelopes [NVIDIA, 2022]. On the software side, two complementary strategies dominate. First, edge-oriented architectures — MobileNetV2 [Sandler et al., 2018], EfficientNet [Tan & Le, 2019], and MobileViT [Mehta & Rastegari, 2022] — are designed from scratch under tight parameter and FLOP budgets. Second, post-hoc compression, in particular structured pruning and learned quantization, is widely used to shrink off-the-shelf models for TensorRT-class inference stacks [Han et al., 2016; Jacob et al., 2018]. Industrial anomaly-detection systems reported in the literature are typically evaluated on workstation or server GPUs; comparatively few report end-to-end deployments on Jetson-class devices, and real production-line validations outside the laboratory are rare.

### Position of this work

KairosAD sits at the intersection of these three threads. It adopts a *supervised* formulation that exploits the dense masks available in the benchmarks — and, by extension, in the QC records of real production lines — uses MobileSAM's promptable segmentation as the localization component rather than a full-size SAM, and is validated not only on MVTec AD and ViSA but also on two Jetson embedded devices and on a live production line at ICE Lab, University of Verona. This combination — supervised promptable segmentation, edge-sized cost, and factory-floor validation — is, to our knowledge, new.
