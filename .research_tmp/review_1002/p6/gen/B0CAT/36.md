**Related Works**

The deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) has been driven by the development of massive foundation models, including GPT-4 [1], PaLM [2], LLaMA [3], and Llama 2 [4]. These models demonstrate state-of-the-art performance in few-shot learning and dialogue, yet their substantial parameter counts create significant computational and memory bottlenecks for resource-constrained environments. While these foundational works establish the capability of LLMs, they primarily target high-end data center infrastructure, leaving a gap in efficient deployment on edge devices with strict power and latency constraints.

To mitigate the high memory and compute demands of LLM inference, several algorithm-level optimization techniques have been proposed, encompassing quantization, pruning, sparsity, and knowledge distillation. Quantization methods such as LLM.int8() [12] and SmoothQuant [14] reduce precision to 8-bit, enabling significant memory savings and speedups with minimal accuracy loss. Pruning and sparsity approaches, including SparseGPT [16], Pruner-Zero [15], and Deja Vu [13], exploit the inherent sparsity of LLMs to reduce inference latency by ignoring or dynamically activating only necessary weights. Furthermore, knowledge distillation frameworks like MiniLLM [18] and DistiLLM [17] compress large teacher models into smaller student models to reduce inference costs. Despite these advancements, many of these techniques are designed for server-side acceleration or require retraining, and they often do not fully address the simultaneous optimization of energy consumption and latency required for always-on edge applications.

System-level and hardware-aware strategies have also been developed to optimize LLM serving and inference efficiency. These approaches include memory management via PagedAttention and vLLM [8], phase-splitting inference with Splitwise [7], and constraint-aware scheduling with ExeGPT [6]. Additionally, offloading and low-resource inference engines such as FlexGen [22], PowerInfer [5], and LLM in a flash [9] leverage hybrid CPU-GPU or flash-DRAM architectures to run models that exceed available memory. While these systems improve throughput and enable inference on commodity hardware, they generally focus on maximizing throughput or minimizing memory footprint in data center or desktop settings, rather than optimizing for the tight energy budgets and real-time latency constraints characteristic of embedded edge devices.

In the domain of edge-specific optimization, prior work has focused on algorithm-hardware co-design for NLP tasks. EdgeBERT [24] represents a notable example, employing entropy-based early exit and dynamic voltage-frequency scaling (DVFS) to optimize energy for BERT-based models on a 12nm accelerator. This approach utilizes hardware components like low-dropout voltage regulators and all-digital phase-locked loops to manage power at sentence granularity. Related hardware optimization techniques include variation-aware DVFS [26], per-core DVFS using on-chip regulators [27], and latency-predictable optimization frameworks like NeuOS [28]. More recently, reviews have highlighted the need for LLM-hardware co-design and accelerator compilation [10], while general ML frameworks like PyTorch [19], TensorFlow [20], and DeepSpeed [21] provide the foundational software stack. However, existing edge solutions often target smaller, non-generative models or lack the comprehensive co-design necessary to balance the complex trade-offs of latency, energy, and accuracy for modern, large-scale generative LLMs on scalable edge accelerators.
