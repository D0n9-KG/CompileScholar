**Related Works**

Speech-driven 3D facial animation has evolved from early deep learning approaches that generalized speech-to-animation mapping [1] and captured specific speaking styles [10], to more recent architectures that address the ill-posed nature of the task. A prominent sub-family of recent methods employs autoregressive Transformer models to encode long-term audio context, such as FaceFormer, which integrates self-supervised speech representations to improve lip movement accuracy [3]. Another significant approach casts the generation problem as a discrete code query task to mitigate the regression-to-mean problem; CodeTalker utilizes a learned codebook to promote motion vividness and reduce cross-modal mapping uncertainty [4]. These methods, while improving temporal consistency and realism, often rely on deterministic mappings that may not fully capture the inherent stochasticity of human facial expressions.

To address the non-deterministic nature of facial cues and the one-to-many relationship between speech and expression, several works have adopted diffusion-based frameworks. FaceDiffuser was among the first to employ diffusion techniques for this task, using pre-trained HuBERT models to encode audio and generate non-deterministic animations [5]. Similarly, 3DiFACE proposes a lightweight audio-conditioned diffusion model that enables stochasticity and motion editing, allowing for greater fidelity and diversity in generated animations [9]. More recently, Media2Face extends this paradigm by operating in a generalized neural parametric facial asset latent space, accepting multi-modality guidance from audio, text, and images to enhance expressiveness and style adaptability [16]. Despite these advancements in diversity, these methods typically depend on pre-trained self-supervised audio encoders, which can introduce feature coupling issues for phonetically similar syllables.

The integration of self-supervised speech representation learning has become a standard practice to handle data scarcity and capture rich acoustic features. Models like wav2vec 2.0 [14] and HuBERT [15] have demonstrated the feasibility of learning powerful speech representations from unlabeled data, which are subsequently fine-tuned or used as encoders in animation pipelines. FaceXHuBERT leverages the HuBERT model to incorporate both lexical and non-lexical information, enabling the capture of personalized cues such as identity and emotion without requiring a large lexicon [6]. Similarly, FaceFormer and FaceDiffuser integrate these self-supervised representations to align audio and motion modalities effectively [3][5]. However, a critical limitation persists in these approaches: the prevalence of near-homophone syllables with distinct lip shapes leads to significant coupling in self-supervised feature spaces, resulting in an averaging effect that reduces the precision of lip shape generation.

Beyond basic lip synchronization, recent research has focused on disentangling emotional and stylistic factors from speech content to achieve more expressive and controllable animations. EMOTE proposes decoupled losses for speech and emotion, leveraging the spatial and temporal differences between lip movements and facial expressions to enable explicit emotion control [7]. EmoTalk introduces an emotion disentangling encoder to separate emotion and content in speech, driving a decoder to generate 3D faces with enhanced emotional styles [8]. In the domain of speaking styles, Mimic explores the coupled information between style and semantic content, using disentangled representations to synthesize realistic, subject-specific animations [11]. Additionally, expressive animation with controllable emotions has been pursued through emotion controller modules that adjust expression parameters based on emotion type and intensity [2]. While these methods successfully enhance expressivity and controllability, they often treat the audio encoding as a monolithic feature vector, failing to explicitly decorrelate the semantic features that cause ambiguity in phonetically similar contexts.

Finally, broader multimodal and holistic approaches have emerged to enrich the input signals. EMAGE generates full-body gestures from audio and masked gestures, utilizing compositional VQ-VAEs to enhance fidelity and diversity in holistic motion [19]. Other works have explored mining audio, text, and visual information for talking face generation [18] or cross-modal retrieval based on joint embeddings [17]. Early lip-syncing efforts, such as Wav2Lip, focused on accurate lip movement in unconstrained videos using powerful discriminators [12], while foundational end-to-end speech recognition systems like Deep Speech laid the groundwork for robust audio processing [13]. These diverse approaches highlight the complexity of the task, yet the specific challenge of semantic decorrelation in audio feature spaces to prevent averaging effects in lip generation remains an open problem addressed by our work.
