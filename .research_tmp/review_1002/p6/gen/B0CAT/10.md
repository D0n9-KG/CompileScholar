Multi-token prediction (MTP) and related pre-training objectives aim to enhance the sample efficiency and downstream capabilities of large language models by extending the standard next-token prediction (NTP) paradigm. Representative approaches in this family include ProphetNet, which predicts future n-grams for sequence-to-sequence pre-training [3], and the multi-token prediction framework that utilizes multiple independent output heads to predict the next $n$ tokens simultaneously [5]. These methods have demonstrated that training models to anticipate multiple future tokens can improve performance on generative benchmarks, such as coding tasks, and facilitate the development of induction heads and algorithmic reasoning capabilities [5]. Furthermore, research into the internal representations of transformers has shown that hidden states often contain sufficient signal to predict subsequent tokens, supporting the feasibility of such multi-step prediction objectives [4]. However, prior work indicates that while these benefits are pronounced for larger models, smaller language models (SLMs) often struggle to effectively leverage the MTP objective during pre-training, suggesting a gap in adapting these objectives for resource-constrained architectures [5].

To address the challenges of training complex objectives, curriculum learning strategies have been widely adopted in machine learning. This field encompasses a broad taxonomy of approaches that order training data from easy to hard samples, utilizing various pacing functions to introduce difficulty [1][2]. These strategies have been successfully applied across diverse tasks to improve performance over random shuffling without additional computational costs [2]. The core principle involves ranking samples and determining the optimal schedule for introducing more difficult data, which can significantly influence model convergence and final performance [2]. Despite their success in various domains, the application of curriculum learning to the specific context of multi-token prediction for language models remains underexplored. A key open problem is determining the optimal curriculum direction—whether to start with simple next-token prediction and progress to multi-token prediction, or vice versa—and how such scheduling affects the trade-off between downstream task performance and inference efficiency in smaller models [1][2].

Inference acceleration techniques, particularly self-speculative decoding, offer a complementary avenue for improving LLM efficiency by leveraging the model's own architecture to draft and verify tokens. This family of approaches includes methods that use layer-skipping to create a lightweight draft model, such as LayerSkip [7], SWIFT [10], and the Draft & Verify scheme [9]. Other variants include Kangaroo, which employs double early exiting to optimize the drafting process [8], and Medusa, which adds multiple decoding heads to predict subsequent tokens in parallel using tree-based attention [12]. Additionally, KOALA enhances speculative decoding by transforming the draft head into a multi-layer architecture with adversarial learning to improve prediction accuracy [11]. These methods aim to reduce the sequential bottleneck of autoregressive decoding by allowing parallel verification of multiple candidate tokens [6][12]. However, many of these approaches require additional training, fine-tuning, or specific architectural modifications to achieve high token acceptance rates, which can limit their plug-and-play applicability and introduce overhead that may negate speedups, particularly when the draft model's latency becomes non-negligible [8][10][12].
