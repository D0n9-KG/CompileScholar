**Related Works**

The foundational paradigm of Generative Retrieval (GR) replaces traditional index-retrieve pipelines with a single neural network that directly generates document identifiers. This approach was introduced by the Differentiable Search Index (DSI) [14], which encodes corpus information into model parameters, and further advanced by the Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI) [5] and autoregressive entity retrieval systems like GENRE [3]. Subsequent works have refined this paradigm by optimizing document identifier construction, such as learning semantic tokens via GenRet [4], using term-sets [8], or employing n-grams as identifiers [13]. Other approaches focus on bridging the gap between indexing and retrieval, including DSI-QG for query generation [7], SE-DSI for semantic enhancement [10], and ASI for end-to-end docid learning [9]. Despite these advancements, a shared limitation persists: most methods rely on static, pre-processed identifiers or fixed corpus structures, which limits their ability to handle dynamic corpora or out-of-distribution data effectively [19][20].

To address scalability and the limitations of parametric memory, researchers have explored the relationship between generative and dense retrieval, as well as hybrid indexing strategies. Theoretical analyses have shown that GR with atomic identifiers is equivalent to single-vector dense retrieval [15], while hierarchical identifiers mimic multi-vector dense retrieval [12]. To mitigate the high cost of updating parametric indexes, methods like IncDSI [23] and DSI++ [24] enable real-time document addition, while CLEVER [18] focuses on continual learning for dynamic corpora. Furthermore, hybrid approaches like Generative Dense Retrieval (GDR) [22] combine GR’s deep interaction with dense retrieval’s scalability, and Nonparametric Decoding [21] introduces external memory to overcome fixed parameter capacity. A common open problem in this family is the trade-off between the deep semantic interactions of generative models and the computational efficiency and update flexibility required for large-scale, dynamic real-world systems [16][22].

The decoding and optimization strategies for GR have been a focal point for improving retrieval accuracy and ranking. Standard autoregressive decoding often suffers from a mismatch between the generation objective and the final ranking target. To bridge this gap, LTRGR introduces a learning-to-rank framework [6], while PAG guides generation through simultaneous decoding [32]. Other works address the specific challenges of constrained decoding, such as ensuring distinct identifiers in multi-graded relevance settings via GR² [11] or using few-shot indexing to reduce training overhead [17]. Additionally, the integration of GR with downstream tasks has been explored in unified frameworks like CorpusLM [33] and UniGen [34], which aim to align retrieval with answer generation. However, these methods often rely on specific architectural modifications or additional training phases, and the inherent limitations of beam search in approximating optimal retrieval paths remain a significant theoretical and practical challenge [30].

Theoretical analyses of GR’s generalization and robustness are less developed compared to empirical studies. Recent work has begun to examine the impact of constraints and decoding algorithms on model performance. For instance, the use of concurrent verifiers [25] and tractable probabilistic models [28] has been studied in the context of constrained generation, while GUARD [27] addresses the distributional shift caused by inference-time constraints. Empirical studies have highlighted the poor out-of-distribution (OOD) robustness of GR models compared to dense retrieval [19][20]. Furthermore, the training-testing discrepancy in tree-based models under beam search has been analyzed to define Bayes optimality under such constraints [30]. A shared limitation in this area is the lack of comprehensive theoretical bounds that fully characterize the error introduced by corpus-specific constraints and beam search heuristics, leaving the generalization capabilities of GR on unseen corpora not fully understood [26][30].
