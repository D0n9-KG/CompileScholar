**Related Works**

The foundational work on Verifiable Differential Privacy (VDP) established the necessity of cryptographic proofs to ensure faithful execution of DP mechanisms, addressing the vulnerability where malicious servers might manipulate noise to mask data breaches [1]. This line of research introduced the concept of requiring publishing entities to output zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) that convince verifiers of both privacy and reliability, resolving the paradox of validating randomness without revealing it [1]. Subsequent efforts extended these principles to interactive settings, such as Interactive Proofs for Differentially Private Counting, which further explored the verification of DP guarantees in distributed environments [4]. While these approaches provide strong theoretical guarantees for central DP, they often rely on complex cryptographic primitives that can introduce significant computational overhead, limiting their scalability in high-throughput systems.

In the domain of Local Differential Privacy (LDP), researchers have focused on preventing manipulation attacks by malicious data providers who might skew estimations through output manipulation [3]. To address this, verifiable randomization mechanisms have been proposed, allowing data collectors to verify the completeness of the agreed-upon randomization process for each provider [3]. Additionally, cryptographically secure techniques for randomized response have been developed to guarantee unconditional privacy and prevent cheating respondents from affecting the tally beyond their own vote [5]. These methods ensure that the randomization is faithful, thereby mitigating the risk of plausible deniability inherent in standard LDP protocols [3][5]. However, these solutions often incur high communication costs or require specific cryptographic assumptions that may not align with the efficiency requirements of large-scale distributed deployments.

A parallel line of research has explored the use of Secure Multi-Party Computation (MPC) to achieve DP in distributed settings, aiming to combine the utility of the central model with the privacy guarantees of the local model. Systems like Crypt$\epsilon$ employ two non-colluding untrusted servers to run DP programs on encrypted data, achieving the "best of both worlds" without a trusted data collector [6]. Early work on distributed noise generation also highlighted the potential of sharing noise generation among parties to enhance privacy [7]. More recent advancements have focused on the specific challenge of securely sampling noise distributions, such as biased coins [8] and discrete Gaussian noise [9], which are critical for the accuracy of DP mechanisms. These MPC-based approaches prevent parties from obtaining others' information while maintaining utility comparable to the central model [10]. Despite these theoretical advances, practical implementations face challenges related to performance and the complexity of security proofs, as noted in the analysis of secure computation tools [6].

Recent efforts have sought to benchmark and compare the efficiency of these secure sampling protocols to address the lack of experimental evaluations in the field [10]. Studies have examined various secure sampling methods, noting that they often have different security assumptions and isolated theoretical analyses [10]. For instance, DPrio has been proposed to improve the utility of Prio-based systems, addressing efficiency concerns in differential privacy [2]. The benchmarking of these protocols reveals that while many secure sampling methods exist, there is a significant gap in comprehensive measurements of their efficiency and practical deployment [10]. This highlights an open problem in the field: the need for mechanisms that balance strong verifiability and privacy guarantees with the computational and communication efficiency required for real-world distributed applications. Our work builds upon these foundations by proposing Verifiable Distributed Differential Privacy (VDDP), which integrates verification mechanisms to address the vulnerabilities of unfaithful execution in distributed settings, offering improved efficiency in proof generation and communication costs compared to previous state-of-the-art mechanisms.
