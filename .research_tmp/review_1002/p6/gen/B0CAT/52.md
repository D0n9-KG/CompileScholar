Dense retrieval architectures have evolved significantly from sparse methods, primarily through the adoption of dual-encoder frameworks that leverage pre-trained language models. Representative approaches in this domain include BERT-based encoders such as Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR) [1], unsupervised variants like Contriever [2], and decoder-based models such as RePLAMA [5]. DPR demonstrated that dense representations learned via contrastive learning could outperform traditional sparse models like BM25 by substantial margins on open-domain question answering tasks [1]. To address the computational inefficiency of cross-encoders, Sentence-BERT introduced siamese network structures to derive efficient sentence embeddings suitable for similarity search [3]. Subsequent work, such as SimCSE, further refined these embeddings by utilizing simple contrastive learning objectives with dropout as data augmentation, regularizing the anisotropic space of pre-trained embeddings to be more uniform [4]. While these methods established dense retrieval as a viable alternative to sparse baselines, a shared limitation persists regarding the specific mechanisms by which retrieval knowledge is acquired; early works focused heavily on performance metrics rather than dissecting the interplay between pre-training and fine-tuning phases [1][3][4].

The representation of text within these dual-encoder frameworks varies significantly, influencing both efficiency and semantic fidelity. Key sub-families include the use of the [CLS] token, mean pooling of hidden states, and prompt-based representations. DPR typically relies on the [CLS] token of the BERT encoder to represent the passage or query [1]. In contrast, Contriever employs a fully unsupervised approach that often utilizes mean pooling to aggregate token representations, aiming to capture holistic semantic meaning without labeled data [2]. PromptReps introduced a different paradigm by using prompt-based representations to enhance dense retrieval, arguing that standard pooling methods may not fully exploit the capabilities of large language models [6]. These differing representation strategies reflect a broader tension in the field: while [CLS] tokens are computationally efficient, mean pooling and prompt-based methods may offer richer semantic contexts. However, the relative contribution of these representation choices to the acquisition of retrieval knowledge, distinct from the backbone architecture itself, remains under-explored in comparative studies [2][6].

The choice of backbone architecture and the datasets used for evaluation have expanded the scope of dense retrieval research. Representative backbones include encoder-only models like BERT and decoder-only models like LLaMA, while standard evaluation benchmarks include Natural Questions (NQ) and MS MARCO. Early dense retrievers, such as DPR, were predominantly built upon encoder-only BERT architectures and evaluated on datasets like NQ [1][8]. The introduction of MS MARCO provided a large-scale, human-generated dataset derived from real search queries, offering a more diverse and challenging benchmark for machine reading comprehension and retrieval tasks [7]. More recently, the shift towards decoder-only architectures, exemplified by RePLAMA, has challenged the dominance of encoder-only models by leveraging the generative capabilities of large language models for retrieval [5]. Despite this architectural diversity, most prior analyses have been confined to specific combinations of encoder types and datasets, such as BERT on NQ. Consequently, there is a lack of comprehensive studies that systematically isolate the effects of backbone architecture (encoder vs. decoder) and representation pooling strategies across multiple datasets like NQ and MS MARCO, leaving open questions about the universality of knowledge acquisition patterns in dense retrievers [1][5][7][8].
