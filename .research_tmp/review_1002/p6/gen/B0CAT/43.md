**Related Works**

The training of large language models (LLMs) has evolved from single-device execution to complex distributed systems, necessitating diverse parallelization strategies. Representative approaches in this domain include model parallelism techniques such as Megatron-LM [10] and its efficient large-scale implementation [9], as well as hybrid parallelism frameworks like DeepSpeed and Megatron-Turing NLG [11]. Furthermore, automatic parallelization systems such as GSPMD [12], Alpa [14], and Galvatron [15] aim to automate the selection of optimal parallelism dimensions. Additionally, specialized sequence parallelism methods, including Sequence Parallelism [16], DeepSpeed-Ulysses [17], and Ring Attention [18], address the memory constraints of long-context training. Despite these advancements, the vast search space of configurable strategies makes it difficult to achieve high efficiency in practice, as manual tuning is error-prone and existing automatic systems often rely on limited search spaces or specific hardware assumptions.

To support these distributed training efforts, specialized hardware architectures have been developed to handle the computational demands of modern LLMs. Key examples include the NVIDIA Blackwell architecture [1], the TPU v4 supercomputer with its optically reconfigurable interconnect [2], and the Pathways system used for training PaLM [8]. These systems provide the foundational compute and communication capabilities required for scaling models to hundreds of billions of parameters. However, the rapid evolution of these hardware platforms creates a moving target for software optimization, where performance characteristics vary significantly across different generations and configurations, complicating the generalization of optimization techniques.

Performance modeling and estimation tools are essential for guiding optimization and system-level studies without the high cost of exhaustive empirical testing. Existing methodologies include analytical models like Amped [19] and Calculon [20], which provide high-level co-design insights. Simulation-based approaches, such as Astra-Sim [23] and its successor Astra-Sim 2.0 [24], enable software/hardware co-design exploration for distributed platforms. Trace-driven tools, including Daydream [21], which uses CUPTI traces to model DNN execution, and DPro [25], a generic performance diagnosis toolkit, offer more granular insights into execution behaviors. Profiling infrastructure such as NVProf [3], CUPTI [4], and PyTorch Kineto [5] provides the low-level data necessary for these analyses. While these tools offer valuable insights, many existing profilers do not aim to answer predictive questions about new configurations, and analytical models often lack the fidelity to capture the complex interactions of modern LLM execution stacks, leaving a gap in accurately predicting performance for novel model and deployment setups.

The foundational Transformer architecture [6] and subsequent large-scale language models [7] have driven the need for these system-level innovations. As models continue to scale, the interplay between algorithmic complexity and system efficiency becomes increasingly critical. The challenge remains to develop accurate, trace-driven performance models that can effectively characterize and predict the behavior of modern LLMs across diverse hardware and software configurations, thereby facilitating efficient exploration of the vast space of training strategies.
