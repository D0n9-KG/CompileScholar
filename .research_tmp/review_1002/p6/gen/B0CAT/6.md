**Related Works**

Summarizing long-form narratives presents distinct challenges compared to standard document summarization, primarily due to the need to capture intricate plotlines and character dynamics. Early approaches focused on specific narrative structures, such as novel chapters [1] and movie scripts [2]. More recent work has emphasized the selection of salient content, such as identifying key scenes in movie scripts to improve summary accuracy [3]. However, these methods often rely on specific structural cues or extractive techniques that may not generalize well to the diverse and unstructured nature of long-form fiction, where thematic coherence and character interaction are paramount.

A significant body of research has addressed the computational limitations of Transformer architectures, which traditionally scale quadratically with sequence length. To handle longer inputs, several efficient attention mechanisms have been proposed. BigBird utilizes sparse attention to reduce complexity to linear [4], while Longformer combines local windowed attention with global attention to process documents of thousands of tokens [5]. Other approaches include Reformer, which uses locality-sensitive hashing to achieve $O(L\log L)$ complexity [6], and Linformer, which approximates self-attention via low-rank matrices for linear complexity [8]. Additionally, LongT5 offers an efficient text-to-text transformer specifically designed for long sequences [7]. While these models extend the context window, they often require specialized pretraining or fine-tuning, and their performance can still degrade when relevant information is distributed across very long contexts [10].

Alternative strategies for handling long texts involve extending the context window of existing Large Language Models (LLMs) or processing inputs in chunks. Position Interpolation (PI) extends the context window of RoPE-based models like LLaMA with minimal fine-tuning [9]. Another approach, SLED, partitions input into overlapping chunks and fuses information in the decoder, leveraging short-text pretrained LMs [11]. Unlimiformer offloads cross-attention to a k-nearest-neighbor index to handle unlimited input lengths [12], while CachED enables end-to-end training on entire documents using gradient caching [13]. Despite these advancements, these methods often struggle with the "lost in the middle" phenomenon, where models fail to robustly utilize information located in the center of long contexts [10], and may still face challenges in maintaining factual consistency across the entire narrative.

Recent work has shifted towards multi-agent frameworks and specialized evaluation metrics for narrative summarization. Chain-of-Agents (CoA) employs multiple worker agents to sequentially process segmented portions of text, followed by a manager agent to synthesize the final output, mitigating focus issues in long contexts [14]. In the domain of book-length summarization, BooookScore provides a systematic exploration of LLM-based summarizers, identifying common coherence errors in hierarchical and incremental summarization workflows [15]. Furthermore, Agent-as-a-Judge frameworks, such as NarrativeFactScore, utilize Character Knowledge Graphs to evaluate and refine summaries for factual accuracy, addressing the limitations of traditional metrics like ROUGE and BERTScore in capturing character relationships [16]. While these multi-agent and evaluation-focused approaches offer promising directions, they often require complex orchestration of LLMs and specialized knowledge graphs, which can be computationally intensive and difficult to scale across diverse storytelling domains without fine-tuning.
