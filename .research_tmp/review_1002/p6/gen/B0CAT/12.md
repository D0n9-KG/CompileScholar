Event camera data reduction techniques can be broadly categorized into bio-inspired filtering strategies, spiking neural network (SNN) based downscaling, and heuristic or threshold-based subsampling methods. Early approaches to managing the high bandwidth and computational demands of event streams often drew inspiration from biological visual systems. For instance, bio-inspired filtering algorithms have been proposed to reduce data volume while preserving essential information [3]. Similarly, insect-inspired spatio-temporal downsampling techniques have been developed to mimic the efficient processing capabilities of biological eyes, aiming to compress event data without significant loss of structural integrity [5]. These bio-inspired methods typically rely on fixed physiological models or predefined thresholds to determine which events to retain. While effective in specific scenarios, these approaches often lack adaptability to diverse scene dynamics, as their filtering criteria are static and not learned from the data distribution.

In parallel, the integration of spiking neural networks (SNNs) with event cameras has led to the development of specialized downsampling methods that align with the temporal nature of spiking computation. Research has compared the performance of various DVS data spatial downscaling methods when used in conjunction with SNNs, highlighting the interplay between data reduction and neural architecture [4]. Furthermore, neuromorphic downsampling techniques have been introduced to process event camera output in a manner consistent with neuromorphic hardware constraints, ensuring that the reduced data remains compatible with low-power, asynchronous processing units [6]. These SNN-centric approaches often optimize for energy efficiency and hardware compatibility rather than general-purpose task performance. A shared limitation in this family of works is that the downsampling strategy is frequently co-designed with a specific neural architecture, limiting the transferability of the reduced event stream to other model types or tasks.

More recent work has focused on heuristic and threshold-based subsampling strategies, particularly in the context of convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for video classification. Spatial and temporal downsampling has been explored to reduce the density of events for visual classification tasks, often using simple metrics such as event density or time intervals [1]. Other robust methods for downsampling event camera data have been proposed to handle noise and redundancy, aiming to create a more compact representation of the scene [2]. Recent studies have pushed the boundaries of event subsampling in event-based video classification, demonstrating that the number of events per video can be reduced by an order of magnitude with minimal accuracy drop [7]. However, these studies also reveal that at high subsampling rates, the performance degradation is not solely due to information loss but is exacerbated by training instability and increased sensitivity to hyperparameters [7]. Consequently, most existing heuristic or threshold-based strategies rely on fixed rules that do not adapt to the specific importance of events in a given scene, leading to suboptimal performance when the event budget is strictly constrained.

Despite these advances, a critical gap remains in the ability to perform intelligent, adaptive sampling that preserves task-relevant information without requiring task-specific model adaptation. Most prior methods, whether bio-inspired, SNN-based, or heuristic, operate on fixed criteria that do not account for the varying importance of events across different scenes or tasks. This lack of adaptability limits their effectiveness in zero-shot scenarios, where downsampled events must remain usable for models trained on the original, high-density event stream. The challenge of defining a flexible, probabilistic framework that can estimate event importance on-the-fly and maintain structural integrity for downstream tasks remains an open problem in the field.
