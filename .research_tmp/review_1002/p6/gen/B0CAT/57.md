**Related Works**

Generative retrieval (GR) has evolved from early autoregressive entity retrieval systems to comprehensive model-based indexing frameworks. Early approaches, such as GENRE, introduced the concept of retrieving entities by generating their unique names token-by-token, mitigating the memory footprint issues of dense vector storage [1]. This paradigm was extended to document retrieval through the Differentiable Search Index (DSI), which encodes corpus information into Transformer parameters to map queries directly to document identifiers (docids) [2]. Subsequent works refined this architecture to improve recall and scalability; for instance, the Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI) introduced a prefix-aware decoder and semantic docids to enhance end-to-end optimization [4], while Ultron devised richer semantic docids and a three-stage training workflow to capture deeper corpus knowledge [5]. DynamicRetriever further explored pre-training models to embed token-level and document-level information, eliminating the need for sparse or dense indices [3]. More recent advancements have focused on scaling and optimizing these models for large-scale benchmarks; RIPOR proposed an optimization framework to address the sequential decoding challenges in large collections [6], and GR$^2$ extended GR to handle multi-graded relevance by ensuring distinct and relevant identifiers [10]. Despite these architectural improvements, most existing GR models are developed and evaluated on static document collections, leaving their performance in dynamic environments largely unexplored.

The design of document identifiers (docids) is a critical determinant of GR performance, with approaches ranging from numeric codes to text-based representations. Early methods often relied on hierarchical structures or fixed rule-based identifiers, such as document titles or substrings, which may fail to capture complete semantic information [12]. To address this, GenRet proposed learning to tokenize documents into short discrete representations via a discrete auto-encoding approach, allowing for more flexible and semantic docids [7]. Similarly, Multiview Identifiers Enhanced Generative Retrieval (MIEGR) argued that single-view identifiers are insufficient and proposed using synthetic identifiers alongside titles and substrings to facilitate holistic ranking from multiple perspectives [11]. Other works, such as GERE, utilized document titles and evidence sentence identifiers for generative evidence retrieval in fact verification tasks [8], while CorpusBrain pre-trained a generative retrieval model using specific tasks to support knowledge-intensive language tasks [9]. While text-based and learned docids offer superior semantic alignment, numeric-based docids, though efficient, often suffer from a lack of semantic richness. A shared limitation across these docid design strategies is the difficulty in balancing efficiency with the ability to generalize to unseen or new documents, particularly when the identifier space is fixed or overly constrained.

Recent research has begun to address the challenge of dynamic corpora, where document collections evolve continuously. DSI++ explored updating Transformer memory with new documents, marking an early step toward incremental indexing [13]. CLEVER specifically tackled continual learning for GR by introducing Incremental Product Quantization to encode new documents with low computational cost and a memory-augmented learning mechanism to prevent catastrophic forgetting [15]. CorpusBrain++ proposed a continual generative pre-training framework to adapt models to evolving knowledge bases [14]. Furthermore, recent evaluations have highlighted the practicality of GR in dynamic scenarios; studies on the StreamingQA benchmark demonstrated that GR models are more adaptable to evolving knowledge and more efficient in terms of inference FLOPs, indexing time, and storage footprint compared to dual encoder models [16]. However, these works often focus on specific mechanisms for updating or evaluating GR, and there remains a lack of systematic comparison between different docid types (text-based vs. numeric-based) in dynamic settings. Consequently, the specific factors contributing to the generalization gap between text-based and numeric-based docids in evolving corpora remain an open problem, motivating our systematic evaluation and the proposal of a multi-docid design that leverages the strengths of both paradigms.
