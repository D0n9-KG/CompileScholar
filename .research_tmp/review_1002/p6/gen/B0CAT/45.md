**Related Works**

This section reviews prior work in two primary thematic areas: the analysis of app reviews for sentiment and emotion, and the utilization of Large Language Models (LLMs) for automated annotation tasks.

**App Review Analysis and Emotion Mining**
Research on app reviews has traditionally focused on sentiment polarity and usability analytics to guide software evolution. Representative approaches in this domain include general-purpose analytics tools and specific empirical studies on user experience. For instance, Appsent was developed as a mobile analytics tool to facilitate the analysis of app reviews, addressing the labor-intensive nature of manual review reading by providing user-friendly interfaces and meaningful analytics [2]. Similarly, empirical analyses have examined specific usage contexts, such as mobile learning apps, to understand user experience dynamics [3]. More recently, efforts have expanded beyond simple polarity to include multilabel and multiclass sentiment and emotion classification. A notable contribution in this area is the creation of a multilabel multiclass sentiment and emotion dataset derived from Indonesian mobile application reviews, which aims to capture the complexity of user feedback more granularly than binary sentiment labels [1]. However, a shared limitation across these approaches is the underexploration of fine-grained emotion classification specifically within the context of app reviews. While tools like Appsent provide high-level analytics [2] and datasets like the Indonesian review corpus offer multilabel structures [1], there remains a gap in structured annotation frameworks that explicitly address the challenges of fine-grained emotion interpretation, such as those defined by Plutchik’s taxonomy, in this specific domain.

**LLM-Assisted Annotation and Human-LLM Collaboration**
The second theme encompasses the application of Large Language Models (LLMs) to automate or assist in text annotation tasks, ranging from political and financial texts to pragmatic linguistic features. This family of approaches includes studies evaluating LLMs as substitutes for human experts, semi-automated crafting of outputs, and collaborative annotation systems. For example, research has assessed LLMs as substitutes for human experts in annotating political text [4] and as financial data annotators, studying their effectiveness and efficiency [6]. In the domain of linguistics, studies have explored the potential of LLM-assisted annotation for corpus-based pragmatics and discourse analysis, specifically for identifying apology components, finding that models like GPT-4 approach human coder accuracy [7]. Furthermore, semi-automated approaches using models like Gemini Pro have been applied to annotating materials science text [5]. To address the limitations of fully automated systems, recent work has proposed human-LLM collaborative annotation systems, such as MEGAanno+ [8], and methods for effective verification of LLM labels to ensure quality [9]. Despite these advancements, a common open problem persists regarding the feasibility of full automation. While LLMs significantly reduce manual effort and show substantial agreement with human annotators in domains like pragmatics [7] and finance [6], the complexity of emotional and pragmatic interpretation often requires human oversight. Consequently, full automation remains challenging, and the most effective current paradigm involves hybrid models where LLMs assist rather than replace human judgment, particularly when dealing with nuanced or domain-specific annotation guidelines.
