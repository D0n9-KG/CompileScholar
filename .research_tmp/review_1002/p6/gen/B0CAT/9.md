**Related Works**

The synthesis of medical imaging data to address class imbalance and data scarcity is a critical area of research, encompassing generalizable neuroimaging synthesis, encoder-decoder lesion generation, and GAN-based oversampling techniques. Early approaches utilized fully convolutional networks to generate synthetic multiple sclerosis lesions by encoding lesion information as binary masks, aiming to augment training sets for supervised detection algorithms [7]. More recently, the use of Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) has become prevalent, with frameworks like DeepSMOTE fusing deep learning with SMOTE to generate high-quality artificial images that enhance minority classes without the mode collapse issues often associated with standard GANs [6]. SinGAN-Seg has also been employed for synthetic training data generation specifically for medical image segmentation tasks [4]. While these methods effectively increase the volume of minority class samples, they often rely on complex training procedures or specific architectural constraints that may not fully capture the nuanced distribution of rare pathological features like paramagnetic rim lesions.

Transfer learning and domain adaptation strategies are frequently employed to improve model generalization across different imaging domains and to leverage knowledge from related tasks. Systematic reviews indicate that transfer learning is vital in MRI for addressing image variations and re-utilizing models trained on related tasks, with applications ranging from dementia classification to tumor segmentation [3]. Theoretical analyses have highlighted the importance of carefully selecting which information to transfer, noting that the degree of probabilistic change in the algorithm bounds the potential improvement [2]. Furthermore, synthetic data generation is increasingly viewed through the lens of generalizability, where the goal is to create data that helps models learn robust features applicable to unseen domains [1]. Despite these advances, a significant open problem remains in ensuring that transferred knowledge or synthetic data does not introduce domain-specific biases that degrade performance on the target clinical task, particularly when the source and target distributions differ significantly.

The robustness of generative models to noisy labels and the inversion of GANs represent another thematic cluster, focusing on handling imperfect data and leveraging latent space properties. Standard conditional GANs can suffer from poor sample quality when trained on noisy labels, leading to the development of Robust Conditional GANs (RCGAN) which corrupt labels during training to force the generator to produce clean samples [11]. Additionally, GAN inversion techniques allow for the mapping of real images back into the latent space of a pretrained GAN, enabling applications in image restoration and manipulation [9]. This capability is closely related to the use of conditional GANs, where data is fed to both generator and discriminator to control the output distribution [10]. Recent work has also explored correcting noisy multilabel predictions by modeling label noise as latent space shifts, providing a post-correction mechanism that can be applied to pre-trained models [12]. Similarly, Noisy Prediction Calibration (NPC) uses generative models to estimate transition matrices and correct noisy predictions from pre-trained classifiers without requiring retraining [13]. A common limitation in this family of approaches is the computational cost of inversion or the complexity of modeling the noise distribution, which can hinder real-time clinical deployment or the integration of these methods into existing diagnostic pipelines.
