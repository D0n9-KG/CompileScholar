Open-vocabulary object detection (OVD) has emerged as a critical paradigm to overcome the closed-set limitations of traditional detectors, which are constrained by the fixed categories of annotated datasets [1]. The field has evolved through several methodological families, including visual-semantic space mapping, novel visual feature synthesis, region-aware training, pseudo-labeling, knowledge distillation, and transfer learning [1]. A foundational approach in this domain relies on contrastive language-image pretraining, such as CLIP, which learns robust image representations from large-scale image-text pairs to enable zero-shot transfer to downstream tasks [2]. However, directly applying these image-level models to object detection often results in poor performance due to a domain shift, as they lack fine-grained alignment between specific image regions and text spans [7]. To address this, region-based methods like RegionCLIP extend pretraining to align image regions with template captions, significantly improving detection accuracy for novel categories [7]. Furthermore, approaches like CoDet reformulate region-word alignment as a co-occurring object discovery problem, leveraging visual similarities to align objects with shared concepts in captions, thereby reducing reliance on pre-aligned vision-language spaces [8]. Despite these advancements, existing OVD detectors often exhibit significant variability when handling vocabularies of different semantic granularities, posing challenges for real-world deployment where user-defined classes may vary in specificity [9].

The expansion of detection vocabularies is further supported by large-scale datasets and training strategies that bridge the gap between classification and detection. Datasets like COCO and LVIS have been pivotal in advancing scene understanding, with LVIS specifically introducing a long-tail distribution of over 1,000 categories to challenge detectors in low-sample regimes [3][4]. To leverage the broader vocabulary of image classifiers, methods like Detic train detector classifiers on image-level classification data, expanding the detector’s vocabulary to tens of thousands of concepts without complex assignment schemes [6]. This approach demonstrates that detectors can achieve strong performance even for classes lacking box annotations, effectively closing the performance gap for rare categories [6]. Additionally, semantic hierarchy-based methods, such as SHiNe, utilize class hierarchies to generate robust classifier vectors by integrating super- and sub-categories into hierarchy-aware sentences [9]. While these methods enhance robustness across diverse vocabulary granularities, they often require offline processing or specific hierarchical structures, which may not always be available or optimal for arbitrary user-defined vocabularies at inference time [9].

A persistent challenge in open-vocabulary detection is the sensitivity of detectors to the quality and scope of the user-provided vocabulary. While models allow users to specify classes in natural language, overly broad or mis-specified vocabularies can hamper overall detection performance [9]. Current state-of-the-art detectors, including those based on CLIP [2], RegionCLIP [7], CoDet [8], and Detic [6], generally assume that the provided vocabulary is well-suited to the image content. However, in practical scenarios, users may include irrelevant classes or fail to specify the precise terms present in the scene. Existing surveys highlight that the permission and usage of weak supervision signals discriminate various methodologies, yet few address the dynamic refinement of the vocabulary itself during inference [1]. Most prior work focuses on improving the alignment between visual features and text embeddings or expanding the pre-trained vocabulary size [6][7][8]. Consequently, there remains an open problem regarding the automatic tailoring of user-defined vocabularies to the specific content of a given image without requiring additional training or complex re-parameterization of the detector [9]. This gap motivates the development of plug-and-play mechanisms that can refine vocabulary at inference time, ensuring that only relevant classes are considered for detection, thereby enhancing the versatility and reliability of open-vocabulary systems [1].
