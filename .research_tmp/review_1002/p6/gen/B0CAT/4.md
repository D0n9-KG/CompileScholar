**Related Works**

Research into the mechanisms of In-Context Learning (ICL) has evolved from understanding the fundamental role of demonstrations to addressing specific biases and noise robustness. A foundational line of inquiry examines the necessity and impact of ground-truth labels in demonstrations. Early studies challenged the assumption that correct input-label mappings are critical, finding that random labels often yield comparable performance to ground-truth ones, suggesting that ICL relies more on the label space, input distribution, and format than on strict correctness [5]. Subsequent work quantified this impact, introducing metrics like Label-Correctness Sensitivity and the Ground-truth Label Effect Ratio to show that the importance of correct labels varies based on prompt verbosity and model size [1]. However, these analyses primarily focus on classification tasks and leave open the question of how these dynamics apply to more complex, open-ended text generation where label correctness is inherently more nuanced.

A second major theme addresses the inherent biases in LLMs that affect ICL performance, specifically label biases and the imbalance between parametric and contextual knowledge. Researchers have identified three types of label biases—vanilla, context, and domain—and proposed calibration methods using random in-domain words to mitigate them, noting that prior calibration techniques often fail to address domain-specific restrictions [10]. Similarly, early calibration approaches estimated model bias towards certain answers using content-free inputs to stabilize few-shot performance, significantly reducing variance across prompt choices [11]. More recently, the tension between prior parametric knowledge and contextual input has been addressed through contrastive decoding techniques. These methods optimize a contrastive objective to prevent models from relying excessively on encoded priors, thereby enhancing context grounding and reducing factual inconsistencies [9]. While these bias-mitigation strategies improve stability, they generally assume clean demonstrations and do not explicitly account for noisy annotations in the training data.

The most relevant recent work focuses on the robustness of ICL to noisy annotations, particularly in text generation. It has been shown that while ICL may be robust to noise in classification, noisy annotations significantly degrade performance in text generation tasks [7]. To address this, Local Perplexity Ranking (LPR) was proposed, which decomposes perplexity into inherent and matching components and ranks candidates among semantic neighbors to filter out mismatched pairs [7]. This approach assumes that noisy samples yield higher local perplexities than clean ones. Other methods have framed the problem as learning with noisy labels, proposing safeguards against performance degradation by selecting more useful demonstrations despite label corruption [6]. Additionally, demonstration retrieval has been advanced by unified models that cast task signals into list-wise ranking formulations to select high-quality examples [3], and by in-context vectors that steer latent states to improve effectiveness and controllability [4]. Despite these advances, existing perplexity-based methods like LPR suffer from two key biases: the annotation itself and the domain-specific knowledge inherent in LLMs. When the noise ratio is high, the assumption that noisy samples have higher perplexity breaks down, leading to biased cleanliness estimates. Furthermore, contrastive decoding methods, while effective for reasoning and context grounding [2][8], are typically applied at the decoding stage rather than for pre-filtering noisy demonstration corpora. Our work addresses these gaps by introducing a dual debiasing framework that corrects perplexity estimates using synthesized neighbors, yielding a robust Sample Cleanliness Score that remains effective even under extreme noise ratios.
