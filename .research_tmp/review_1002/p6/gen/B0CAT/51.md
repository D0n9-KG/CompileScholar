**Related Works**

Federated Online Learning to Rank (FOLTR) has emerged as a privacy-preserving paradigm for search systems, evolving from centralized Online Learning to Rank (OLTR) methods. Early OLTR approaches, such as Pairwise Differentiable Gradient Descent (PDGD), addressed the limitations of linear models by enabling the optimization of non-linear neural networks through unbiased gradient estimation [4]. Subsequent work extended these capabilities to handle the explore-exploit trade-off in neural ranking models using implicit feedback [7]. To address user privacy, FOLTR frameworks were developed to train rankers collaboratively without sharing raw data. Foundational studies introduced evolution strategies for federated ranking [1][2] and proposed effective, privacy-preserving implementations [3]. More recent investigations have examined the robustness of these systems, specifically analyzing the impact of non-IID data distributions on performance [5] and evaluating defenses against untargeted poisoning attacks that compromise search effectiveness [6]. While these methods establish the efficacy of federated ranking, they primarily focus on model training and security, leaving the specific challenge of data removal under privacy regulations largely unaddressed.

Machine unlearning, driven by legislation such as the "right to be forgotten," aims to erase specific data contributions from trained models. In centralized settings, this is distinct from federated contexts where data remains local. Federated unlearning methods have been proposed to handle this distributed nature. One approach utilizes rapid retraining, allowing data holders to jointly conduct unlearning while keeping data local, with formal convergence analysis demonstrating preserved utility [10]. Another strategy involves local unlearning at the target client followed by a few rounds of federated learning to update the global model, achieving performance comparable to retraining from scratch with higher efficiency [11]. Alternative methods include subtracting accumulated historical updates and using knowledge distillation to restore model performance without client participation [9]. Additionally, specialized frameworks have been developed for specific domains, such as federated recommendation systems using log-based rollback mechanisms [12] and heterogeneous knowledge graph embeddings employing cognitive neuroscience-inspired techniques like retroactive interference [13]. General tools like Federaser also enable efficient client-level data removal [8]. Despite these advancements, most existing methods rely on single evaluation metrics or assume specific network architectures, limiting their generalizability and verifiability in complex ranking scenarios.

Recent research has begun to apply unlearning specifically to FOLTR systems. A key study investigates how to remove client contributions in FOLTR without compromising ranker effectiveness or requiring full retraining, highlighting the challenge of measuring unlearning success [14]. This work aligns with the broader trend of adapting unlearning for online, implicit feedback-driven systems. However, prior proposals in this niche, as well as general federated unlearning methods, often lack a systematic analysis of both under-unlearning and over-unlearning scenarios. They frequently rely on single evaluation metrics, which can be controversial and insufficient for a comprehensive assessment of privacy guarantees. Our work addresses this gap by rigorously assessing five unlearning strategies using adapted and newly proposed metrics, providing a verifiable analysis of their effectiveness and efficiency in balancing data privacy and system performance within FOLTR.
