### Related Works

**Optimal Taxation Theory and Policy Evaluation**
The foundational literature on income taxation encompasses classical theoretical frameworks, empirical elasticity-based derivations, and specific policy evaluations. Early theoretical contributions established the principles of optimal income taxation, including the exploration of optimum income tax structures [4] and the integration of public production efficiency into tax design [5]. Subsequent research refined these models by incorporating labor supply elasticities to derive optimal rates [6] and by analyzing the taxation of top labor incomes through the lens of three key elasticities [7]. More recent methodological advances have introduced sufficient statistics approaches to handle unemployment and wage responses in optimal income taxation [8]. On the policy evaluation side, studies have examined the distributional impacts of specific mechanisms, such as the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) [1][2], and have provided broader arguments for progressive tax systems based on basic research [3]. Despite their analytical rigor, these traditional models often rely on homogeneous agent assumptions or static equilibrium conditions, limiting their ability to capture the dynamic, heterogeneous, and sometimes irrational behaviors of real-world taxpayers that complicate the equity-efficiency trade-off.

**Agent-Based Modeling and AI-Driven Economic Simulation**
The application of computational methods to economic policy design includes agent-based modeling (ABM) and deep reinforcement learning (DRL) approaches. ABM has been extensively utilized in economics and finance to simulate complex systems, offering a framework to model heterogeneous agents and emergent macroeconomic phenomena [11]. In the specific domain of taxation, the AI Economist framework introduced a two-level deep reinforcement learning approach to discover dynamic tax policies in simulated economies [9]. This data-driven method learns from observational data without relying on explicit economic modeling assumptions, demonstrating that AI-driven policies can improve the equality-productivity trade-off by 16% over baselines, including the Saez tax framework [9]. Furthermore, the broader impact of machine learning on economics has been recognized as a transformative force for policy analysis [10]. However, existing AI-driven and ABM approaches typically rely on numerical reward functions or predefined behavioral rules, which may not fully capture the nuanced, language-based reasoning and adaptive decision-making processes characteristic of human taxpayers and policymakers.

**Large Language Models in Financial and Economic Applications**
Recent advancements in artificial intelligence have led to the integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into financial and economic domains. Surveys of LLMs for financial applications highlight their progress in processing complex textual data, forecasting, and decision support, while also identifying challenges related to reliability and domain-specific adaptation [12]. While LLMs have shown promise in various financial tasks, their application to macroeconomic policy design, particularly in the context of dynamic tax policy optimization, remains underexplored. Current literature primarily focuses on the technical capabilities of LLMs in financial analysis rather than their utility as autonomous agents within economic simulations. This gap suggests that the potential of LLMs to serve as adaptive, reasoning-based agents that can iteratively optimize tax policies by balancing equity and productivity in a simulated macroeconomic environment has not yet been fully realized or benchmarked against traditional optimal taxation models.
