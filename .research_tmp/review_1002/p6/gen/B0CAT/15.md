Brain tumor segmentation has evolved significantly since the introduction of the U-Net architecture, which established the encoder-decoder paradigm for biomedical image segmentation [1]. The field is heavily benchmarked by the BraTS challenge, which provides multi-parametric MRI data for evaluating algorithms on glioma sub-region delineation [2]. Early approaches assessed various machine learning methods for segmentation and survival prediction, highlighting the heterogeneity of gliomas [3]. As deep learning advanced, specific architectures emerged to address 3D volumetric data. The autoencoder-regularized semantic segmentation network won the BraTS 2018 challenge by addressing limited training data through reconstruction constraints [5]. Subsequently, the two-stage cascaded U-Net achieved first place in the 2019 challenge by refining the segmentation process [6]. More recently, transformer-based models have been introduced to capture long-range dependencies that convolutions may miss. TransBTS integrates 3D CNNs with transformers for global feature modeling [11], while Swin UNETR utilizes Swin transformers to overcome the limited kernel size of standard FCNNs [12]. Despite these architectural innovations, many methods still rely on extensive training from scratch or complex ensembles to achieve state-of-the-art performance.

The nnU-Net framework has become a dominant baseline in recent years due to its self-configuring nature and robust performance across various biomedical tasks [10]. In the context of brain tumors, nnU-Net was adapted for the BraTS 2020 challenge, where modifications such as region-based training and aggressive data augmentation led to first-place results [7]. Further extensions for the 2021 challenge explored larger networks, group normalization, and axial attention mechanisms, demonstrating that minor architectural tweaks to the nnU-Net baseline can yield competitive results [8]. The BraTS 2022 challenge continued this trend, with winning solutions often relying on ensembles of multiple deep learning frameworks, including nnU-Net, DeepSeg, and DeepSCAN, to maximize accuracy on diverse datasets [9]. While these methods achieve high Dice scores, they typically require significant computational resources and training time. Furthermore, the reliance on cross-validation ensembles, as seen in [7][8][9], suggests that single-model efficiency remains a challenge, and the full potential of pre-trained weights is often underutilized in these standard pipelines.

Transfer learning offers a pathway to mitigate the data scarcity and computational costs associated with 3D medical imaging. Traditional approaches often transfer 2D ImageNet weights to 3D tasks, though this can lead to a loss of volumetric information [14]. To address this, Med3D proposed a heterogeneous 3D network co-trained on a diverse 3DSeg-8 dataset to extract general 3D features, accelerating convergence for tasks like lung and liver segmentation [13]. Models Genesis further advanced this field by creating generic autodidactic models via self-supervision, demonstrating that 3D pre-trained models can outperform both learning from scratch and 2D transfer learning in segmentation and classification tasks [14]. However, the specific application of these 3D pre-trained strategies to the nuanced task of brain tumor segmentation, particularly in integrating axial-coronal-sagittal convolutions with 2D pre-trained weights, remains an area with limited exploration. Existing methods often fail to fully leverage the efficiency gains of pre-training in the specific context of glioma sub-region segmentation, leaving room for improved efficiency and performance in fast training settings.
