**Related Works**

The acceleration of Vision Transformers (ViTs) has been approached through two primary technical avenues: algorithmic model compression and hardware-specific architectural optimizations. Within the domain of model compression, existing literature explores learned quantization, hardware-aware automated quantization, and specialized low-bit quantization for attention-based models. Learned quantization methods, such as LQ-Nets, utilize differentiable quantization functions to optimize network weights for compactness and accuracy [1]. Concurrently, hardware-aware frameworks like HAQ employ reinforcement learning to determine optimal mixed-precision bitwidths for each layer, directly incorporating hardware feedback such as latency and energy into the quantization policy [4]. For attention-based architectures specifically, GOBO introduces a quantization technique that compresses the vast majority of parameters in BERT-like models to 3 bits without requiring fine-tuning, demonstrating plug-in compatibility with various accelerator architectures [5]. While these methods effectively reduce model size and computational load, they generally focus on linear layers or standard matrix multiplications. A shared limitation across these approaches is that they often leave complex, non-linear arithmetic operations—such as Softmax and LayerNorm—unoptimized or mapped to general-purpose processors, resulting in communication overheads that hinder end-to-end efficiency on specialized hardware [5][6].

In the context of ViT-specific hardware acceleration, recent works have focused on token pruning and fixed-point approximations to mitigate the high computational and memory costs of the transformer architecture. HeatViT proposes a hardware-efficient adaptive token pruning framework designed for embedded FPGAs, which dynamically identifies and consolidates non-informative tokens to reduce workload [6]. To further enhance hardware efficiency, HeatViT employs 8-bit fixed-point quantization and utilizes polynomial approximations for non-linear functions, aiming to minimize the impact of quantization error on accuracy [6]. However, this approach relies on fixed-point arithmetic and polynomial approximations, which may not fully capture the dynamic range required for sensitive operations like Softmax and LayerNorm without significant accuracy degradation or complex hardware logic. Consequently, while token pruning reduces the number of computations, it does not fundamentally resolve the challenge of efficiently mapping the full spectrum of ViT operations, particularly those sensitive to precision, onto low-precision hardware datapaths [6].

The development of new data formats represents a critical area for overcoming the precision limitations of traditional fixed-point and floating-point representations. The Block Data Representations (BDR) framework has been introduced to explore a wide spectrum of narrow-precision formats, identifying Microscaling (MX) formats that utilize shared microexponents for ultra-fine scaling factors [3]. These MX formats have been shown to outperform state-of-the-art quantization approaches, including narrow-precision floating-point and block floating-point, in large-scale generative pretraining and inference tasks [3]. By leveraging multiple levels of quantization scaling, MX formats offer a superior trade-off between accuracy and hardware efficiency compared to conventional formats. However, prior to the current work, there has been a lack of dedicated hardware implementations that fully exploit these new arithmetic formats for the specific, complex operations inherent in Vision Transformers. Existing hardware accelerators often restrict the use of such advanced formats to matrix multiplication units, leaving the non-linear components to be processed on the CPU or using less efficient approximations, thereby failing to achieve the potential speedups and area efficiencies that new data formats like MXInt can provide [3].
