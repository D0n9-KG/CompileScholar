**Related Works**

Fairness in recommender systems has evolved from isolated bias mitigation to a comprehensive multi-stakeholder framework. Early surveys established the foundational definitions and fragmented nature of fairness in recommendation [1][2]. Subsequently, the field recognized that recommendation is inherently a multi-stakeholder problem, requiring the balancing of interests among users, items, and platforms [6][7]. This perspective shifted focus from single-sided optimization to two-sided markets, where fairness must be guaranteed to both customers and producers simultaneously [5][14]. Recent works have formalized this through axiomatic approaches rooted in fair division, such as envy-freeness and dominance over uniform ranking, to quantify impact beyond mere exposure [17]. Despite these theoretical advances, a shared limitation persists: many approaches treat user and item fairness concerns separately or lack a unified mechanism to precisely control the trade-off between the two, often resulting in suboptimal balances that fail to account for the dynamic interplay between stakeholder utilities [12][13].

To address the trade-off between ranking accuracy and item fairness, various algorithmic strategies have been proposed, ranging from policy learning to economic-inspired redistribution. One family of approaches utilizes policy learning to optimize utility metrics while satisfying exposure constraints, expanding the class of learnable ranking functions to stochastic policies [9]. Another line of work draws from inequality measurement in economics, employing generalized Gini welfare functions to minimize the Gini index of item exposure, thereby promoting equality among items [11]. More recently, the concept of fair re-ranking has been reframed through the lens of taxation, where redistribution of ranking slots is conceptualized as a tax on items [8]. Specific implementations include provider max-min fairness re-ranking [4], vertical allocation-based fair exposure amortizing to balance relevance and fairness [10], and personalized consumer and producer fairness re-ranking that integrates both sides into a joint objective [12]. Additionally, models like TFROM have been designed to theoretically analyze and maintain higher performance levels while ensuring two-sided fairness [13]. However, these methods often suffer from a lack of controllability; for instance, item-level tax policies may fail to ensure continuity or precise estimation of accuracy loss under specific fairness constraints, making it difficult to predict the exact cost of fairness interventions [8][10].

Evaluation frameworks for fair re-ranking have traditionally relied on static metrics, which hinder comprehensive assessment. Standardized toolkits like FairDiverse have emerged to provide consistent benchmarks for fairness and diversity algorithms across different information retrieval tasks [3]. These frameworks allow for the comparison of pre-processing, in-processing, and post-processing techniques [3]. However, existing evaluations often rely solely on a single fairness metric or fixed operating points, which obscures the underlying mechanisms of the accuracy-fairness trade-off [8]. This limitation prevents researchers from understanding how different elasticity levels between item groups influence the transfer of fairness costs from items to users. Consequently, there is a gap in the literature for evaluation frameworks that can dynamically analyze algorithm performance across varying elasticity levels, facilitating the selection of the most suitable approach for specific market conditions [8]. Our work addresses this by introducing the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) and the ElasticRank algorithm, which leverage elasticity calculations to adjust inter-item distances, thereby providing a more nuanced and controllable approach to fair re-ranking.
