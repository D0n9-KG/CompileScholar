Controllable text-to-speech (TTS) synthesis has evolved significantly, with recent research focusing on manipulating prosodic features such as speaking rate, style, and pitch. Early approaches to expressive synthesis relied on unit-selection methods [1] and later transitioned to neural architectures that introduced explicit control mechanisms. A prominent line of work involves non-autoregressive models, such as FastSpeech [2] and its successor FastSpeech 2 [3], which address the slow inference and lack of robustness in autoregressive systems by generating mel-spectrograms in parallel. These models often incorporate duration predictors to manage speaking rate and prosody, though FastSpeech 2 specifically improves upon its predecessor by directly training on ground-truth targets and introducing explicit variation information like pitch and energy [3]. More recent methods, such as the speaking rate attention-based duration predictor [5], condition the duration module to achieve implicit speaking rate control, demonstrating that fine-tuning pre-trained models can yield better subjective scores and lower speaker rate errors compared to baselines. Despite these advancements, these approaches typically require specialized modules, additional training, or fine-tuning of the model weights to achieve specific prosodic adjustments, limiting their utility for post-hoc, inference-time manipulation without retraining [2][3][5].

Parallel to explicit duration control, a distinct family of approaches utilizes latent variable modeling to capture and control prosodic and stylistic attributes. This includes the use of Global Style Tokens (GSTs) in Tacotron, which learn unsupervised embeddings to model acoustic expressiveness and enable style transfer [6]. Similarly, hierarchical generative models based on variational autoencoders (VAEs) have been proposed to control latent attributes such as speaking style, accent, and background noise through categorical and Gaussian latent variables [8]. Another significant contribution is the extension of Tacotron to learn a latent embedding space for prosody transfer, allowing the synthesis of audio that matches the prosody of a reference signal, even across different speakers [7]. While these latent space methods offer powerful capabilities for style and prosody transfer, they generally depend on the model being trained with these specific latent structures or requiring the extraction of embeddings from reference audio. Consequently, they are often tied to specific architectures and do not easily extend to arbitrary pre-trained TTS models for direct, post-hoc editing of internal representations without architectural modifications or additional training pipelines [6][7][8].

Addressing the specific challenge of mispronunciation, traditional methods have largely relied on grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) dictionaries, which are less practical in low-resource settings or for out-of-vocabulary words. Recent efforts have sought to mitigate this by leveraging speech data from non-target speakers for one-off correction of mispronunciations in grapheme-input TTS systems [10]. This approach attempts to bridge the gap between text input and accurate phonetic realization without relying solely on static dictionaries. However, such methods often operate at the input level or require specific correction modules, rather than manipulating the internal representations of the synthesis model itself. This limitation highlights a broader gap in the field: the lack of model-agnostic techniques that can directly edit the internal states of pre-trained TTS models to correct pronunciation errors or adjust prosody at inference time. Existing solutions, whether based on duration prediction [2][3][5], latent style tokens [6][7][8], or input-level correction [10], generally necessitate either retraining, architectural specificity, or external dictionary lookups, thereby restricting the flexibility and efficiency of real-time speech synthesis refinement.
