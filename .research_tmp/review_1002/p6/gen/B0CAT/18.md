**Related Works**

Industrial anomaly detection (IAD) has evolved significantly with the advent of deep learning, encompassing a diverse range of methodological sub-families including autoencoder-based reconstruction, generative adversarial networks (GANs), memory-bank outlier detection, and foundation-model-driven approaches. Early deep learning efforts focused on convolutional autoencoders, which identify defects by thresholding pixel-wise reconstruction errors, though these methods often struggle with localization inaccuracies and intensity-invariant defects [3]. To address the scarcity of anomalous samples, semi-supervised paradigms like GANomaly utilize conditional generative adversarial networks to learn the distribution of normal data, mapping inputs to latent spaces to detect deviations [4]. More recently, memory-bank methods such as PatchCore and SoftPatch have gained prominence; PatchCore employs a maximally representative memory bank of nominal patch-features to achieve state-of-the-art detection and localization, while SoftPatch extends this by incorporating noise discriminators to handle label-level noise in real-world scenarios [6][7]. A comprehensive survey of these techniques highlights the trade-offs between neural network architectures, supervision levels, and loss functions, noting that while performance has improved, the computational demands of these models remain a barrier for widespread industrial adoption [1].

The integration of foundation models has introduced a new paradigm in IAD, characterized by the use of large-scale pre-trained vision and vision-language models. This family includes self-supervised Vision Transformers (ViTs) like DINO and DINOv2, which learn robust visual features without supervision, and multimodal models such as CLIP, which leverage natural language supervision for zero-shot transfer [10][12][13]. AnomalyDINO, for instance, adapts DINOv2 for few-shot anomaly detection, demonstrating that high-quality visual features alone can rival vision-language models in industrial settings [5]. Furthermore, the Segment Anything Model (SAM) has emerged as a powerful tool for image segmentation, offering impressive zero-shot transfer capabilities due to its promptable design and massive training dataset [11]. While these foundation models provide superior generalization and feature representation, their inherent size and computational complexity make them impractical for direct deployment on resource-constrained embedded devices commonly found in Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs) [9].

To bridge the gap between high-performance foundation models and edge deployment, recent research has focused on model compression and lightweight adaptation strategies. This category includes knowledge distillation, pruning, quantization, and the development of mobile-specific architectures. Knowledge distillation, which trains a small student model to mimic a large teacher, is a representative technique for reducing model size while retaining accuracy [18]. Similarly, pruning methods remove redundant weights, and quantization reduces numerical precision to lower memory footprint and latency [16][17]. Specifically for segmentation tasks, MobileSAM addresses the inefficiency of the original SAM by replacing the heavyweight image encoder with a lightweight one, utilizing decoupled distillation to maintain performance on mobile devices [14]. Additionally, STLM proposes a SAM-guided two-stream lightweight model that leverages SAM’s generalization capabilities while employing lightweight encoders for efficient inference [8]. Despite these advancements, the field of Tiny Machine Learning (TinyML) continues to face challenges in jointly designing hardware and software for optimal performance on IoT devices, indicating that further optimization is required to fully realize the potential of foundation models in real-time industrial quality control [15].
