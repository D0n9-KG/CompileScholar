**Related Works**

Active learning (AL) aims to reduce annotation costs by strategically selecting the most informative unlabeled data for labeling. Early foundational work introduced uncertainty sampling, where models query instances with low confidence to maximize information gain [1][2]. Subsequent research expanded this to specific domains, such as multi-class image classification, where various uncertainty metrics are compared to identify high-value examples [3]. Comprehensive surveys have further synthesized these strategies, highlighting their utility in reducing manual labeling efforts [4]. More recent approaches have focused on improving the efficiency and task-agnostic nature of AL for deep networks. For instance, some methods employ matrix partitioning or pre-clustering to select active instances [8][9], while others frame the problem as core-set selection to ensure the selected subset is representative of the entire dataset [10]. Additionally, novel architectures have been proposed to predict target losses for unlabeled inputs, allowing the model to identify likely misclassifications efficiently [11]. Despite these advancements, most existing AL methods operate in centralized settings, often neglecting the computational overhead of deep models or the specific challenges of distributed data environments.

To address model uncertainty more rigorously, several studies have integrated Bayesian principles into deep learning. Bayes by Backprop offers a principled algorithm for learning weight distributions, using variational free energy to regularize weights and improve generalization [5]. Similarly, dropout has been reinterpreted as an approximate Bayesian inference method, enabling the extraction of uncertainty information from standard deep neural networks without significant computational cost [6]. These techniques provide a mathematical grounding for quantifying epistemic uncertainty, which is crucial for identifying samples near decision boundaries. However, while these methods effectively capture model uncertainty, they are typically applied in isolated training contexts and do not inherently account for the dynamic fluctuations in model parameters that occur during collaborative, distributed training processes.

Federated Learning (FL) has emerged as a paradigm for training models on decentralized data while preserving privacy. Foundational work established the framework for federated optimization, addressing the challenges of uneven data distribution and communication efficiency across a large number of nodes [13]. The introduction of Federated Averaging (FedAvg) demonstrated that iterative model averaging could robustly handle non-IID data distributions, significantly reducing communication rounds [14]. Further research has investigated the robustness of FL to data heterogeneity, showing that local training iterations and communication frequency play critical roles in mitigating the effects of biased sampling [19]. To address the high cost of annotation in FL, several studies have proposed Federated Active Learning (FAL). Early efforts applied AL strategies to waste and disaster image classification [15] and emergency response systems [16]. More formally, F-AL was proposed to collaboratively implement AL in a distributed manner, outperforming random sampling and separate client-level AL [17]. Recent work has also explored client selection strategies to maximize training efficiency based on local data characteristics [22].

Despite the progress in FAL, existing methods often fail to fully account for the heterogeneity of data distributions across clients and the resulting fluctuations in global and local model parameters. Some approaches attempt to address non-IID data through distribution regularization [18] or by leveraging knowledge-aware sampling that aligns local and global model discrepancies [20]. Others focus on inter-class diversity, proposing strategies that integrate global and local selector models to handle varying levels of local heterogeneity [21]. However, these methods may not sufficiently capture the temporal dynamics of epistemic variations or the specific oscillations of decision boundaries during training. Consequently, there remains a gap in efficiently identifying unlabeled samples with high epistemic variations that oscillate around decision boundaries, particularly in heterogeneous federation settings where data freeze and awaken mechanisms could enhance selection efficiency.
