3D human pose estimation from monocular images or videos has evolved significantly, moving from early geometric approaches [1] to deep learning-based systems. Early methods often relied on explicit 2D-to-3D lifting, where a simple feed-forward network could surprisingly achieve low error rates by mapping ground-truth 2D joints to 3D space [4]. To address the inherent ambiguity and occlusion in monocular settings, subsequent works introduced probabilistic and volumetric representations. For instance, coarse-to-fine volumetric prediction discretizes 3D space to predict per-voxel likelihoods, improving upon direct coordinate regression [2]. Similarly, HEMlets introduce part-centric heatmap triplets to bridge the gap between 2D observations and 3D interpretations, effectively handling depth uncertainty [21]. More recently, diffusion models have been employed to model the multi-hypothesis nature of pose estimation, predicting multiple plausible poses to mitigate the overconfidence of single-pose predictors [15][16]. While these methods improve robustness, they often increase computational complexity or require complex sampling procedures, and many still struggle to explicitly encode the hierarchical kinematic constraints of the human body structure.

To better capture the structural dependencies of the human skeleton, graph-based methods have become dominant. These approaches represent the skeleton as a graph, with joints as nodes and bones as edges. Initial graph convolutional networks (GCNs) exploited spatial-temporal relationships to model joint interactions [3][5]. To address the limitations of undirected graphs, directed graph convolutions were proposed to explicitly reflect the hierarchical parent-child relationships among joints, conditioning the graph topology on input poses [11]. Furthermore, modulated graph convolutions and semantic GCNs have been developed to enhance the representation of joint-bone interactions [6][5]. Other architectures, such as Graph Stacked Hourglass Networks, utilize multi-scale encoder-decoder structures to learn both local and global features across different skeletal scales [23]. Despite these advancements, standard GCNs often rely on pairwise interactions, which may be insufficient for modeling complex, high-order dependencies or long-range correlations between non-linked body parts, leading to suboptimal performance in occluded or dynamic scenarios.

Transformer architectures have emerged as a powerful alternative for modeling long-range dependencies, building upon the foundational attention mechanisms introduced in [20]. In the context of pose estimation, GraFormer adapts transformers for graph-oriented data, while HDFormer introduces high-order directed attention to capture complex bone and joint relationships, significantly reducing parameter counts compared to previous SOTA models [7][10]. To handle temporal sequences, Strided Transformers and MixSTE employ hierarchical and mixed spatio-temporal encoders to aggregate long-range information efficiently, reducing redundancy in video-based pose estimation [18][19]. MHFormer further extends this by modeling multi-hypothesis dependencies through self- and cross-hypothesis communication, addressing the inverse problem nature of pose estimation [9]. Additionally, non-local operations have been integrated into various architectures to capture global context without the computational burden of fully connected layers [14]. However, existing transformer-based methods often increase model size and introduce uncorrelated noise by making networks deeper to learn dependencies between non-linked parts. They frequently fail to explicitly modulate joints by their belonging body parts, treating the skeleton as a flat sequence rather than a structured hierarchy. This lack of explicit sub-structure modeling limits the efficiency and accuracy of capturing potential long-range dependencies across different scales of human anatomy.
