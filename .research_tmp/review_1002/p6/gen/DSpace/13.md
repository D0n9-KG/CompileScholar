## Related Work

**Gradient Conflict Resolution Strategies**
Prior research addressing long-tailed recognition has employed diverse strategies to mitigate the inherent bias toward head classes. One prominent approach involves decoupling the training process into separate phases for representation learning and classifier training, where head and tail classes are handled distinctly to prevent feature collapse [5, 8]. Alternatively, several methods modify the optimization landscape directly; for instance, some works utilize parametric class-wise learnable centers to rebalance the optimization dynamics [10, 11], while others apply heuristic re-weighting of positive and negative pairs based on effective sample counts [2]. Other strategies focus on data-level interventions, such as re-sampling and thresholding [1], or architectural modifications like bilateral-branch networks with cumulative learning [6]. Furthermore, adjustments to the loss function itself, including logit adjustment in softmax cross-entropy [9], class-averaging and class-complement mechanisms in Balanced Contrastive Learning [12], and mutual information maximization between features and labels [13], have been proposed to correct optimization biases. More recent contrastive-specific approaches include probabilistic sampling of pairs based on estimated class distributions [18], subclass-balancing via clustering head classes [19], and targeted convergence to uniformly distributed hypersphere targets [21]. However, standard Supervised Contrastive Learning (SCL) without modification [17] often fails to generalize well in imbalanced settings. In contrast to these heuristic or structural adjustments, our Aligned Contrastive Loss (ACL) explicitly eliminates gradient conflicts and imbalanced attraction/repulsion forces through a rigorous theoretical analysis, addressing the root cause of performance degradation rather than merely compensating for it.

**Theoretical Foundations**
The theoretical underpinnings of long-tailed learning vary significantly across the literature. A substantial body of work relies primarily on empirical observations without deriving explicit theoretical mechanisms for the observed improvements [1, 5, 6, 17, 19, 23]. Other studies provide specific analytical frameworks, such as optimization analysis of SCL bias [10, 11], geometric analysis of feature space distribution [12, 21], or theoretical frameworks based on data overlap and effective number of samples [2]. Some works offer statistical grounding for logit adjustment [9], information-theoretic bounds [13], or statistical distribution modeling using von Mises-Fisher mixtures [18]. While these contributions provide valuable insights into specific aspects of imbalance, none of the cited prior works perform a detailed gradient analysis of SCL to identify the specific conflict mechanisms between positive and negative pairs. Our work distinguishes itself by providing this causal explanation, identifying gradient conflicts as the fundamental issue that standard contrastive objectives fail to resolve in long-tailed scenarios.

**Multi-View Training Assumptions**
A common assumption in contrastive learning literature is that increasing the number of views (augmentations) consistently enhances model performance. This premise is explicitly adopted in several foundational and recent works, which assume that multi-view training always improves contrastive learning outcomes [22, 23]. However, this assumption may not hold in imbalanced settings where the distribution of classes is skewed. Our findings challenge this prevailing view by demonstrating that while multi-view training generally boosts performance, contrastive learning does not consistently enhance model generalization as the number of views increases in long-tailed scenarios. By acknowledging this limitation, our ACL algorithm is designed to be robust to the diminishing returns of additional views, ensuring stable performance across varying augmentation strategies.

**Evaluation Scope**
The evaluation of long-tailed recognition methods varies in scope and dataset diversity. Several studies validate their approaches across a broad range of benchmarks, including CIFAR, ImageNet, Places, and iNaturalist [2, 5, 6, 8]. Other works focus on single large-scale datasets, such as ImageNet only [17, 21, 22, 23], or specific subsets like MNIST, CIFAR-10, and ImageNet [1]. Some evaluations extend to six major image classification datasets [4], long-tailed benchmarks and semantic segmentation [11, 13], or ImageNet classification with downstream detection and segmentation tasks [14]. Additionally, some methods are evaluated on ImageNet self-supervised and semi-supervised learning benchmarks [15, 16] or are limited to CIFAR-100/100-LT [19]. Our ACL algorithm is validated across the same broad spectrum of CIFAR, ImageNet, Places, and iNaturalist datasets, demonstrating its generalizability and state-of-the-art performance across diverse data distributions and scales.
