## Related Work

### Backbone Architectures
Prior research on dense retrieval has predominantly relied on encoder-only architectures, specifically BERT-based models, to establish baseline performance and analyze retrieval dynamics [1, 2, 3, 4]. While Sentence-BERT [3] and SimCSE [4] utilize BERT to derive sentence embeddings for similarity search and contrastive learning, and DPR [1] and Contriever [2] employ dual-encoder structures for open-domain tasks, these works largely confine their analysis to the encoder-only paradigm. In contrast, recent efforts have begun to explore decoder-only architectures, such as RePLAMA [5], which introduces a decoder-based dense retriever for open-domain question answering. This paper extends the architectural scope by explicitly comparing encoder-only (BERT) and decoder-only (LLaMA) backbones, revealing that the dominance of pre-trained knowledge observed in BERT-based models does not universally hold for decoder-based architectures.

### Representation Aggregation
The method of aggregating token representations into a single vector is a critical design choice in dense retrieval, with prior work generally focusing on specific strategies in isolation. DPR [1] relies exclusively on the CLS token to represent passages, whereas Contriever [2] employs mean pooling, and other foundational works like Sentence-BERT [3] and SimCSE [4] standardize on mean pooling for their respective sentence embedding frameworks. Although Contriever [2] shares this paper’s approach of utilizing both mean pooling and CLS tokens in its comparative analysis, most prior studies do not systematically isolate the impact of these aggregation methods on knowledge acquisition. By contrasting CLS token usage in DPR with mean pooling in Contriever, this work demonstrates that the choice of representation strategy significantly influences how retrieval knowledge is acquired and adjusted during fine-tuning.

### Dataset Scope
The evaluation of dense retrieval models has historically been conducted on a variety of datasets, ranging from broad open-domain question answering benchmarks to specific semantic similarity tasks. Early works such as DPR [1] evaluated on a wide range of open-domain QA datasets, while Sentence-BERT [3] and SimCSE [4] focused on Semantic Textual Similarity (STS) and Natural Language Inference (NLI) tasks to validate embedding quality. More recently, large-scale benchmarks like MS MARCO [7] and Natural Questions [8] have become standard for evaluating retrieval performance in machine reading comprehension and open-domain QA. This paper aligns with the latter group by utilizing both MSMARCO and Natural Questions [7, 8], ensuring that the observed trends in knowledge acquisition are robust across different retrieval tasks and data distributions, rather than being artifacts of a single dataset.

### Reproducibility and Code Availability
While prior works have contributed significant methodological advancements in dense retrieval, such as the dual-encoder architecture in DPR [1], the unsupervised learning approach in Contriever [2], and the contrastive learning frameworks in Sentence-BERT [3] and SimCSE [4], none of the cited studies explicitly position themselves as reproducibility studies with a fully public implementation dedicated to verifying nuanced findings on neuron activation versus knowledge reorganization. This paper distinguishes itself by ensuring full reproducibility and making its implementation publicly available, thereby allowing the community to verify and build upon the specific insights regarding the interplay between pre-training and fine-tuning in dense retrievers.
