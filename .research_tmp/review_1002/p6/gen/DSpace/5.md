## Related Work

**Embedding Adaptation Strategy**
Prior research on instruction-following text embeddings has primarily relied on modifying the encoding process to incorporate instruction signals. One dominant approach involves fine-tuning the embedding model specifically for each instruction or task, as seen in methods that adapt pre-trained architectures like BERT for downstream tasks [4, 6, 7, 9]. Alternatively, several frameworks adopt a concatenation strategy, where the instruction text is directly appended to the input text during the encoding phase to condition the representation [10, 12, 14]. A distinct approach treats the instruction as a query, encoding the expected answers to instruction-derived questions to generate the final embedding [13]. In contrast, our work introduces a post-hoc transformation strategy that adapts pre-computed generic embeddings in real time, avoiding the need to re-encode the corpus or modify the original encoder for each new instruction.

**Computational Efficiency Profile**
The computational cost of instruction-following embeddings varies significantly across existing methods. Some approaches, such as those relying on fine-tuned LLMs or encoders for answer generation, incur standard inference latencies associated with large model processing [13]. Similarly, methods that use prompt-based control for retrieval or standard instruction-conditioned encoding operate with typical inference overheads [12, 14]. Other strategies, such as unified models that eliminate separate retrieval components, aim to reduce system latency but still require full model inference [10]. Notably, some prior work, including Sentence-BERT, achieves real-time, lightweight inference suitable for efficient similarity search [6]. However, many instruction-specific methods, particularly those requiring full corpus re-encoding for new instructions, suffer from high computational overhead [9]. Our method aligns with the efficiency profile of lightweight inference [6], achieving dramatic speedups of 6–300x by transforming pre-computed vectors rather than re-encoding text.

**Information Source for Instruction Alignment**
Existing methods differ in how they source the information necessary to align embeddings with specific instructions. A common paradigm involves feeding explicit instruction tokens directly into the encoder, thereby conditioning the embedding on the instruction text [10, 12, 14]. Other approaches rely on external data sources, such as synthetic data generated by proprietary large language models to fine-tune open-source models [9], or the encoding of expected answers to questions derived from the instruction [13]. Our work posits that instruction-relevant information is inherently encoded within generic embeddings but remains underutilized. By leveraging this inherent signal, we avoid the dependency on explicit instruction tokens or external synthetic data, instead using a guided transformation to surface the relevant attributes already present in the base representation.

**Training Data Requirement**
The data requirements for training instruction-following embedding models range from extensive to minimal. Many state-of-the-art methods, including those based on Transformer architectures and instruction-tuned retrievers, rely on large-scale instruction-tuning datasets to learn robust instruction-following capabilities [3, 9, 10, 12, 14]. Conversely, foundational embedding models often utilize unlabeled data with self-supervised objectives, such as contrastive learning with dropout or global co-occurrence statistics, to learn general representations [1, 2, 4, 7]. Some methods, like InBedder, are trained on abstractive question answering tasks, which may require specific task-oriented data [13]. Our approach requires only a small amount of text data with instruction-focused label annotation to guide the space transformation. This minimal data requirement distinguishes our method from those needing large-scale instruction datasets [3, 9, 10, 12, 14] and aligns with the data efficiency observed in Sentence-BERT [6], while offering superior instruction-following performance through guided transformation.
