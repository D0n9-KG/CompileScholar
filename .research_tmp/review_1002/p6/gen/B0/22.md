**Related Works**

Semantic segmentation has evolved significantly with the advent of deep learning. Early foundational work introduced fully convolutional networks (FCNs) to enable end-to-end pixel-wise prediction [1]. Subsequent advancements focused on capturing global context and multi-scale features, such as DeepLab’s atrous convolution [2], ParseNet’s global feature aggregation [3], and PSPNet’s pyramid pooling module [4]. More recently, Transformer-based architectures have demonstrated superior performance by modeling long-range dependencies, exemplified by SegFormer [6] and SETR [7]. These supervised methods, however, rely heavily on expensive pixel-level annotations, motivating the development of unsupervised domain adaptation (UDA) techniques.

UDA for semantic segmentation aims to transfer knowledge from a labeled source domain to an unlabeled target domain. Early approaches utilized adversarial learning to align feature distributions, such as methods adapting structured output spaces [8] and using conditional GANs [9]. Other strategies focused on learning texture-invariant representations [10] or addressing intra-domain gaps through self-supervision [11]. To improve the quality of pseudo-labels, researchers have employed discriminative patch representations [12], synergistic image and feature adaptation [13], and separated semantic features [14]. More recent methods have shifted towards consistency-based training, such as PixMatch’s pixelwise consistency [15], and have explored the impact of modern network architectures in UDA settings, as seen in DAFormer [16] and HRDA’s multi-resolution training [17]. Self-training remains a core component, with various techniques proposed to mitigate noise, including class-balanced sampling [18], maximum squares loss [19], confidence regularization [20], and self-supervised depth estimation [21]. Additionally, curriculum learning [22], bidirectional learning [23], uncertainty-aware label refinement [24], and prototypical pseudo-label denoising [25] have been introduced to enhance adaptation robustness. Cross-domain mixed sampling has also shown effectiveness in leveraging both labeled and pseudo-labeled data [26].

Despite these advances, traditional UDA methods typically assume that the category sets of the source and target domains are known and identical. This assumption is often unrealistic in real-world scenarios where private classes may exist in the target domain. Universal Domain Adaptation (UNDA) addresses this by handling arbitrary category shifts. Universal Multi-Source Domain Adaptation (UMDA) extends this to multiple source domains with unknown target label sets [27]. Methods like DANCE utilize self-supervised neighborhood clustering and entropy-based alignment to handle open-set and partial settings without prior knowledge of the category overlap [30]. Other approaches focus on detecting open classes [28] or using self-supervised tasks like rotation recognition to separate known and unknown samples [29]. OVANet proposes a one-vs-all network to learn a threshold for rejecting unknown classes based on inter-class distances [31].

Our work, UniDA-SS, builds upon these foundations but specifically targets the semantic segmentation domain under the Universal setting. Unlike previous UNDA methods that often rely on global feature alignment or simple thresholding, we address the specific challenge of low confidence scores for common classes in the target domain. We introduce UniMAP, which employs Domain-Specific Prototype-based Distinction (DSPD) to separate domain-specific features and Target-based Image Matching (TIM) to promote effective learning of common classes. This approach offers a robust solution for adaptation without prior knowledge of category settings, complementing existing adversarial and self-training frameworks.
