**Related Works**

Opinion mining in the context of mobile application reviews has traditionally focused on sentiment polarity, aiming to classify user feedback as positive, negative, or neutral. Early tools such as Appsent were developed to alleviate the labor-intensive nature of manually reading app reviews, providing developers with automated analytics to guide software evolution and improve user satisfaction [2]. While these tools successfully addressed the volume of feedback, they primarily operated at a coarse-grained level, often overlooking the nuanced emotional states that drive user behavior. More recent efforts have begun to explore finer-grained aspects of user experience. For instance, empirical analyses of mobile learning apps have examined usage experiences to understand specific user pain points, though these studies often remain descriptive rather than providing structured, large-scale datasets for computational modeling [3]. Furthermore, specific datasets have been created to capture multilabel sentiment and emotion in app reviews, such as work focusing on Indonesian mobile applications, which highlights the growing interest in moving beyond binary sentiment to capture complex emotional responses [1]. However, the systematic application of established psychological taxonomies, such as Plutchik’s wheel of emotions, to app review data remains underexplored, particularly regarding the development of rigorous annotation guidelines that account for the ambiguity inherent in emotional expression.

The challenge of fine-grained emotion classification is compounded by the subjectivity and complexity of emotional interpretation. Unlike part-of-speech tagging or basic semantic tagging, which can be automated with high accuracy, pragmatic and discursive features often lack a direct mapping to lexical forms, necessitating manual annotation that is both time-consuming and error-prone [7]. This limitation has spurred research into the feasibility of using Large Language Models (LLMs) to assist or replace human annotators. Recent studies have evaluated LLMs as substitutes for human experts in various domains. In political text annotation, LLMs have shown potential to streamline the process, although their reliability in capturing subtle nuances is still being debated [4]. Similarly, in materials science, semi-automated approaches using models like Gemini Pro have been employed to craft annotated outputs, demonstrating that LLMs can handle domain-specific text with appropriate prompting [5]. In the financial sector, studies have assessed the effectiveness and efficiency of LLMs as data annotators, finding that while they offer significant speed advantages, careful validation is required to ensure data quality [6].

Specific to linguistic and pragmatic annotation, research has compared the performance of different LLM architectures against human coders. For example, in the annotation of apology components in English, GPT-4 was found to outperform GPT-3.5, with accuracy approaching that of human coders, suggesting that advanced LLMs can be successfully deployed to make pragma-discursive corpus annotation more efficient and scalable [7]. These findings align with broader trends in annotation methodology, where human-LLM collaboration is increasingly viewed as a viable strategy. Systems such as MEGAnno+ have been proposed to facilitate this collaboration, leveraging the speed of LLMs while retaining human oversight for quality control [8]. Furthermore, recent work emphasizes the importance of effective verification mechanisms in human-LLM collaborative annotation, arguing that verifying LLM labels is crucial for maintaining high accuracy in complex annotation tasks [9].

Our work builds upon these foundations by adapting Plutchik’s emotion taxonomy specifically for app reviews. While previous studies have demonstrated the potential of LLMs in general text annotation [4][5][6][7] and the benefits of human-LLM collaboration [8][9], few have addressed the specific challenges of fine-grained emotion classification in the app review domain. We contribute to this literature by developing a structured annotation framework and dataset, documenting the challenges in emotion classification, and evaluating the cost-effectiveness and agreement of LLM-based automation with human-labeled data. By integrating insights from pragmatic annotation [7] and collaborative systems [8][9], we aim to provide a robust pipeline for capturing the complexity of emotions in user feedback, bridging the gap between coarse-grained sentiment analysis and detailed psychological emotion modeling.
