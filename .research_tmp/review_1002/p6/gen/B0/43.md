**Related Works**

The training of large language models (LLMs) has become a central focus in machine learning, driven by the architectural advancements of the Transformer [6] and the scaling laws observed in models like GPT-3 [7] and PaLM [8]. As model sizes expand to hundreds of billions of parameters, efficient distributed training systems are essential. Frameworks such as Megatron-LM [9][10] and DeepSpeed [11] have established foundational techniques for 3D parallelism, combining data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism to scale training across thousands of GPUs. More recently, sequence parallelism has emerged to address memory constraints associated with long contexts, with methods like Ring Self-Attention [16], DeepSpeed-Ulysses [17], and Ring Attention [18] enabling the training of extremely long sequences by partitioning data along the sequence dimension.

To automate the complex configuration of these parallelism strategies, several systems have been proposed. Galvatron [15] employs decision trees and dynamic programming to search for optimal hybrid parallelism strategies. Similarly, GSPMD [12] and Alpa [14] utilize compiler-based approaches to infer tensor partitioning and automate inter- and intra-operator parallelism, respectively. These systems aim to reduce the manual effort required to tune distributed training configurations, although the vast search space of possible strategies remains a significant challenge for achieving peak efficiency in production environments.

Accurate performance modeling is critical for guiding these optimization efforts and for system-level co-design. Early work in this area includes Astra-Sim [23] and its successor Astra-Sim 2.0 [24], which enable software/hardware co-design exploration for distributed deep learning platforms by modeling hierarchical networks and disaggregated systems. Amped [19] proposes an analytical model specifically for predicting the performance of distributed Transformer training, while Calculon [20] offers a methodology for high-level co-design of systems and LLMs. However, many of these analytical models rely on simplified assumptions that may not fully capture the complex execution behaviors of modern LLMs on heterogeneous hardware.

Trace-driven performance modeling offers a more accurate alternative by leveraging real execution data. Daydream [21] was an early tool that used low-level traces collected by CUPTI [4] to build fine-grained dependency graphs, allowing for the prediction of runtime under various optimizations. Other profiling tools, such as NVProf [3] and PyTorch Kineto [5], provide detailed insights into GPU and CPU execution but often lack the predictive capabilities needed to estimate performance for new, untested configurations. While these tools are valuable for diagnosing bottlenecks, they do not always facilitate the efficient exploration of model and deployment configurations required for large-scale cluster optimization.

Lumos distinguishes itself by combining the accuracy of trace-driven modeling with the predictive power needed for configuration exploration. Unlike analytical models that may oversimplify complex interactions, Lumos replays execution traces from production clusters to achieve high fidelity. Furthermore, it extends beyond simple replay by validating its ability to estimate performance for new setups based on existing traces, thereby facilitating efficient exploration of the vast space of model and deployment configurations on modern hardware like NVIDIA H100s [1] and TPU v4 [2]. This approach addresses the gap between static profiling tools and analytical models, providing a robust toolkit for optimizing large-scale LLM training.
