**Related Works**

Differential Privacy (DP) has emerged as the de facto standard for protecting individual privacy in data analysis [1][6]. However, the security guarantees of DP rely heavily on the faithful execution of noise generation mechanisms by the entities responsible for adding noise. In centralized settings, a malicious server can manipulate results by sampling noise from incorrect distributions or generating correlated noise, effectively using the randomness of DP as a smokescreen for nefarious activities [1]. This vulnerability is particularly acute in distributed environments where no single entity is fully trusted.

Early work on addressing these trust issues introduced the concept of Verifiable Differential Privacy (VDP). VDP requires the publishing entity to provide a zero-knowledge proof (ZKP) that convinces a verifier that the output is both differentially private and reliable [1]. This approach resolves the paradox of verifying randomness without learning it by mixing private and public randomness. While VDP established a theoretical foundation for verifiable privacy, its initial implementations were primarily focused on counting queries and faced significant computational overheads in proof generation.

In the context of Local Differential Privacy (LDP), where data providers add noise locally, malicious actors can manipulate their outputs before transmission, exploiting the fact that estimations are normalized by the randomization probability [3]. To counter this, verifiable randomization mechanisms have been proposed to allow data collectors to verify the completeness of the agreed-upon randomization process for each provider [3]. Similarly, cryptographic techniques have been developed to guarantee unconditional privacy for respondents in polls, ensuring that cheating respondents cannot affect the tally beyond their own vote [5]. These works highlight the critical need for verifying the integrity of local noise generation to prevent output manipulation attacks.

More recently, the focus has shifted to distributed models that leverage Secure Multi-Party Computation (MPC) to achieve DP without a trusted central server. Systems like Crypt$\epsilon$ employ two non-colluding untrusted servers to run DP programs on encrypted data, aiming to combine the utility of the central model with the privacy guarantees of the local model [6]. In this distributed setting, the efficient and secure sampling of noise becomes a primary challenge. Several studies have proposed methods for securely sampling discrete Gaussian noise and other distributions using MPC [8][9]. For instance, protocols for securely sampling biased coins have been developed with specific applications to DP [8], while others focus on discrete Gaussian noise for multi-party settings [9].

Despite these advancements, existing secure sampling protocols often suffer from high communication costs and computational overheads, limiting their practicality. Recent benchmarking efforts have highlighted the lack of comprehensive experimental evaluations comparing the performance of various MPC-based sampling protocols [10]. These benchmarks reveal that while many methods exist, they differ significantly in security assumptions and efficiency, with a notable gap in practical deployment metrics. Furthermore, specialized protocols like DPrio have been designed to improve utility in Prio-based systems, though they may not address the full spectrum of verifiability requirements against malicious server behavior [2].

Our work builds upon these foundations by introducing Verifiable Distributed Differential Privacy (VDDP). Unlike previous VDP approaches that rely heavily on ZKPs, we demonstrate that while ZKPs are sufficient, they are not necessary for achieving DP under verifiability requirements. We propose efficient mechanisms, including the Verifiable Distributed Discrete Laplacian Mechanism (VDDLM) and an improved Verifiable Randomized Response (VRR) solution, which significantly reduce proof generation and communication costs compared to state-of-the-art methods [1][3][10].
