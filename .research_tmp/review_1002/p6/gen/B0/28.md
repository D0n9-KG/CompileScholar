**Related Works**

Active learning (AL) has emerged as a pivotal strategy to mitigate the high costs of human annotation in machine learning systems. Early foundational work introduced uncertainty sampling, where models select instances for which they have the lowest confidence, significantly reducing the amount of labeled data required for effective training [1][2]. Subsequent research expanded these principles to complex domains, such as multi-class image classification, where various uncertainty metrics are employed to identify high-value examples for Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) [3]. Comprehensive surveys have further synthesized these approaches, highlighting the trade-offs between different query strategies [4]. More recent advancements focus on improving the efficiency and generalizability of AL for deep networks. For instance, methods have been proposed to predict target losses for unlabeled inputs, allowing the model to efficiently suggest data likely to result in incorrect predictions [11]. Additionally, core-set selection approaches have been developed to choose subsets of data that ensure the learned model remains competitive for the remaining data points, addressing the limitations of traditional heuristics in batch settings [10]. Other techniques utilize pre-clustering [9] or matrix partitioning [8] to enhance instance sampling, while semi-supervised frameworks leverage temporal output discrepancies to refine selection [7].

Federated Learning (FL) addresses the challenge of training models on decentralized, privacy-sensitive data by aggregating locally computed updates without sharing raw data. The foundational work on federated optimization established the framework for training high-quality centralized models from unevenly distributed data across a large number of nodes [13]. This was further advanced by practical methods for communication-efficient learning of deep networks, which demonstrated robustness to unbalanced and non-IID data distributions [14]. However, the heterogeneity of data distribution across clients remains a significant challenge, leading to fluctuations in global and local model parameters. Research has investigated the robustness of FL to such data heterogeneity, showing that performance depends on local training iterations and communication frequency [19]. To mitigate these issues, distribution-regularized FL techniques have been proposed to handle non-IID data more effectively [18]. Furthermore, active federated learning strategies have been introduced to select clients for training based on their potential to improve the global model, thereby reducing communication costs and training iterations [22].

Federated Active Learning (FAL) combines the benefits of AL and FL to reduce annotation costs while preserving data confidentiality. Initial efforts applied AL within the FL framework to collaboratively select informative instances in a distributed manner, demonstrating that FAL can outperform conventional random sampling and client-level separate AL [17]. Subsequent studies have explored FAL for specific applications, such as waste and natural disaster image classification [15] and emergency response systems [16]. A critical challenge in FAL is the mismatch between the active sampling goals of the global model and asynchronous local clients, particularly under non-IID data conditions. To address this, Knowledge-Aware Federated Active Learning (KAFAL) was proposed, which samples based on discrepancies between local and global models to intensify specialized knowledge [20]. Similarly, recent work has re-examined FAL by focusing on inter-class diversity, demonstrating that the superiority of global versus local selector models depends on this diversity. The proposed LoGo strategy integrates both models to remain robust against varying levels of local heterogeneity and global imbalance [21].

Despite these advancements, existing FAL methods often fail to fully account for the dynamic heterogeneity of data distributions and the associated parameter fluctuations. Our proposed method, CHASe, addresses these gaps by focusing on epistemic variations (EVs) that oscillate around decision boundaries. By tracking EVs through inference inconsistencies across epochs and calibrating decision boundaries with a new alignment loss, CHASe enhances both the effectiveness and efficiency of data selection in heterogeneous federation settings. This approach builds upon the theoretical foundations of uncertainty estimation in deep learning, such as Bayesian approximations via dropout [6] and weight uncertainty [5], but adapts them specifically for the collaborative and heterogeneous nature of federated active learning.
