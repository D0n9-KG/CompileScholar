**Related Works**

Sequential recommendation has evolved significantly from early recurrent neural network (RNN) approaches to modern transformer-based architectures. Early methods, such as GRU4Rec, applied RNNs to model session-based user behaviors, addressing the limitations of matrix factorization in sparse data scenarios [2]. Subsequent works introduced attention mechanisms to capture user intent more effectively; for instance, NARM utilized a hybrid encoder with attention to model both sequential behavior and the user’s main purpose within a session [4]. The advent of the Transformer architecture, which relies solely on self-attention mechanisms to process sequences, marked a paradigm shift in sequence modeling [6]. In the context of recommendation, SASRec proposed a self-attention based sequential model that balances long-term semantics with local relevance, outperforming RNN-based methods [7]. BERT4Rec further advanced this line of work by introducing a bidirectional encoder using a Cloze task to predict masked items, thereby leveraging both left and right context to enhance representation learning [8]. More recently, researchers have explored efficient variants of self-attention, such as low-rank decomposed networks to reduce computational overhead [11], and sparse-interest networks to handle diverse user intentions [10]. These developments underscore the centrality of the self-attention module in capturing complex correlations among user behaviors.

Cross-domain sequential recommendation (CDSR) extends sequential recommendation by leveraging user interactions across multiple domains to mitigate data sparsity and improve prediction accuracy. Early cross-domain approaches often relied on collective matrix factorization or shared embedding spaces to transfer knowledge between domains [13][15]. However, these methods frequently struggled with the dynamic nature of user preferences and the specific challenges of sequential data. To address this, several frameworks have been proposed that explicitly model cross-domain interactions. For example, DTCDR introduced a dual-target framework to handle cross-domain recommendation, while Pi-Net proposed a parallel information-sharing network for shared-account scenarios [14][20]. More recent works have focused on the flow of information between domains. C^2DSR utilized graph neural networks to mine inter-sequence item relationships and jointly learn single- and cross-domain preferences, aiming to overcome the bottlenecks of implicit transfer modules [16]. Similarly, Mixed Information Flow networks incorporated both behavioral and knowledge transfer units to decide when and how to use cross-domain information [21].

Despite these advancements, many existing CDSR frameworks are built upon self-attention transformers but seek improvements by injecting additional domain-specific components, such as domain-aware module blocks or mixed attention layers [22]. While these components can be beneficial, they often overlook the inherent potential of the core self-attention mechanism itself. For instance, MAN employs local and global attention modules to extract domain-specific and cross-domain information, yet it still relies on complex auxiliary structures [22]. Other approaches, such as Pacer and Runner, address negative transfer by adaptively weighting prediction losses based on the degree of negative transfer estimated between single-domain and cross-domain models [17]. Similarly, MDMTRec and other multi-domain frameworks attempt to balance multi-task learning across domains [18][19]. However, these methods typically treat cross-domain learning as a separate optimization challenge or rely on explicit transfer modules, rather than enhancing the fundamental attention mechanism to naturally facilitate knowledge transfer. Our work diverges from these approaches by focusing on the self-attention module itself, formulating cross-domain learning as a multi-objective problem to dynamically minimize cross-domain attention scores while optimizing recommendation performance. This perspective allows for a more integrated and efficient handling of negative transfer and complementary knowledge exchange, without the need for heavy hyper-parameter tuning or complex additional modules.
