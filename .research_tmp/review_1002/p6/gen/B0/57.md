**Related Works**

Generative Retrieval (GR) has emerged as a paradigm shift in information retrieval, moving away from the traditional index-retrieve pipeline towards end-to-end neural models. Early foundational work, such as GENRE, introduced the concept of retrieving entities by autoregressively generating their unique names, thereby avoiding the need for large dense vector stores [1]. This approach was extended to document retrieval by the Differentiable Search Index (DSI), which demonstrated that a single Transformer could encode an entire corpus in its parameters, mapping queries directly to document identifiers (docids) [2]. Subsequent studies further validated this model-based indexing paradigm. DynamicRetriever proposed a pre-training model-based system that embeds document-level information into model parameters, eliminating the need for sparse or dense indices [3]. Similarly, the Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI) and Ultron explored sequence-to-sequence networks that generate docids directly, with Ultron specifically addressing the challenge of semantically deficient identifiers through richer docid designs and multi-stage training [4][5]. More recently, RIPOR has shown that GR models can be scaled to large, standard benchmarks, addressing previous skepticism regarding their real-world applicability by optimizing the sequential decoding nature of docid generation [6].

A critical component of GR is the design of document identifiers. Existing methods generally fall into two categories: numeric-based and text-based docids. Numeric docids, often derived from clustering or quantization, offer high efficiency but may lack semantic richness. In contrast, text-based docids, such as titles or substrings, leverage the pretrained knowledge of language models. Autoregressive Search Engines proposed using n-grams as identifiers to avoid imposing hierarchical structures on the search space, allowing for more flexible matching [12]. Other works have explored synthetic identifiers that integrate contextualized information, or multiview identifiers that combine titles, substrings, and synthetic codes to provide holistic ranking perspectives [11]. Furthermore, GenRet introduced a method to learn document tokenization via discrete auto-encoding, aiming to capture complete semantic information that fixed rule-based docids often miss [7]. Recent efforts have also extended GR to handle multi-graded relevance, ensuring that identifiers are both semantically relevant and distinct [10].

Despite these advances, most GR research has focused on static corpora, where the document collection remains fixed during training and evaluation. However, real-world search engines operate on dynamic corpora where documents are continuously added. The adaptability of GR in such evolving environments has been a subject of recent investigation. DSI++ explored updating transformer memory with new documents, while CorpusBrain++ proposed a continual generative pre-training framework to maintain performance as knowledge evolves [13][14]. CLEVER addressed the continual learning problem by introducing Incremental Product Quantization to encode new documents with low computational cost and a memory-augmented mechanism to prevent catastrophic forgetting [15]. Additionally, recent empirical studies have benchmarked GR on dynamic corpora, such as StreamingQA, finding that GR models are more adaptable to evolving knowledge and more efficient in inference and storage compared to dual-encoder baselines [16].

However, a systematic comparison of different docid types in dynamic settings remains limited. While some works highlight the efficiency of numeric docids, others suggest the superior generalization of text-based identifiers due to their alignment with language model priors. Our work builds upon these insights by systematically evaluating representative GR approaches over dynamic corpora. We reveal that text-based docids, particularly those with fine-grained designs, exhibit superior generalization to unseen documents, often surpassing BM25 and dense retrieval methods. In contrast, numeric-based docids, while efficient, suffer from significant performance drops due to overfitting to the initial document set. By identifying the critical advantages of text-based docids—semantic alignment, fine-grained design, and lexical diversity—we propose a novel multi-docid design that combines the efficiency of numeric identifiers with the effectiveness of text-based ones, achieving improved performance in dynamic environments without requiring additional retraining.
