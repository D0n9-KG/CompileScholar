Continual learning (CL) aims to enable models to acquire new knowledge sequentially without suffering from catastrophic forgetting. Early approaches addressed this by modifying network architectures or optimization constraints. Progressive Neural Networks introduced lateral connections to leverage prior features while preventing weight interference [1]. Regularization-based methods, such as Elastic Weight Consolidation, mitigate forgetting by penalizing changes to weights deemed important for previous tasks [2]. More recent optimization-based strategies, like Gradient Episodic Memory (GEM) and its efficient variant A-GEM, constrain gradient updates to avoid increasing the loss on past tasks [6][8]. Gradient Projection Memory (GPM) and Trust Region Gradient Projection (TRGP) further refine this by projecting gradients onto subspaces orthogonal to those of previous tasks, thereby minimizing interference while allowing beneficial transfer [3][4].

Rehearsal-based methods represent a distinct class of CL algorithms that store a subset of past data in a memory buffer and train on it concurrently with new data. This strategy has shown superior performance in many settings. Deep Generative Replay uses a generative model to synthesize past data, reducing storage requirements [5]. iCaRL combines experience replay with a class-incremental learning strategy to maintain strong classifiers and representations [7]. These methods typically employ a concurrent rehearsal strategy, where old and new data are mixed during training. However, the optimality of this concurrent approach remains an open question, particularly regarding how task similarity influences the trade-off between forgetting and generalization.

Theoretical understanding of CL has largely focused on regularization and optimization constraints, with limited attention to rehearsal strategies. Recent works have provided theoretical guarantees for orthogonal gradient descent in the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime, establishing bounds on forgetting and generalization [9]. The NTK overlap matrix has been introduced to quantify task similarity and its impact on catastrophic forgetting, showing that forgetting increases as tasks align [10]. From a loss approximation viewpoint, regularization-based CL can be unified as a second-order Taylor approximation, highlighting the importance of Hessian accuracy for convergence and generalization [11]. In the linear setting, studies have analyzed continual classification on separable data, linking weak regularization to sequential max-margin problems [12]. Other works have derived exact expressions for forgetting in overparameterized linear regression, connecting CL to alternating projections and the Kaczmarz method [13].

More recent theoretical analyses have begun to characterize the factors influencing forgetting and generalization in overparameterized models. One study provided the first explicit forms for expected forgetting and generalization error in linear models, demonstrating how overparameterization, task similarity, and ordering affect performance [14]. Another analysis of linear regression via SGD revealed that task arrangement and step size significantly impact forgetting, with tasks having larger eigenvalues in their covariance matrices causing more forgetting when trained later [15]. The role of overparameterization in mitigating forgetting has also been examined, showing that sufficient model capacity can reduce risk gain on previous tasks even without explicit regularization [16]. Furthermore, theoretical insights into multi-task and replay-based CL with overparameterized linear models have characterized the impact of buffer size and model capacity on forgetting rates [17].

Despite these advances, a comprehensive theoretical comparison of concurrent versus sequential rehearsal strategies is lacking. While prior work has analyzed forgetting in linear models [13][14][15][16][17], few have explicitly compared the concurrent and sequential training paradigms. Our work fills this gap by providing a theoretical analysis of rehearsal-based CL in overparameterized linear models. We characterize the forgetting and generalization error for both concurrent and sequential rehearsal, showing that sequential rehearsal is superior when tasks are less similar. This insight motivates a hybrid rehearsal method that adapts the training strategy based on task similarity, offering a novel theoretical and practical contribution to the field of continual learning.
