**Related Works**

The rapid deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) has necessitated rigorous evaluation frameworks to assess their capabilities and societal impacts. Comprehensive benchmarks such as HELM [1] and BIG-bench [13] have established multi-metric approaches to evaluate LLMs across diverse scenarios, including accuracy, calibration, and fairness. However, these general-purpose benchmarks often treat fairness as a secondary metric or rely on discrete accuracy-based evaluations that may overlook nuanced behavioral biases. Recent surveys highlight the complexity of fairness in LLMs, noting that biases can propagate from training data to downstream tasks [3][6]. While existing literature categorizes fairness research into intrinsic and extrinsic biases [3], there remains a gap in metrics that capture the internal confidence dynamics of model decisions, particularly when accuracy metrics appear neutral.

A significant body of work focuses on gender bias in coreference resolution, a task closely related to the Winograd Schema Challenge [10]. Early studies introduced datasets like WinoBias [11] and Winogender schemas [12] to demonstrate that coreference systems systematically link gendered pronouns to stereotypical occupations. Subsequent research expanded this to large-scale, real-world data, such as the BUG dataset [17], and investigated bias in open-ended generation using the BOLD dataset [14]. More recently, studies have examined how LLMs amplify gender stereotypes in occupation prediction [15] and how biases transfer from pre-trained models to prompt-adapted versions [4]. Despite these advances, most evaluations rely on standard fairness metrics like Equalized Odds, which measure prediction correctness but fail to account for the model’s uncertainty or confidence levels during the decision process.

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) has emerged as a critical area for assessing the reliability and trustworthiness of neural networks. Foundational work in deep learning distinguishes between aleatoric and epistemic uncertainty [22][25][29], with recent surveys providing comprehensive overviews of UQ in deep neural networks [18] and natural language processing [19]. In the context of LLMs, researchers have explored UQ to detect hallucinations and misclassifications [20][21][26][27]. For instance, semantic entropy has been proposed to measure uncertainty in language generation [26], while other works investigate the calibration of LLMs’ self-evaluation capabilities [28]. Recent benchmarks have begun to integrate UQ into LLM evaluation, revealing that higher accuracy does not always correlate with higher certainty [8]. However, the intersection of UQ and fairness remains underexplored. While some studies suggest that uncertainty can be leveraged to improve the fairness-utility trade-off [35] or that fairness interventions might impact reliability [32], there is a lack of standardized metrics that explicitly incorporate uncertainty to evaluate fairness in LLMs.

Our work bridges these domains by proposing UCerF, an uncertainty-aware fairness metric, and a new gender-occupation dataset for coreference resolution. Unlike previous fairness benchmarks that focus solely on discrete outcomes [7][9], our approach captures the implicit impact of model confidence on group fairness. By addressing the data size and diversity limitations of existing datasets [9][17], we provide a more robust foundation for evaluating the internal biases of modern LLMs, thereby advancing the transparency and accountability of AI systems.
