Fairness in recommender systems has emerged as a critical research area, driven by the need to mitigate bias in data-driven decision-making processes. Early surveys have systematically organized the foundations, methods, and applications of fairness in recommendation, highlighting the fragmented nature of early studies and the necessity for a unified perspective [1][2]. As the field matured, the focus shifted from single-stakeholder fairness to multi-stakeholder recommendation, recognizing that recommendation systems operate within two-sided markets involving both users and item providers [6][7]. This shift acknowledges that optimizing for one party often comes at the expense of the other, necessitating a balanced approach that considers the interests of multiple parties simultaneously [7].

A significant body of literature has addressed this trade-off by proposing algorithms that balance user utility with item fairness. FairRec, for instance, maps the fair recommendation problem to a constrained allocation of indivisible goods, guaranteeing specific fairness metrics for both producers and customers [5][14]. Similarly, TFROM theoretically analyzes the relationship between recommendation quality, customer fairness, and provider fairness, designing models that maintain performance while ensuring two-sided fairness [13]. Other approaches, such as CPFair, integrate consumer and producer fairness constraints into a joint optimization framework, demonstrating that it is possible to improve fairness on both sides without significantly degrading overall recommendation quality [12]. Furthermore, policy learning frameworks have been developed to optimize utility metrics while satisfying exposure fairness constraints, expanding the class of learnable ranking functions to stochastic policies [9].

Recent works have increasingly drawn analogies from economics to formalize the fairness-accuracy trade-off. The concept of fair re-ranking has been re-examined through the lens of taxation, where redistributing ranking slots is viewed as a tax on high-utility items to benefit lower-utility ones [8]. This perspective highlights that previous item-level tax policies often lack continuity and controllability, leading to unpredictable shifts in accuracy and fairness. To address these limitations, methods like Tax-rank have been proposed to levy taxes based on utility differences, aiming for more precise control over the trade-off [8]. Additionally, the use of generalized Gini welfare functions has been explored to specify normative criteria for fairness, leveraging non-smooth optimization to minimize inequality in item exposure [11]. These economic-inspired approaches provide a rigorous framework for understanding how fairness constraints impact ranking performance.

Despite these advances, the underlying mechanisms governing the accuracy-fairness trade-off remain not fully understood. Existing evaluations often rely on single fairness metrics, which may not capture the full complexity of the trade-off across different elasticity levels. The FairDiverse toolkit has been introduced to standardize the evaluation of fair and diverse algorithms, addressing inconsistencies in experimental setups and enabling more direct comparisons [3]. However, there is a need for evaluation frameworks that can analyze algorithm performance across varying degrees of fairness sensitivity. Our work builds upon these foundations by introducing the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve) and the ElasticRank algorithm, which utilize elasticity calculations to adjust inter-item distances, thereby providing a more comprehensive assessment of fair re-ranking algorithms. By drawing on the economic analogy of commodity tax transfer, we aim to elucidate the mechanisms behind the accuracy-fairness trade-off and offer a robust solution for fair re-ranking.
