**Related Works**

The alignment of Large Language Models (LLMs) with human values has become a central focus in AI safety, primarily driven by the risks of harmful outputs [10]. Standard alignment techniques, such as Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), have proven effective in training models to be helpful and harmless [1][2]. However, the robustness of these safety measures is increasingly questioned. Research has demonstrated that safety alignment can be easily subverted through fine-tuning on small datasets of malicious examples, a phenomenon termed "Shadow Alignment" [5]. Similarly, LoRA fine-tuning has been shown to efficiently undo safety training in large models like Llama 2-Chat [4], and even benign fine-tuning can inadvertently compromise safety [6]. Furthermore, adversarial attacks, including jailbreaking via adaptive prompts [7] and universal adversarial suffixes [8], highlight the fragility of current alignment strategies, often achieving high attack success rates against state-of-the-art models [9].

To address these vulnerabilities and enhance control over model behavior, recent work has shifted towards mechanistic interpretability and activation steering. The "linear representation hypothesis" posits that high-level concepts are encoded as linear directions in the model's activation space [26]. This hypothesis is supported by evidence of emergent linear representations in sequence models [28] and the low-dimensional linear geometry of contextualized word representations [29]. Building on this, Representation Engineering (RepE) offers a top-down approach to manipulate these population-level representations to improve transparency and safety [13]. Specifically, Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA) and Activation Addition (ActAdd) methods compute steering vectors by contrasting activations of positive and negative examples, allowing for inference-time control over behaviors such as sentiment and toxicity [17][18]. More recently, Conditional Activation Steering (CAST) has been proposed to selectively apply steering based on input context, enabling fine-grained control over specific content categories [19].

A significant body of literature focuses specifically on the mechanism of refusal in LLMs. Studies have revealed that refusal behavior is often mediated by a single direction in the activation space; erasing this direction can disable refusal, while adding it can elicit refusal even for harmless prompts [11]. This insight has been leveraged for white-box jailbreaks and for understanding how adversarial suffixes suppress the refusal-mediating direction [11]. To refine this control, Affine Concept Editing (ACE) proposes an affine decomposition of activation vectors, combining subspace projection and activation addition to achieve more precise control over refusal than directional ablation alone [12]. Other approaches, such as Spectral Editing of Activations (SEA), project representations into directions with maximal covariance with positive demonstrations to mitigate undesirable behaviors like hallucination [14]. Additionally, category-wise safety steering methods aim to extract informative steering vectors for specific safety categories, offering a training-free alternative to traditional alignment [15].

Despite these advances, existing methods often rely on predefined refusal templates or manual analysis to identify steering directions. For instance, many steering techniques require labeled pairs of harmful and harmless prompts to compute contrastive vectors [17][18]. Furthermore, the identification of viable steering directions and target layers remains a challenge, particularly in adversarial settings or with weakly aligned models. Our work, COSMIC, addresses this gap by introducing an automated framework for direction selection using cosine similarity metrics. Unlike prior methods that depend on model outputs or specific refusal tokens, COSMIC operates entirely independently of output tokens, identifying viable steering directions and layers through intrinsic activation properties. This approach ensures robustness across diverse alignment conditions, providing a reliable mechanism for steering models toward safer behavior without the assumptions inherent in template-based detection.
