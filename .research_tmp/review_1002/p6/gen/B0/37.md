**Related Works**

The deployment of Vision Transformers (ViTs) on hardware accelerators has become a critical area of research due to their superior performance in computer vision tasks compared to traditional convolutional networks. However, the complex arithmetic operations inherent in ViTs, such as Softmax and LayerNorm, pose significant challenges for low-precision hardware implementation. These operations are sensitive to numerical precision, often leading to accuracy degradation when mapped to efficient, low-bit-width hardware units. Consequently, existing acceleration strategies frequently offload these complex operations to the CPU, resulting in substantial communication overhead between the CPU and the accelerator, which limits overall system efficiency.

Early efforts in neural network quantization focused on reducing the bit-width of weights and activations to improve inference speed and reduce memory footprint. General quantization techniques, such as those described in [1] and [2], have demonstrated that deep neural networks can be compressed with minimal accuracy loss. However, these methods often treat all layers uniformly or rely on post-training quantization that may not account for the specific hardware constraints of modern accelerators. More recent approaches have introduced hardware-aware quantization frameworks. For instance, HAQ [4] utilizes reinforcement learning to automatically determine mixed-precision quantization policies by incorporating hardware simulator feedback, thereby optimizing for latency and energy rather than just model size. While effective for general deep learning workloads, these methods do not specifically address the unique sensitivity of transformer-specific operations like Softmax and LayerNorm to low-precision arithmetic.

In the domain of attention-based models, GOBO [5] proposed a quantization technique that compresses BERT models to 3 bits without requiring fine-tuning. GOBO demonstrated that maintaining low-bit weights during computation can significantly reduce memory traffic and energy consumption on architectures like TPUs and custom FPGAs. Although GOBO targets NLP models, its insights into memory-bound inference and low-bit weight retention are relevant to ViT acceleration. However, GOBO does not fully explore the trade-offs between accuracy and hardware utilization for the specific nonlinear functions prevalent in vision transformers.

Recent work has begun to address ViT-specific hardware challenges. HeatViT [6] proposes a hardware-efficient adaptive token pruning framework for ViTs on embedded FPGAs. By dynamically identifying and consolidating non-informative tokens, HeatViT reduces computational load. Furthermore, HeatViT employs 8-bit fixed-point quantization and uses polynomial approximations for nonlinear functions to mitigate quantization error. While this approach improves hardware efficiency, it relies on fixed-point formats and pruning strategies that may not fully exploit the potential of newer, more flexible data representations for complex arithmetic operations.

The emergence of Microscaling (MX) data formats offers a promising alternative to traditional fixed-point and floating-point representations. The Block Data Representations (BDR) framework [3] introduced shared microexponents (MX), which utilize ultra-fine scaling factors to outperform state-of-the-art quantization approaches, including narrow-precision floating-point and block floating-point. MX formats have shown effectiveness in large-scale generative pretraining and inference, demonstrating that shared microexponents can maintain high accuracy while enabling efficient hardware implementation. This work builds upon these insights by applying the Microscaling Integer (MXInt) format specifically to ViT acceleration. Unlike previous methods that offload complex operations to the CPU or rely on fixed-point approximations, our approach maps all ViT operations, including Softmax and LayerNorm, onto FPGA hardware using MXInt-specific datapath designs. This eliminates CPU-accelerator communication overhead and achieves significant speedups while maintaining high accuracy, addressing the limitations of both general quantization methods and ViT-specific pruning techniques.
