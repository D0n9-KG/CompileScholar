**Related Works**

Conversational Recommender Systems (CRSs) have emerged as a pivotal paradigm for capturing dynamic and fine-grained user preferences through multi-turn natural language interactions. Unlike traditional systems that rely on static interaction histories, CRSs allow for real-time clarification of user demands. Early approaches focused on modeling the interaction process explicitly. For instance, [1] proposed an estimation-action-reflection framework to facilitate deep interaction between conversational and recommender components. Similarly, [2] introduced Conversational Path Reasoning (CPR), which models recommendation as an interactive path reasoning problem on a graph, explicitly utilizing user feedback to prune irrelevant attributes. To address the cold-start problem, [3] unified the modeling of attributes and items, enabling the system to interactively explore user preferences without relying on hand-crafted decision functions. More recently, the integration of large language models (LLMs) has transformed CRS capabilities. [4] explored knowledge-enhanced prompt learning to unify conversational and recommendation tasks, while [8] demonstrated the potential of LLMs as zero-shot conversational recommenders. Furthermore, [9] focused on unleashing the retrieval potential of LLMs to enhance recommendation accuracy, and [7] proposed demonstration-augmented prompt learning to broaden the model’s view of user preferences.

A significant challenge in developing robust CRSs is the scarcity and bias of high-quality conversational data. Existing datasets often suffer from long-tail issues, where a large proportion of items are rarely mentioned, leading to reduced recommendation diversity [6]. To mitigate this, [6] introduced LOT-CRS, a framework that simulates balanced datasets to improve long-tail recommendation performance. Additionally, data simulation techniques have been employed to enhance model robustness. [5] proposed improving CRSs via counterfactual data simulation, generating synthetic interactions to augment training data. These methods highlight the importance of synthetic data in addressing data sparsity and bias, although they primarily focus on dataset construction rather than the interactive feedback mechanism itself.

The core contribution of our work lies in the design of a generative simulated user to facilitate automatic multi-turn interaction, inspired by recent advances in reward modeling and verification. The concept of using verifiers to improve reasoning performance has been established in complex problem-solving tasks. [10] demonstrated that training verifiers to judge the correctness of model completions significantly improves performance on mathematical reasoning tasks by selecting the best candidate from multiple generations. This paradigm of reward-guided search has been further advanced by Generative Reward Models (GenRM). [11] introduced GenRM, which unifies Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) and AI Feedback (RLAIF) by training LLMs on self-generated reasoning traces, achieving superior out-of-distribution performance compared to traditional Bradley-Terry models. Similarly, [12] proposed training verifiers using a next-token prediction objective, allowing them to leverage the text generation capabilities of LLMs for better verification and integration with instruction tuning.

Our proposed GRSU framework adapts these generative reward model principles to the domain of conversational recommendation. Unlike previous works that rely on static datasets or simple bandit-based exploration [3][6], GRSU employs a simulated user that provides both coarse-grained item scoring and fine-grained attribute-based critiques. This approach mirrors the generative verification strategies in [11][12], where the model generates explicit feedback to guide the selection of optimal responses. By unifying these feedback actions into an instruction-based format, our method enables seamless integration with modern LLM architectures, addressing the trade-off between interaction effectiveness and user experience degradation.
