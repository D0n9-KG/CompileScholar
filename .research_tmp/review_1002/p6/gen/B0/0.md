**Related Works**

The design of tax policies aimed at reducing economic inequality has long been a central topic in public economics. Traditional approaches often rely on static models that assume rational, homogeneous agents. Mirrlees [4] and Diamond [5] laid the foundational theoretical groundwork for optimal income taxation, establishing the trade-offs between equity and efficiency under specific behavioral assumptions. However, these early models frequently struggle to account for the heterogeneity of taxpayer behavior and the complex, often irrational, responses to policy changes. More recent advancements in optimal taxation theory have sought to refine these models by incorporating realistic labor supply elasticities. Saez [6] demonstrated how to derive optimal income tax rates using elasticity estimates, while subsequent work by Saez [7] explored the optimal taxation of top labor incomes, highlighting the critical role of behavioral responses. Further extensions, such as the sufficient statistics approach by Chetty et al. [8], have attempted to address unemployment and wage responses, yet these frameworks often remain limited in their ability to simulate dynamic, heterogeneous agent interactions in real-time.

Parallel to theoretical developments, empirical studies have examined the impact of specific tax instruments on income distribution. The Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) has been widely analyzed for its effectiveness in reducing inequality among low-income households. Research by [1] and [2] provides evidence on the distributional effects of the EITC, confirming its role as a significant tool for poverty reduction. However, while these policies are effective within their specific scope, they do not offer a comprehensive, adaptive framework for the entire tax system. The broader case for progressive taxation, as argued by [3], emphasizes the need for policies that can dynamically adjust to changing economic conditions to maintain social stability and reduce disparities in education and healthcare.

The integration of computational methods into economic policy design represents a significant shift from static analytical models to dynamic simulations. Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) has emerged as a powerful tool for simulating complex economic systems where agents interact in heterogeneous environments. Recent surveys highlight the growing importance of ABM in economics and finance, noting its capacity to capture emergent behaviors that traditional models miss [11]. This approach allows for the simulation of diverse taxpayer behaviors, moving beyond the representative agent assumption. Furthermore, the application of machine learning in economics has opened new avenues for policy optimization. The impact of machine learning on economic research has been substantial, enabling the analysis of large-scale datasets and the discovery of non-linear relationships [10].

A notable precedent for combining AI with tax policy design is the "AI Economist" framework [9]. This study utilized deep reinforcement learning to train social planners that discover tax policies in dynamic economies, aiming to improve the trade-off between equality and productivity. By simulating competitive pressures and market dynamics, the AI Economist demonstrated that AI-driven policies could outperform baseline systems, including the Saez tax framework, by 16% in terms of the equality-productivity trade-off. This work validated the simulation environment by showing that baseline tax systems performed consistently with economic theory. However, the AI Economist primarily relied on reinforcement learning agents that learn from observational data without explicit linguistic reasoning or the nuanced behavioral modeling provided by Large Language Models (LLMs).

The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) offers a new dimension for agent-based modeling. Recent surveys indicate that LLMs are increasingly being applied in financial and economic contexts, showing promise in understanding complex textual data and simulating human-like decision-making processes [12]. Unlike traditional reinforcement learning agents, LLMs can incorporate natural language reasoning, potentially allowing for more realistic simulations of taxpayer heterogeneity and irrational behavior. This capability addresses a key limitation in previous AI-driven tax policy models, which often lacked the ability to model the diverse and sometimes non-rational motivations of individual households. By integrating LLMs with ABM, as proposed in this study, it becomes possible to create a scalable, data-driven framework that not only optimizes tax rates but also accounts for the complex, heterogeneous behaviors of real-world taxpayers, offering a more robust solution for adaptive fiscal policy evaluation.
