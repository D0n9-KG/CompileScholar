Event cameras, or dynamic vision sensors (DVS), have emerged as a powerful modality for visual perception due to their asynchronous, per-pixel operation and high temporal resolution. Unlike conventional frame-based cameras, event cameras output data only when a pixel’s brightness changes, resulting in a sparse stream of events that can capture motion with microsecond latency [1]. However, this high temporal fidelity comes at a significant cost: the resulting data streams are massive, imposing severe constraints on bandwidth, memory storage, and computational resources, particularly for edge-based applications [7]. To mitigate these challenges, a substantial body of research has focused on event downsampling, aiming to reduce the number of events while preserving the essential information required for downstream tasks.

Early approaches to event reduction often relied on fixed heuristics or simple threshold-based strategies. For instance, spatial and temporal downsampling techniques have been explored to reduce data volume for visual classification tasks, typically by aggregating events over fixed time windows or spatial grids [1]. Similarly, bio-inspired filtering algorithms have been proposed to mimic biological visual systems, reducing data by filtering out less significant changes [3]. While these methods offer a degree of data reduction, they are generally static and lack adaptability to varying scene dynamics. They do not account for the context-specific importance of events, potentially discarding critical information in complex scenes while retaining redundant data in static ones.

More recent work has sought to improve the robustness and efficiency of downsampling through more sophisticated algorithms. EvDownsampling, for example, introduces a robust method for downsampling event camera data, aiming to maintain performance across different conditions [2]. In the domain of neuromorphic computing, spatial downscaling methods have been evaluated using Spiking Neural Networks (SNNs), demonstrating that reduced data can still support effective processing in energy-efficient architectures [4]. Furthermore, insect-inspired spatio-temporal downsampling strategies have been developed to optimize input for event-based systems, drawing on biological principles to balance information retention and data reduction [5]. These approaches represent a step forward by incorporating more complex logic than simple thresholds, yet they often remain tied to specific hardware constraints or fixed processing pipelines.

The challenge of balancing event rate and task performance has been further examined in the context of deep learning. Recent studies have investigated the boundaries of event subsampling in video classification using Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs). It has been shown that the number of events per video can be reduced by an order of magnitude with minimal accuracy loss, highlighting the redundancy in raw event streams [7]. However, this research also reveals that at high subsampling rates, performance degradation is not solely due to information loss but is also influenced by training instability and increased sensitivity to hyperparameters [7]. This suggests that the method of subsampling is critical; random or uniform downsampling may fail to preserve the structural integrity of the scene, leading to suboptimal model performance.

Despite these advancements, most existing methods rely on fixed heuristics or task-specific adaptations, limiting their generalizability. There is a lack of a unified, probabilistic framework that can dynamically assess event importance in an online setting. Our work addresses this gap by proposing POLED, a probabilistic framework that models event importance through an event-importance probability density function (ePDF). Unlike prior threshold-based or heuristic-driven approaches [1][2][3][4][5][6], POLED allows for arbitrary definition and adaptation of the ePDF to different applications. By estimating event importance on-the-fly from raw event streams, our method enables scene-specific adaptation without requiring offline calibration. Furthermore, we introduce zero-shot event downsampling, ensuring that downsampled events remain compatible with models trained on the original stream, thereby eliminating the need for task-specific retraining. This approach contrasts with methods that may require specific architectural adjustments or training regimes to handle reduced data [7]. By prioritizing structurally important events through a contour-preserving ePDF, we demonstrate that intelligent, probabilistic sampling is crucial for maintaining high performance under strict event-budget constraints.
