Dense retrieval has emerged as a dominant paradigm for open-domain question answering, challenging the long-standing dominance of sparse vector space models like TF-IDF and BM25. The foundational work by Karpukhin et al. introduced Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR), demonstrating that a dual-encoder framework using BERT can learn dense representations from a small number of question-passage pairs [1]. This approach significantly outperformed strong Lucene-BM25 baselines, establishing new state-of-the-art results on multiple open-domain QA benchmarks [1]. The success of DPR relies on encoding text into sense representations that are compared via shallow similarity operations, such as the inner product, leveraging the semantic understanding acquired during the pre-training of the backbone language model [1].

To address the computational inefficiencies of cross-encoder models, which require both sentences to be fed into the network simultaneously, Sentence-BERT (SBERT) was proposed to derive semantically meaningful sentence embeddings using siamese and triplet network structures [3]. SBERT enables the comparison of embeddings using cosine similarity, reducing the inference time for finding the most similar pair in a large collection from hours to seconds while maintaining high accuracy [3]. This shift towards efficient, pre-computed embeddings paved the way for scalable dense retrieval systems. Furthermore, the quality of these embeddings is heavily influenced by the training objective. SimCSE demonstrated that simple contrastive learning frameworks, whether unsupervised (using dropout as noise) or supervised (using NLI pairs), can greatly advance the state-of-the-art for sentence embeddings [4]. SimCSE showed that the contrastive objective regularizes the anisotropic space of pre-trained embeddings to be more uniform, thereby improving retrieval performance [4].

Beyond supervised contrastive learning, efforts have been made to reduce reliance on labeled data. Contriever introduced a fully unsupervised dense retriever, utilizing a contrastive learning objective on a large corpus of web documents to learn retrieval capabilities without explicit question-passage pairs [2]. This work highlights the potential of self-supervised signals in shaping the representation space for retrieval tasks. Additionally, the architectural choice of the backbone model has been explored to enhance retrieval performance. While early dense retrievers predominantly utilized encoder-only architectures like BERT, recent research has investigated decoder-only models. RePLAMA, for instance, explored the use of decoder-based dense retrievers for open-domain QA, suggesting that the autoregressive nature of decoder models might offer distinct advantages in capturing contextual dependencies for retrieval [5]. Similarly, PromptReps investigated enhancing dense retrieval through prompt-based representations, aiming to leverage the generative capabilities of large language models to improve the quality of the encoded representations [6].

The evaluation of these dense retrieval systems typically relies on standard benchmarks that reflect real-world user queries. The MS MARCO dataset, comprising over one million anonymized questions sampled from Bing’s search query logs and millions of web passages, has become a standard benchmark for evaluating machine reading comprehension and retrieval tasks [7]. Its scale and derivation from real user queries distinguish it from other datasets, providing a robust testbed for measuring the effectiveness of dense retrievers in ranking retrieved passages given a question [7]. Another prominent benchmark is Natural Questions, which provides a large-scale dataset for question answering, allowing researchers to evaluate the end-to-end performance of retrieval and answer extraction systems [8]. These datasets, particularly MS MARCO and Natural Questions, serve as the primary evaluation grounds for comparing different representation approaches, such as CLS tokens versus mean pooling, and backbone architectures, including encoder-only BERT and decoder-only LLaMA models.
