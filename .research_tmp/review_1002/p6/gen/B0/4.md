**Related Works**

In-context learning (ICL) has emerged as a pivotal paradigm for leveraging large language models (LLMs) without explicit parameter updates. Early investigations into the mechanics of ICL revealed that the model’s reliance on ground-truth labels is less absolute than previously assumed. Min et al. demonstrated that randomly replacing labels in demonstrations often results in minimal performance degradation, suggesting that ICL primarily leverages the label space, input distribution, and format rather than strict input-label correspondence [5]. However, subsequent work by Liu et al. challenged this view, showing that the impact of correct input-label mappings is highly dependent on experimental configurations, such as prompt verbosity and model size [1]. This variability highlights the sensitivity of ICL to demonstration quality, motivating research into robust selection and calibration strategies.

A significant body of literature addresses the instability of ICL arising from label biases. Brown et al. identified that LLMs exhibit inherent biases towards certain answers, which can be mitigated through contextual calibration using content-free inputs [11]. Building on this, Zhao et al. categorized these biases into vanilla, context, and domain-label biases, proposing a calibration method that significantly improves performance by controlling for estimated label biases [10]. While these methods focus on correcting model-level biases during inference, they do not explicitly address the noise present in the demonstration data itself.

The quality of demonstrations is critical, particularly in text generation tasks where noisy annotations can severely degrade performance. Unlike text classification, where ICL has shown some robustness to noise, recent studies indicate that text generation is highly sensitive to flawed demonstrations [7]. To address this, Li et al. proposed Local Perplexity Ranking (LPR), which decomposes perplexity into inherent and matching components to identify and replace noisy candidates with cleaner neighbors [7]. This approach relies on the assumption that noisy samples yield higher local perplexities than clean ones. However, this assumption may break down in scenarios with high noise ratios, where the distinction between noisy and clean samples becomes blurred. Furthermore, existing methods often overlook the influence of domain-specific knowledge inherent in LLMs, which can introduce additional biases into perplexity estimates.

To enhance the selection of demonstrations, researchers have also explored retrieval-based approaches. Wang et al. introduced the Unified Demonstration Retriever (UDR), a single model designed to retrieve relevant examples across a wide range of tasks using a multi-task list-wise ranking framework [3]. While UDR improves the relevance of selected demonstrations, it does not explicitly account for annotation noise. Similarly, in-context vectors (ICV) have been proposed to make ICL more effective and controllable by steering the latent space of LLMs, thereby reducing the reliance on explicit demonstrations in the prompt [4]. Although ICV offers computational efficiency and better control, it does not directly address the problem of noisy labels in the source corpus.

Recent work has also focused on the specific challenge of noisy labels in ICL. Zhang et al. formalized the task of in-context learning with noisy labels, proposing methods inspired by supervised learning with noisy labels to safeguard against performance degradation [6]. These approaches typically rely on ranking or filtering mechanisms to identify clean samples. However, many of these methods, including LPR, assume that perplexity is a reliable indicator of cleanliness. Our work reexamines this paradigm, highlighting two sources of bias in perplexity: the annotation itself and the domain-specific knowledge of the LLM. By introducing a dual debiasing framework that uses synthesized neighbors to correct perplexity estimates, we aim to uncover absolute sample cleanliness regardless of the overall corpus noise level, addressing the limitations of existing perplexity-based methods.

Additionally, decoding strategies such as Contrastive Decoding have been shown to improve reasoning and generation quality by leveraging the difference between strong and weak models [2][8]. While these methods enhance the generation process, they operate at the decoding stage and do not directly address the quality of the input demonstrations. Our approach complements these techniques by ensuring that the demonstrations provided to the model are as clean as possible, thereby providing a robust foundation for subsequent decoding strategies.
