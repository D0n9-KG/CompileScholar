**Related Works**

Industrial anomaly detection (IAD) has evolved significantly with the advent of deep learning, transitioning from traditional statistical methods to sophisticated neural network architectures. Comprehensive surveys highlight the rapid development of these techniques, categorizing them by supervision levels, loss functions, and architectural choices [1]. Early approaches often relied on unsupervised methods, such as convolutional autoencoders, which reconstruct normal images and flag anomalies based on reconstruction error [3]. To improve segmentation accuracy, structural similarity metrics have been integrated into these frameworks to better capture local image dependencies [3]. More recently, semi-supervised paradigms like GANomaly have emerged, utilizing generative adversarial networks to learn the distribution of normal samples and detect deviations in latent space [4].

A major shift in recent years has been the adoption of foundation models and self-supervised learning. Vision Transformers (ViT), originally designed for natural language processing [21], have been adapted for vision tasks, demonstrating strong performance in feature extraction [22]. Self-supervised methods, such as DINO, have shown that ViTs can learn rich semantic features without labels, which are particularly effective for anomaly detection [12]. This trend continues with DINOv2, which produces robust, all-purpose visual features that generalize across diverse datasets without fine-tuning [13]. These features have been leveraged in few-shot anomaly detection; for instance, AnomalyDINO adapts DINOv2 for patch-based nearest neighbor search, achieving state-of-the-art results on benchmarks like MVTec-AD with minimal data requirements [5]. Similarly, PatchCore utilizes a memory bank of nominal patch-features to achieve high detection accuracy, addressing the cold-start problem in industrial settings [6]. To handle real-world noise, methods like SoftPatch introduce noise discriminators to refine the memory bank, ensuring robustness in practical applications [7].

Despite the high accuracy of these models, their deployment on resource-constrained embedded devices remains a significant challenge for Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs). The field of Tiny Machine Learning (TinyML) focuses on the joint design of hardware and software to enable AI on such devices [15]. To reduce model size and computational cost, various compression techniques are employed. Knowledge distillation allows a smaller "student" model to learn from a larger "teacher," effectively reducing parameters while maintaining performance [18]. Pruning methods remove redundant weights to streamline architectures [16], while quantization reduces the precision of numerical values, significantly lowering memory footprint and latency [17]. Neural Architecture Search (NAS) further automates the design of efficient models tailored to specific hardware constraints [19].

The Segment Anything Model (SAM) has introduced a new paradigm in image segmentation, offering zero-shot transfer capabilities due to its promptable design and massive training dataset [11]. However, the original SAM is computationally heavy, making it unsuitable for edge deployment. To address this, MobileSAM was developed, replacing the heavyweight ViT-H encoder with a lightweight one through decoupled distillation, resulting in a model that is over 60 times smaller while retaining comparable performance [14]. Recent work has further explored the integration of SAM with lightweight architectures for anomaly detection. For example, STLM proposes a two-stream lightweight model guided by SAM’s knowledge to enhance feature differentiation in anomalous regions, balancing efficiency and generalization [8]. Our work, KairosAD, builds upon these advancements by leveraging MobileSAM within a supervised framework, specifically optimizing for deployment on NVIDIA Jetson devices to bridge the gap between high-performance detection and practical industrial constraints.
