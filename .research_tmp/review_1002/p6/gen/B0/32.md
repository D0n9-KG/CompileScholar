**Related Works**

Research into the internal representations of neural networks has evolved significantly, moving from general similarity metrics to specific analyses of large language models (LLMs). Early foundational work introduced tools to compare representations across layers and networks. Singular Vector Canonical Correlation Analysis (SVCCA) was proposed as a fast, affine-invariant method to probe learning dynamics, revealing that networks converge to final representations from the bottom up [2]. Subsequent studies refined these comparison techniques, noting that Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) suffers from limitations when comparing high-dimensional representations with limited data points [3]. To address this, Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) was introduced as a robust similarity index that reliably identifies correspondences between representations, even across different initializations [3]. Further investigations using projection-weighted CCA demonstrated that networks which generalize converge to more similar representations than those that memorize, highlighting the link between representation dynamics and generalization performance [1].

In the context of LLMs, recent studies have utilized these similarity metrics to understand layer-specific functions and representation evolution. Research has shown that fine-tuned transformers exhibit clusters of similar representations across layers, with a block diagonal structure indicating that later layers contribute marginally to task performance [5]. This finding suggests that the internal mechanics of LLMs are not uniform, and that specific layers play distinct roles. Other work has explored how representation dissimilarity measures can reveal insights into model generalization and the effects of architectural choices, such as activation functions, on internal representations [6]. Additionally, studies on the quality of intermediate representations have found that layers prior to the final output often yield more informative features for downstream tasks, challenging the assumption that final layers are always optimal [12].

Beyond similarity analysis, several studies have identified specific layers as critical for particular functions, such as factual recall and knowledge storage. Causal intervention techniques have revealed that mid-layer feed-forward modules are decisive in factual predictions, enabling direct editing of factual associations [7]. Similarly, research on knowledge injection has demonstrated that shallow layers hold the key to integrating new knowledge, with performance degrading significantly when these layers are removed or modified [9]. The concept of "Concept Depth" further suggests that LLMs process concepts of varying complexities in different layers, with simpler tasks handled in shallow layers and more complex inferential tasks requiring deeper layers [11]. These data-dependent analyses typically rely on fine-tuned models or specific probing tasks, limiting their applicability to post-hoc settings.

Our work builds upon these foundations by introducing a data-oblivious approach to identify intrinsic critical layers in pre-fine-tuned LLMs. While previous methods often require task-specific data to identify critical layers [5][7][9][11], we analyze representation dynamics via CKA to detect layers with significant shifts in representation space. Our spectral analysis reveals that these shifts are driven by changes in top principal components, encoding semantic transitions. This approach allows us to identify critical layers without relying on downstream task performance, offering a more generalizable framework for understanding LLM interpretability and robustness. By linking these intrinsic critical layers to practical applications such as efficient domain adaptation and backdoor defense, we extend the utility of representation analysis beyond post-hoc interpretation to proactive model optimization and security.
