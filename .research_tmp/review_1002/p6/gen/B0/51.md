Federated Online Learning to Rank (FOLTR) has emerged as a critical paradigm for balancing search effectiveness with user privacy. By training ranking models collaboratively across decentralized data sources, FOLTR preserves individual user data while optimizing search results based on implicit feedback, such as clicks [1][3]. Early approaches utilized evolution strategies to facilitate this distributed learning process [1][2]. To address the limitations of linear models in capturing complex query-document relationships, subsequent research introduced differentiable pairwise losses, such as Pairwise Differentiable Gradient Descent (PDGD), which allows for the optimization of non-linear models like neural networks [4]. This was further advanced by methods that directly learn neural ranking models from implicit user feedback, managing the explore-exploit trade-off to achieve strong regret bounds [7]. However, the efficacy of these systems is often challenged by data heterogeneity. Recent studies have highlighted that non-IID data distributions across clients can significantly impact the performance of state-of-the-art FOLTR approaches, posing challenges for global model convergence [5]. Furthermore, the security of these federated systems is not guaranteed; they remain susceptible to poisoning attacks that aim to compromise search effectiveness, necessitating robust defense mechanisms [6][15].

As data protection legislation, such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), establishes the "right to be forgotten," the ability to remove user contributions from trained models has become a legal and ethical imperative [10][11]. This has spurred the development of machine unlearning techniques. In centralized settings, unlearning typically requires access to all training data for retraining, a constraint that does not apply to federated learning where data remains local [10]. Consequently, federated unlearning methods have been proposed to erase client contributions without full data access. One approach involves rapid retraining, where data holders jointly conduct the unlearning process efficiently while keeping their training data local, preserving model utility with high efficiency [10]. Another strategy, known as Federated Unlearning (FU), performs local unlearning at the client to be erased and uses the resulting model as an initialization for a few rounds of federated learning with remaining clients, achieving performance comparable to retraining from scratch at a fraction of the cost [11].

Specific to the domain of ranking and recommendation, recent works have tailored unlearning strategies to the unique dynamics of online learning. Federaser enables efficient client-level data removal from federated learning models, providing a foundation for privacy-preserving updates [8]. In the context of on-device recommendations, Federated Recommendation Unlearning (FRU) utilizes a log-based rollback mechanism to remove user contributions by rolling back and calibrating historical parameter updates, thereby enhancing robustness against malicious clients [12]. For knowledge graph embeddings, FedLU addresses data heterogeneity and knowledge forgetting by combining mutual knowledge distillation with an unlearning method inspired by cognitive neuroscience, specifically retroactive interference and passive decay [13]. Additionally, knowledge distillation has been employed in federated unlearning to restore model performance after subtracting accumulated historical updates, a method that does not rely on client participation and is applicable to various neural network architectures [9].

Despite these advancements, the application of unlearning to FOLTR remains underexplored. A recent study specifically addresses how to forget clients in FOLTR, proposing an effective and efficient unlearning method that removes a client's contribution without compromising overall ranker effectiveness or requiring full retraining [14]. However, a significant gap persists in the rigorous evaluation of these strategies. Current evaluation practices are often controversial, and previous proposals of unlearning methods have predominantly relied on single evaluation metrics, which may not capture the nuances of under-unlearning or over-unlearning [14]. This paper addresses this limitation by systematically analyzing the unlearning capabilities of five strategies within FOLTR. By employing adapted and newly proposed metrics, we provide a comprehensive assessment of both the effectiveness and efficiency of these methods, offering insights into balancing data privacy and system performance in a verifiable manner.
