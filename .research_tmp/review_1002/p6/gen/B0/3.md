Controllable Text-to-Speech (TTS) synthesis has evolved significantly from early statistical parametric approaches to modern neural architectures. Early efforts in expressive prosody focused on unit-selection methods to enhance naturalness [1]. The advent of neural end-to-end models, such as Tacotron, improved quality but introduced challenges regarding inference speed and controllability [2]. To address these issues, non-autoregressive models like FastSpeech and FastSpeech 2 were developed, offering faster synthesis and improved robustness by directly predicting mel-spectrograms in parallel [2][3]. While these models achieve high quality, they often lack fine-grained control over specific prosodic attributes without additional architectural modifications or specialized training pipelines.

A major line of research has focused on introducing explicit control mechanisms into TTS models. One prominent approach involves the use of latent style embeddings. For instance, Global Style Tokens (GSTs) were introduced to model acoustic expressiveness without explicit labels, allowing for control over speaking style and speed [6]. Similarly, hierarchical generative models have been proposed to disentangle latent attributes such as accent, noise, and speaking rate, enabling fine-grained control through a variational autoencoder framework [8]. Another significant direction is prosody transfer, where models learn a latent embedding space from reference audio to replicate specific prosodic characteristics onto new text [7]. These methods, while effective, typically require the model to be trained or fine-tuned with specific conditioning inputs, limiting their flexibility for post-hoc adjustments on pre-trained models.

More recent work has explored controlling specific prosodic features like speaking rate. Some approaches condition the duration predictor on speaking rate to achieve implicit control, often requiring fine-tuning of a pre-trained baseline [5]. Other methods have investigated direct manipulation of internal representations, such as the encoder’s output embeddings, to adjust speaking rate without retraining the entire model [9]. Additionally, temporal control of prosodic variation has been studied to allow for more dynamic adjustments during synthesis [4]. However, these techniques often remain specialized to particular attributes or require specific architectural support, and they generally do not address the broader issue of generalizable, model-agnostic editing of internal states for multiple prosodic dimensions simultaneously.

In parallel, the challenge of mispronunciation correction in TTS systems has been addressed primarily through grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) dictionaries. Traditional systems rely heavily on these dictionaries to map text to phonemes, which can be impractical in low-resource settings or for out-of-vocabulary words. Recent advancements have explored using speech from non-target speakers to correct mispronunciations in grapheme-input TTS, aiming to improve accuracy without extensive manual annotation [10]. Despite these improvements, most existing correction methods are tightly coupled with the G2P pipeline and do not offer a unified framework for both prosody control and pronunciation correction at inference time.

Our work, Counterfactual Activation Editing, distinguishes itself by proposing a model-agnostic method that manipulates internal representations of pre-trained TTS models. Unlike previous approaches that rely on specialized modules, additional training, or G2P dictionaries [2][3][5][6][7][8][9][10], our method enables post-hoc control of prosody and correction of mispronunciations without retraining. This approach bridges the gap between static pre-trained models and editable speech synthesis, offering a flexible solution for inference-time refinement.
