Generative retrieval (GR) represents a paradigm shift in information retrieval, replacing traditional index-retrieve pipelines with a single neural network that directly generates document identifiers [1]. This approach was initially introduced by the Differentiable Search Index (DSI), which demonstrated that a Transformer can encode corpus information in its parameters to map queries to document IDs [14]. Subsequent works have explored various strategies for defining these identifiers, ranging from atomic IDs [15] to hierarchical semantic identifiers [4][9]. Recent advancements include learning optimal tokenization for docids [4], using nonparametric decoding to enhance memory capacity [21], and integrating generative retrieval with large language models for unified retrieval and question answering [33][34]. Furthermore, efforts have been made to scale GR to large collections [16] and enable incremental updates for dynamic corpora [18][23][24].

A significant body of literature has focused on the theoretical and empirical limitations of GR, particularly regarding its generalization capabilities. While early studies highlighted the potential of GR, recent work has scrutinized its robustness against out-of-distribution (OOD) data, noting that GR models often struggle with query variations and unseen tasks compared to dense retrieval baselines [19][20]. The reliance on memorizing document identifiers within model parameters has been identified as a source of brittleness, leading to poor memory accuracy and confusion as corpus size increases [22]. To address the disconnect between the autoregressive learning objective and the final ranking target, frameworks such as LTRGR have been proposed to bridge this gap via rank loss [6]. Additionally, the relationship between GR and dense retrieval has been formalized, showing that GR can be viewed as a special case of multi-vector dense retrieval [12] or bi-encoder based dense retrieval [15], which helps explain its scaling behaviors and potential for hybrid approaches [22].

The decoding process in GR, specifically the use of constrained autoregressive generation and beam search, remains a critical area of investigation. Constrained decoding is necessary to ensure that generated identifiers correspond to valid documents in the corpus. However, the interaction between the model’s learned distribution and these hard constraints is not fully understood. In broader autoregressive generation, imposing constraints at inference time can alter the model’s generalization properties [26], and achieving guaranteed constraint satisfaction often requires combining training-time and inference-time methods [27]. Tractable probabilistic models have been used to guide constrained generation in language models [28], offering insights into how constraints affect the output distribution. In the context of retrieval, beam search is the standard inference algorithm, yet it introduces a training-testing discrepancy. Theoretical analyses of tree-based models under beam search have shown that even optimally trained scorers may fail to retrieve the most relevant targets due to the greedy nature of beam search [30]. This discrepancy is particularly relevant to GR, where the hierarchical structure of identifiers often mirrors tree-based indices [29][31]. Recent work has also explored alternative decoding strategies, such as simultaneous decoding to guide autoregressive generation [32], and the use of nonparametric embeddings to mitigate the limitations of fixed parametric memory [21].

Our work builds upon these foundations by providing a theoretical analysis of the inherent limitations of constrained autoregressive generation in GR. Unlike prior empirical studies on OOD robustness [19][20] or theoretical analyses of tree models [30], we derive lower bounds on the error introduced by corpus-specific constraints and examine the suboptimality of using marginal distributions in beam search. This analysis complements existing work on guaranteed generation [27] and constrained decoding [28] by specifically addressing the retrieval setting, where the "constraints" are defined by the finite set of valid document identifiers. By formalizing the impact of constraints and beam search on the Bayes-optimal setting, we aim to clarify the theoretical boundaries of the GR paradigm and inspire more robust decoding algorithms.
