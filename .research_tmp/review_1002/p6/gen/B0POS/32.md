**Related Works**

Research into the evolution of neural network representations has traditionally relied on canonical correlation analysis (CCA) and its variants to compare internal states across layers and models. Early work introduced Singular Vector CCA (SVCCA) to measure representation similarity in a way that is invariant to affine transformations, revealing that networks converge to final representations from the bottom up [2]. Subsequent studies refined these methods, noting that standard CCA can be unreliable for high-dimensional representations with limited data points [3]. To address this, Centered Kernel Alignment (CKA) was proposed as a robust similarity index that reliably identifies correspondences between representations trained from different initializations [3]. Further investigations using CKA and related metrics have explored how representation similarity varies with network width and depth, as well as the dynamics of representational similarity in recurrent and convolutional architectures [1][4]. While these studies establish foundational tools for comparing static or training-time representations, the present paper introduces a data-oblivious approach to identify intrinsic critical layers in pre-fine-tuned LLMs, moving beyond post-hoc, data-dependent analyses of fine-tuned models.

In the context of Large Language Models (LLMs), recent studies have utilized representation similarity to understand layer-specific functions and fine-tuning dynamics. For instance, analyses of fine-tuned Transformers have revealed block diagonal structures in representation similarity, suggesting that later layers contribute marginally to task performance and can sometimes be discarded [5]. Other works have employed representation dissimilarity measures to uncover insights into model generalization, activation function asymmetries, and the impact of architectural scaling on internal features [6]. Additionally, research has examined the quality of intermediate representations, finding that layers often yield more informative features for downstream tasks than final layers, with patterns in entropy and curvature varying across architectures [12]. These studies typically rely on data-dependent probing or analysis of models after fine-tuning. In contrast, our work identifies critical layers in pre-fine-tuned LLMs by analyzing representation dynamics via CKA, demonstrating that layers with significant shifts in representation space are consistently the most affected during fine-tuning across various tasks.

A parallel line of research focuses on identifying specific layers responsible for factual knowledge, truthfulness, and complex concept processing. Causal intervention studies have localized factual associations to middle-layer feed-forward modules, enabling direct editing of these computations [7]. Similarly, internal states of LLMs have been shown to encode truthfulness, allowing classifiers to distinguish between true and false statements based on hidden layer activations [8]. More recently, the concept of "Concept Depth" has been introduced, suggesting that simpler concepts are processed in shallow layers while more complex, inferential tasks require deeper layers [11]. Furthermore, studies on knowledge injection have found that shallow layers are particularly crucial for integrating new domain-specific knowledge, leading to strategies that selectively enhance these layers [9]. While these works highlight the functional importance of specific layers for knowledge storage and processing, they often depend on task-specific probing or post-hoc identification. Our approach differs by providing a data-oblivious method to identify intrinsic critical layers based on spectral analysis of representation shifts, revealing that these layers encode semantic transitions from rationales to conclusions.

Finally, the practical implications of layer-specific dynamics have been explored in the context of model robustness and defense mechanisms. Recent efforts have investigated layer-specific editing to defend LLMs against jailbreak attacks, suggesting that modifying specific layers can mitigate adversarial vulnerabilities [10]. This aligns with our findings that critical layers are highly sensitive to changes during fine-tuning. By identifying these layers in a data-oblivious manner, we apply these insights to two practical scenarios: efficient domain adaptation, where fine-tuning critical layers yields greater loss reduction, and backdoor defense, where freezing these layers significantly reduces attack success rates. Unlike previous works that rely on task-specific data to identify vulnerable or important layers, our method offers a generalizable framework for enhancing LLM interpretability and robustness prior to fine-tuning.
