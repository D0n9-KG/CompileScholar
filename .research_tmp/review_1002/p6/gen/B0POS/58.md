**Related Works**

Conversational Recommender Systems (CRSs) have evolved significantly to address the limitations of static recommendation methods, which often struggle to capture dynamic and fine-grained user preferences [1]. Early approaches focused on modeling the interaction between the system and the user as a path reasoning problem on knowledge graphs, where the system explicitly navigates attribute vertices based on user feedback to prune irrelevant candidates [2]. To handle the cold-start problem, subsequent works integrated attribute inquiry with item recommendation, aiming to unify these actions to better balance exploration and exploitation for new users [3]. More recent advancements have leveraged large language models (LLMs) to enhance CRS capabilities, employing knowledge-enhanced prompt learning to unify attributes and items within a single framework [4]. Other studies have utilized demonstration-augmented prompt learning to broaden the model's perspective and improve recommendation accuracy [7]. Furthermore, LLMs have been explored as zero-shot conversational recommenders, demonstrating strong potential without extensive task-specific training [8], while others have focused on unleashing the retrieval potential of LLMs to improve the grounding of recommendations in external knowledge [9]. Our work builds on these foundations by addressing the specific challenge of capturing intricate user preferences through multi-turn interaction, moving beyond static prompt engineering to an active, feedback-driven interaction paradigm.

A critical bottleneck in CRS research is the reliance on human-in-the-loop data collection, which is costly and slow. To mitigate this, researchers have proposed simulating user interactions to generate synthetic data. For instance, counterfactual data simulation has been used to improve CRS robustness by generating diverse conversation scenarios [5]. Similarly, efforts to alleviate the long-tail problem have involved simulating balanced datasets to ensure that rare items are adequately represented in training, thereby improving recommendation diversity [6]. These methods primarily focus on data augmentation to improve model training. In contrast, our approach introduces a generative reward model-based simulated user, GRSU, which is not merely a data generator but an active agent that provides real-time feedback during the interaction process, enabling the CRS to dynamically refine its understanding of user preferences.

The design of our simulated user is inspired by recent advances in reward modeling and verification for LLMs. Traditional reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) is resource-intensive, leading to the development of Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF) and hybrid approaches like GenRM, which train LLMs on self-generated reasoning traces to produce synthetic preference labels that align with human judgments [11]. Generative verifiers, which use next-token prediction for reward modeling, have shown superior performance in ranking candidate solutions compared to discriminative classifiers, particularly in complex reasoning tasks [12]. This verification paradigm has roots in earlier work on training verifiers to solve math word problems, where generating multiple candidates and selecting the best one via a verifier significantly improved performance [10]. While these methods focus on selecting the best output from a set of candidates in reasoning tasks, we adapt this generative reward modeling paradigm to the conversational recommendation domain. We design two types of feedback actions—generative item scoring and attribute-based item critique—and unify them into an instruction-based format, allowing for a seamless integration of coarse and fine-grained feedback.

Finally, the efficiency of the interaction process is crucial for practical deployment. We draw inspiration from reward-guided search paradigms used in complex reasoning, where beam search is employed to navigate the space of potential interactions efficiently. By combining our unified simulated user with beam search and an efficient candidate ranking method, we strike a balance between the effectiveness of deep preference capture and the computational efficiency required for real-time systems. This distinguishes our work from previous simulation-based approaches that often lack a structured search strategy for optimizing the multi-turn dialogue trajectory.
