**Related Works**

Industrial anomaly detection (IAD) has evolved significantly with the advent of deep learning, shifting from traditional statistical methods to sophisticated neural network architectures. Comprehensive surveys highlight the diversity of approaches, ranging from unsupervised autoencoders to supervised deep learning models, while emphasizing the critical need for robust evaluation metrics and datasets [1]. Early unsupervised methods often relied on reconstruction errors, such as convolutional autoencoders that threshold pixel-wise differences to identify defects [3]. To address the challenge of limited anomaly samples, semi-supervised approaches like GANomaly utilized generative adversarial networks to learn the distribution of normal data, enabling the detection of unseen anomalies [4]. More recently, memory bank-based methods have gained prominence; for instance, PatchCore leverages maximally representative patch-features from nominal images to achieve state-of-the-art performance on benchmarks like MVTec-AD [6]. Similarly, SoftPatch addresses the issue of noisy training data by employing noise discriminators to refine the memory bank, ensuring robustness in real-world scenarios [7]. While these methods offer high accuracy, they often rely on large, computationally heavy backbones that are impractical for edge deployment. In contrast, our work, KairosAD, focuses on a supervised approach that prioritizes model efficiency for embedded devices, bridging the gap between high-accuracy detection and resource-constrained deployment.

The integration of foundation models has recently transformed the landscape of visual anomaly detection. The Segment Anything Model (SAM) introduced a promptable segmentation framework that demonstrates impressive zero-shot generalization across diverse image distributions [11]. However, the original SAM is too large for mobile or embedded applications. To address this, MobileSAM was developed, replacing the heavyweight image encoder with a lightweight one through decoupled distillation, resulting in a model that is over 60 times smaller while maintaining comparable performance [14]. This line of work is crucial for our research, as it enables the use of powerful segmentation capabilities on resource-limited hardware. Furthermore, recent studies have explored the utility of self-supervised features from models like DINOv2 for few-shot anomaly detection, showing that high-quality visual features alone can rival vision-language models [5][13]. These foundation models provide robust feature representations that are essential for generalizing to unseen defect types. KairosAD builds on this progress by specifically leveraging MobileSAM, a lightweight variant of SAM, to achieve efficient and accurate anomaly segmentation without the computational overhead of the original foundation models.

Efficient deployment on edge devices remains a critical challenge in industrial AI. The field of Tiny Machine Learning (TinyML) emphasizes the joint design of hardware and software to enable AI on resource-constrained IoT devices [15]. Various model compression techniques, including pruning, quantization, and knowledge distillation, have been proposed to reduce model size and inference latency [16][17][18]. Pruning methods remove redundant weights, while quantization reduces numerical precision to save memory and speed up computation [16][17]. Knowledge distillation, in particular, allows a smaller "student" model to learn from a larger "teacher," effectively compressing complex architectures for mobile deployment [18]. Additionally, the Transformer architecture, originally designed for NLP, has been adapted for vision tasks, offering parallel processing capabilities and strong long-range dependency modeling [21][22]. However, standard Transformers and large CNNs often exceed the memory and power budgets of embedded systems like the NVIDIA Jetson series. Our approach differs by integrating these efficiency principles directly into the model architecture via MobileSAM, ensuring that KairosAD can be deployed on specific embedded hardware (Jetson NX and AGX) with significantly reduced parameters and faster inference times compared to state-of-the-art models.
