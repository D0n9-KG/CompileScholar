Fairness in recommender systems has emerged as a critical area of research, driven by the need to mitigate bias and ensure equitable outcomes for both users and item providers [1][2]. Early efforts focused on defining fairness metrics and integrating them into ranking objectives, often treating user satisfaction and item exposure as separate concerns [6][7]. Subsequent work recognized the two-sided nature of these platforms, where optimizing for one side can inadvertently harm the other [5][14]. For instance, FairRec proposed mapping fair recommendation to the allocation of indivisible goods to guarantee fairness for both producers and customers [5][14]. Similarly, TFROM and CPFair developed models that jointly optimize for consumer and producer fairness, demonstrating that balancing these interests is essential for sustainable ecosystems [12][13]. While these studies established the importance of two-sided fairness, they largely relied on static fairness constraints or specific allocation principles without deeply analyzing the dynamic trade-off mechanisms between accuracy and fairness. In contrast, this paper draws an analogy to economic tax transfer processes to formalize this trade-off, providing a mechanistic understanding of how item-side fairness constraints translate into user-side accuracy loss.

A parallel line of research has explored the economic underpinnings of fair re-ranking, particularly through the lens of taxation and redistribution. The concept of fair re-ranking as a taxation process has been used to conceptualize the redistribution of ranking slots, offering insights into how resources are allocated among items [8]. Tax-rank, for example, levies taxes based on utility differences to achieve controllability over accuracy loss, addressing limitations in previous item-level tax policies [8]. Other approaches have utilized inequality measurement tools from economics, such as generalized Gini welfare functions, to specify normative criteria for fairness in rankings [11]. These methods aim to minimize exposure inequality or focus on the least satisfied users, leveraging non-smooth optimization techniques to handle non-differentiable objectives [11]. Additionally, VerFair proposed a vertical allocation-based approach to amortize fair exposure, arguing that existing methods fail to fully utilize consumer prior knowledge for optimal fairness-relevance trade-offs [10]. Although these works introduce economic concepts to guide re-ranking, they often focus on specific tax structures or inequality indices without a unified framework for evaluating the sensitivity of the accuracy-fairness trade-off. This work introduces the concept of elasticity to quantify the extent of this trade-off, moving beyond static tax rates to a dynamic measure of utility sensitivity between item groups.

Recent efforts have also focused on standardizing the evaluation of fair and diverse algorithms to address inconsistencies in experimental setups. Toolkits like FairDiverse have been developed to provide a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating fairness and diversity across various information retrieval tasks, supporting multiple algorithms and base models [3]. Similarly, policy learning frameworks have been proposed to optimize ranking utilities while satisfying exposure fairness constraints, expanding the class of learnable ranking functions to stochastic policies [9]. These frameworks provide rigorous languages for expressing fairness specifications and enable the search for fair ranking policies via policy-gradient approaches [9]. However, current evaluation practices often rely on single fairness metrics, which can hinder a comprehensive assessment of algorithm performance across different operating points [3]. By introducing the Elastic Fairness Curve (EF-Curve), this paper offers a comparative analysis framework that evaluates algorithm performance across varying elasticity levels, facilitating the selection of the most suitable approach for specific fairness-accuracy requirements.
