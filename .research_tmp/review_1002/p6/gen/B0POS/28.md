**Related Works**

Active learning (AL) aims to minimize annotation costs by strategically selecting the most informative samples from an unlabeled pool. Early approaches relied on uncertainty sampling, where models query instances with the highest prediction uncertainty [1][2]. Subsequent research expanded this paradigm to specific domains, such as image classification, where various uncertainty metrics are compared to identify high-value examples for convolutional neural networks (CNNs) [3]. Comprehensive surveys have further categorized these strategies, highlighting the trade-offs between query efficiency and model performance [4]. More recent methods have introduced sophisticated mechanisms to handle the computational demands of deep learning. For instance, some approaches utilize a loss prediction module to estimate which unlabeled inputs are likely to yield incorrect predictions, thereby improving selection efficiency [11]. Others frame active learning as a core-set selection problem, aiming to choose a subset of points that ensures the learned model remains competitive for the remaining data [10]. While these methods significantly reduce annotation burdens in centralized settings, they typically assume a single, homogeneous data source. In contrast, our work addresses the limitations of individual AL by operating within a federated framework, where data diversity is constrained by distributed clients and limited annotation budgets.

To better capture model uncertainty, several studies have integrated Bayesian principles into deep learning. Bayes by Backprop, for example, learns a probability distribution over network weights to regularize training and improve generalization [5]. Similarly, dropout has been reinterpreted as an approximate Bayesian inference method, allowing for the estimation of model uncertainty without prohibitive computational costs [6]. These techniques provide a mathematical grounding for quantifying epistemic uncertainty, which is crucial for identifying samples near decision boundaries. However, most of these uncertainty estimation methods are designed for centralized training and do not account for the distributed nature of federated systems. Our proposed CHASe builds upon these insights by specifically tracking epistemic variations (EVs) through inference inconsistencies across training epochs, tailored to the dynamic parameter fluctuations inherent in federated settings.

Federated Learning (FL) enables collaborative model training across decentralized clients while preserving data privacy. Foundational work introduced federated optimization to handle uneven data distributions across a large number of nodes [13]. The FedAvg algorithm further demonstrated that iterative model averaging is robust to non-IID data distributions, significantly reducing communication costs [14]. Recent research has investigated the robustness of FL to data heterogeneity, showing that local training iterations and communication frequency play critical roles in mitigating the effects of biased sampling [19]. Additionally, distribution-regularized approaches have been proposed to address non-IID data challenges [18]. While these studies focus on the optimization and aggregation of models, they generally assume that local data is fully labeled or do not explicitly address the strategic selection of unlabeled data. Our work differs by integrating active learning into the FL pipeline, specifically targeting the heterogeneity of data distribution across clients to improve global model accuracy.

The intersection of Active Learning and Federated Learning, known as Federated Active Learning (FAL), has emerged as a promising area of research. Early efforts applied AL to FL for specific tasks like waste and disaster image classification [15] or emergency response systems [16]. More recent frameworks propose collaborative data selection strategies to reduce annotation workload. For example, F-AL implements AL in a distributed optimization manner to select informative instances for the global model [17]. Other approaches, such as Knowledge-Aware Federated Active Learning (KAFAL), address the mismatch between global and local sampling goals by leveraging discrepancies between local and global models [20]. Furthermore, LoGo integrates global and local selector models to handle varying levels of local heterogeneity and global imbalance [21]. Active Federated Learning has also been explored to select clients based on their data value to maximize training efficiency [22]. Despite these advances, existing FAL methods often fail to fully account for the fluctuations in global and local model parameters caused by data heterogeneity. CHASe distinguishes itself by focusing on samples with high epistemic variations that oscillate around decision boundaries, employing a data freeze and awaken mechanism to enhance both effectiveness and efficiency in heterogeneous federation settings.
