**Related Works**

Long-tailed recognition addresses the challenge of class imbalance, where a few head classes dominate the data distribution while tail classes are under-represented [1]. Early approaches focused on re-balancing strategies, such as re-sampling and re-weighting, to mitigate the bias toward majority classes [1][2]. Class-balanced loss methods, for instance, adjust the loss based on the effective number of samples to account for diminishing returns in data utility [2]. Other techniques include cost-sensitive learning, which jointly optimizes class-dependent costs and network parameters to learn robust features for both majority and minority classes [4]. More recent surveys categorize these efforts into class re-balancing, information augmentation, and module improvement [7]. While these methods effectively address imbalance at the data or loss level, our paper introduces Aligned Contrastive Learning (ACL) to specifically target the gradient conflicts inherent in contrastive optimization, rather than relying solely on re-weighting or sampling adjustments.

A significant line of research has explored the decoupling of representation learning from classifier training. It has been shown that data imbalance may not severely impact the quality of learned representations if the classifier is appropriately adjusted [5]. Bilateral-branch networks further separate these tasks, using one branch for representation and another for classification to prevent re-balancing strategies from damaging feature quality [6]. Subsequent works have refined this decoupling by using stochastic weight averaging to improve generalization of feature extractors and re-training classifiers with stochastic representations [8]. Logit adjustment techniques also modify the decision boundaries to encourage larger margins for rare labels [9]. In contrast to these decoupled approaches, our ACL algorithm operates within a unified contrastive framework, eliminating the need for separate classifier re-training by directly resolving the imbalanced attraction and repulsion gradients during the representation learning phase.

Contrastive learning has emerged as a powerful paradigm for representation learning, with foundational works like SimCLR, MoCo, and BYOL demonstrating its efficacy in self-supervised settings [14][15][16]. Supervised Contrastive Learning (SCL) extends this to the supervised domain, pulling same-class samples together and pushing different-class samples apart [17]. However, standard SCL struggles with long-tailed distributions because it fails to form a regular simplex in the feature space, leading to poor uniformity and separability for minority classes [12][21]. To address this, various adaptations have been proposed. Parametric Contrastive Learning (PaCo) introduces learnable class-wise centers to rebalance optimization [10], while Generalized PaCo extends this to both balanced and imbalanced data [11]. Probabilistic Contrastive Learning (ProCo) estimates class distributions to sample contrastive pairs more effectively [18]. Subclass-balancing contrastive learning clusters head classes into subclasses to preserve semantic structure [19]. Furthermore, mutual information maximization between latent features and labels has been used to provide a theoretical grounding for combining contrastive learning with logit adjustment [13]. Our work builds on these insights by providing a theoretical gradient analysis of SCL that identifies specific gradient conflicts. Unlike previous methods that adjust loss functions or sampling strategies, our ACL algorithm is explicitly designed to eliminate these identified gradient imbalances, ensuring consistent performance enhancement as the number of views increases.
