**Related Works**

The foundation of modern text representation lies in distributed vector models. Early approaches, such as Word2Vec [1] and GloVe [2], established the paradigm of learning static word embeddings that capture syntactic and semantic relationships. These methods, however, were limited by their inability to handle context-dependent meanings and phrase-level compositionality. The advent of the Transformer architecture [3] revolutionized natural language processing by enabling parallelizable, attention-based sequence modeling. This led to the development of pre-trained language models like BERT [4], which introduced bidirectional context encoding. While BERT achieved state-of-the-art results on various NLP tasks, its original design was not optimized for generating independent sentence embeddings, as it required processing sentence pairs jointly, resulting in high computational costs for similarity search. To address this, Sentence-BERT (SBERT) [6] adapted BERT using siamese network structures to produce semantically meaningful sentence embeddings that could be compared via cosine similarity, drastically reducing inference time. Subsequent works further refined these embeddings through contrastive learning. SimCSE [7] demonstrated that simple contrastive objectives, using dropout as noise, could significantly improve embedding quality by regularizing the anisotropic space. More recent advancements include WhitenedCSE [8], which employs whitening-based contrastive learning, and AoE [11], which optimizes embedding angles for semantic textual similarity. Furthermore, the integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) has opened new avenues for embedding generation. For instance, one approach leverages LLMs to generate synthetic data for fine-tuning open-source models, achieving strong performance on benchmarks like BEIR and MTEB without relying on manually collected labeled data [9]. In contrast, our work, GSTransform, does not rely on re-encoding the entire corpus with a new model for each instruction; instead, it utilizes a lightweight transformation mechanism to adapt pre-computed generic embeddings in real time, thereby avoiding the significant computational overhead associated with these re-encoding strategies.

A distinct line of research focuses on instruction-following text embeddings, where the embedding is conditioned on user-provided instructions to highlight specific attributes of the text. INSTRUCTOR [12] introduced a method for computing text embeddings given task instructions, allowing a single embedder to generate tailored representations for diverse downstream tasks without further training. This approach demonstrated robustness to instruction changes and achieved state-of-the-art performance across 70 evaluation tasks. Building on this, InBedder [13] proposed a novel viewpoint that treats instructions as questions about the input text, encoding the expected answers to obtain the representation. This "embed-via-answering" strategy significantly improved instruction-following capabilities and interpretability when applied to both large language models and smaller encoder-based models. Additionally, Promptriever [14] presented the first retrieval model that can be prompted like a language model, demonstrating strong performance in following detailed relevance instructions and increasing robustness to lexical choices in queries. These methods, while effective, typically require the model to process the instruction and text jointly during inference, which can be computationally intensive when applied to large-scale corpora for multiple instructions. Our proposed GSTransform framework differs by observing that instruction-relevant information is inherently encoded in generic embeddings but remains underutilized. Rather than re-encoding the corpus for each new instruction, GSTransform applies a guided space transformation to pre-computed embeddings, achieving dramatic speedups of 6~300x in real-time processing while maintaining high instruction-following quality.

Another emerging direction is the unification of generative and representational tasks within a single model. GRIT [10] introduced generative representational instruction tuning, training a large language model to handle both generation and embedding tasks by distinguishing between them through instructions. This unification allows models like GritLM to set new state-of-the-art results on the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark (MTEB) while also excelling at generative tasks, and it speeds up Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) by eliminating the need for separate retrieval and generation models. While GRIT demonstrates the power of unified instruction tuning, it still relies on the underlying LLM to generate embeddings, which can be resource-intensive. In contrast, GSTransform focuses specifically on the efficiency of adapting existing embeddings. By leveraging a small amount of text data with instruction-focused label annotation to guide the transformation, GSTransform provides a lightweight solution that enhances instruction-following text embedding quality over state-of-the-art methods without the heavy computational burden of full model inference for every query-instruction pair.
