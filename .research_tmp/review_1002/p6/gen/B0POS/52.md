Dense passage retrieval has emerged as a dominant paradigm for open-domain question answering, shifting away from traditional sparse vector space models like TF-IDF or BM25. Karpukhin et al. introduced Dense Passage Retrieval (DPR), demonstrating that a dual-encoder framework using dense representations can significantly outperform strong sparse baselines by 9%-19% in top-20 passage retrieval accuracy [1]. This approach relies on pre-trained backbone language models, such as BERT, which are fine-tuned via contrastive learning to encode text into semantic representations that are compared using shallow similarity operations like inner products. While DPR established the efficacy of this architecture, it primarily focused on encoder-only models and specific datasets. Our work extends this foundational line of research by investigating whether the reliance on pre-trained knowledge holds across different backbone architectures, specifically comparing encoder-only BERT with decoder-only LLaMA models, and by evaluating performance on additional datasets such as MS MARCO [7] and Natural Questions [8].

The mechanism by which dense retrievers acquire knowledge has been a subject of significant debate, particularly regarding the relative contributions of pre-training versus fine-tuning. Recent analyses have argued that retrieval knowledge is primarily gained during the pre-training phase, suggesting that fine-tuning merely adjusts neuron activations rather than reorganizing fundamental knowledge structures. This perspective was largely established in the context of BERT-based encoders using DPR as the representative retriever. However, the universality of this claim remains untested across diverse representation strategies and model architectures. In contrast to previous studies that were limited to specific encoder configurations, our study revisits this idea by testing other representation approaches, specifically comparing the use of CLS tokens with mean pooling, to determine if the dominance of pre-trained knowledge is a universal property of dense retrieval or specific to certain architectural choices.

Alternative dense retrieval frameworks have explored different training paradigms and representation methods to enhance semantic understanding. Contriever, for instance, proposes a fully unsupervised dense retriever that utilizes mean pooling to aggregate token representations, differing from the CLS token approach often used in supervised settings like DPR [2]. Similarly, Sentence-BERT (SBERT) introduced siamese network structures to derive semantically meaningful sentence embeddings efficiently, reducing computational overhead for similarity search [3]. More recently, SimCSE advanced the field by demonstrating that simple contrastive learning objectives, whether unsupervised or supervised, can regularize the anisotropic space of pre-trained embeddings to be more uniform, thereby improving sentence embedding quality [4]. These works highlight the importance of how representations are formed and trained. Our paper builds on these diverse approaches by explicitly analyzing how the choice of pooling strategy (CLS vs. mean pooling) interacts with the backbone architecture to influence the acquisition of retrieval knowledge, thereby providing a more nuanced understanding of the interplay between representation methods and training dynamics.

The shift towards decoder-only models represents a recent trend in dense retrieval, challenging the dominance of encoder-only architectures. RePLAMA introduced a decoder-based dense retriever for open-domain question answering, exploring the potential of autoregressive models in this domain [5]. Additionally, PromptReps has investigated enhancing dense retrieval through prompt-based representations, further diversifying the landscape of how language models can be adapted for retrieval tasks [6]. These studies suggest that decoder-based models may offer distinct advantages or challenges compared to their encoder counterparts. Our work directly addresses this gap by extending the analysis of knowledge acquisition to decoder-only LLaMA models, confirming that while pre-trained knowledge underpins performance in DPR-style tuning, this pattern does not hold universally for decoder-based models, thus providing critical insights into the architectural dependencies of dense retrieval systems.
