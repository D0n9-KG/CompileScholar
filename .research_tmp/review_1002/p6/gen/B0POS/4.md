**Related Works**

In-context learning (ICL) has emerged as a powerful paradigm for leveraging large language models (LLMs) without parameter updates, yet its efficacy is heavily dependent on the quality of provided demonstrations. Early investigations revealed that ICL is surprisingly robust to label noise in classification tasks, suggesting that models can learn from the input distribution and label space even when ground-truth mappings are incorrect [5]. Subsequent work quantified this phenomenon, showing that the impact of correct input-label mappings varies based on prompt verbosity and model size, identifying key factors for noise-resilient ICL [1]. However, these findings primarily focus on classification, where the discrete nature of labels may mask the severity of annotation errors. In contrast, our work highlights that this robustness assumption breaks down significantly in text generation tasks, where noisy annotations cause substantial performance degradation, necessitating a re-examination of noise handling in generative contexts.

To address the instability and biases inherent in ICL, several studies have proposed calibration and decoding strategies. It has been shown that LLMs exhibit biases towards specific answers due to prompt formatting or pre-training data distribution, which can be mitigated by estimating and correcting for these biases using content-free inputs [11]. Similarly, recent work categorizes label biases into vanilla, context, and domain types, proposing calibration methods to improve performance by controlling for these systematic errors [10]. On the decoding side, contrastive decoding has been introduced to optimize generation by maximizing the likelihood difference between strong and weak models, effectively reducing repetition and improving reasoning capabilities [8]. This approach has been further extended to enhance contextual understanding by using adversarial irrelevant passages as negative samples, ensuring that the model relies on input context rather than parametric prior knowledge [9]. While these methods improve the reliability of the model’s output or decoding process, they do not directly address the quality of the input demonstrations. Our paper differs by focusing on the source of noise—the demonstrations themselves—and proposes a method to identify and correct flawed examples before they influence the ICL process.

Recent efforts have focused on selecting high-quality demonstrations to enhance ICL performance. Unified demonstration retrievers have been developed to retrieve relevant examples across various tasks using list-wise ranking formulations, aiming to reduce the cost and complexity of task-specific retrievers [3]. Other approaches, such as In-Context Vectors (ICV), recast ICL as latent space steering to make the process more controllable and efficient, reducing the reliance on explicit prompt examples [4]. More directly related to our work, recent studies have begun to address noisy labels in ICL. One line of work proposes methods inspired by supervised learning with noisy labels to safeguard against performance degradation [6]. Another prominent approach, Local Perplexity Ranking (LPR), detects noisy annotations by ranking local perplexities, operating under the assumption that noisy samples yield higher perplexities than clean ones [7]. LPR decomposes perplexity into inherent and matching components to decouple noise from semantic relevance. However, this perplexity-based paradigm suffers from biases introduced by the annotation itself and the domain-specific knowledge embedded in LLMs, which can lead to incorrect noise detection when the overall noise ratio is high. Our work builds on this line of research by introducing a dual debiasing framework that uses synthesized neighbors to explicitly correct perplexity estimates, yielding a robust Sample Cleanliness Score that accurately uncovers absolute sample cleanliness regardless of the corpus noise level.
