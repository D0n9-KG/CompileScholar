**Fairness Evaluation in Large Language Models**

The rapid deployment of Large Language Models (LLMs) has intensified scrutiny regarding their potential to propagate social biases. Comprehensive surveys highlight that LLMs often capture biases from training data, leading to discriminatory outcomes in downstream tasks [3][6]. Existing evaluation frameworks, such as HELM and BIG-bench, adopt multi-metric approaches to assess various desiderata, including fairness, alongside accuracy and robustness [1][13]. However, these benchmarks often rely on discrete accuracy-based metrics that fail to capture the nuanced internal decision-making processes of models. Recent work has specifically examined gender bias in coreference resolution, demonstrating that LLMs frequently align pronouns with stereotypical occupations, thereby amplifying societal biases beyond what is reflected in ground truth statistics [4][15]. While datasets like WinoBias and BBQ have been instrumental in identifying these biases, they primarily focus on prediction correctness rather than the confidence levels associated with those predictions [11][16]. Our work builds on this line of research by introducing an uncertainty-aware fairness metric, UCerF, which evaluates the implicit impact of model confidence, revealing biases that conventional accuracy-based measures overlook.

**Uncertainty Quantification in NLP**

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) has emerged as a critical area for assessing the reliability and trustworthiness of neural networks. Surveys indicate that understanding the sources of uncertainty, such as aleatoric and epistemic components, is essential for mitigating risks in high-stakes applications [18][19]. In the context of LLMs, recent studies have explored how uncertainty correlates with model performance, finding that larger models may exhibit greater uncertainty and that instruction fine-tuning can affect confidence levels [8][20]. Methods like semantic entropy and LM-polygraph have been proposed to estimate uncertainty in open-ended generation and multiple-choice tasks, respectively [21][26]. Furthermore, research has begun to investigate the intersection of fairness and uncertainty, suggesting that debiasing efforts might impact model reliability [32][35]. However, most existing UQ methods are designed to measure general prediction risk rather than group-specific fairness disparities. Unlike these approaches, our paper specifically leverages uncertainty to define a fine-grained fairness metric, demonstrating that high confidence in incorrect predictions for specific demographic groups constitutes a distinct form of bias.

**Bias Datasets and Benchmarks**

The effectiveness of bias evaluation is heavily dependent on the quality and diversity of the underlying datasets. Previous efforts have documented a "data documentation debt" in algorithmic fairness, with many popular datasets lacking standardized documentation [9]. In the domain of gender bias, datasets such as WinoBias and BUG have provided controlled environments for testing coreference resolution systems, though they are often criticized for being synthetic or limited in scale [11][17]. More recently, compositional benchmarks like CEB have attempted to unify bias evaluation across different social groups and tasks to facilitate cross-model comparison [7]. Despite these advances, current datasets often suffer from issues related to size, diversity, and clarity, which may not adequately reflect the capabilities of modern LLMs. To address these limitations, we introduce a new gender-occupation fairness evaluation dataset comprising 31,756 samples for co-reference resolution, offering a more diverse and suitable resource for benchmarking contemporary LLMs with our proposed uncertainty-aware metric.
