### Related Works

The theoretical foundation of modern income taxation is rooted in the pursuit of optimal equity-efficiency trade-offs. Early seminal works by Mirrlees [4] and Diamond [5] established the mathematical frameworks for optimal income taxation, focusing on production efficiency and the distortionary effects of taxes on labor supply. These models assume rational agents and homogeneous preferences, providing a static baseline for policy analysis. More recent advancements have refined these theories by incorporating specific behavioral elasticities. Saez [6] demonstrated how to derive optimal tax rates using elasticity estimates, while subsequent studies extended this to top incomes [7] and scenarios involving unemployment and wage responses [8]. Additionally, the case for progressive taxation has been extensively debated, with research moving from basic theoretical research to concrete policy recommendations [3]. While these models offer rigorous analytical insights, they often rely on simplified assumptions about taxpayer behavior and lack the adaptability to handle complex, heterogeneous real-world dynamics. In contrast, our work introduces TaxAgent, which moves beyond static theoretical models to simulate dynamic, heterogeneous taxpayer behaviors, thereby addressing the limitations of traditional optimal taxation frameworks that fail to account for irrational behavior and individual heterogeneity.

Empirical studies have evaluated the effectiveness of specific tax instruments, such as the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC), in reducing inequality. Research has examined the EITC’s role in the distribution of income [1] and its broader economic impacts [2]. These studies highlight the potential of targeted tax credits to alleviate poverty but often operate within fixed policy structures that do not adapt to changing economic conditions or individual responses. Traditional systems like the U.S. federal income tax, while effective in reducing inequality, are criticized for their lack of adaptability and rigidity. Although models like the Saez Optimal Taxation adjust dynamically based on elasticity, they still struggle to fully capture the complexity of taxpayer heterogeneity. Our study differs by proposing a scalable, data-driven framework that iteratively optimizes tax rates in response to simulated real-world behaviors, offering a more adaptive solution than static empirical evaluations of existing tax credits.

The integration of computational methods and artificial intelligence into economic policy design represents a significant shift from traditional econometric approaches. The impact of machine learning on economics has been widely discussed, with potential applications in forecasting and policy analysis [10]. A notable development in this area is the "AI Economist," which employs deep reinforcement learning to discover tax policies in dynamic economies [9]. This approach trains social planners to balance equality and productivity without relying on explicit economic modeling assumptions, showing improvements over baseline policies like the Saez framework. However, reinforcement learning agents typically operate in closed-loop simulations with predefined reward functions and may not capture the nuanced, language-driven reasoning required for complex fiscal policy justification. Furthermore, agent-based modeling (ABM) has been increasingly used in economics and finance to simulate complex systems [11], and large language models (LLMs) are emerging as powerful tools for financial applications [12]. Our research builds on these lines of work by uniquely integrating LLMs with ABM. Unlike the AI Economist, which relies on reinforcement learning, TaxAgent utilizes LLMs to enable the government agent to reason and iteratively optimize tax policies, providing a novel, interpretable, and scalable framework for fiscal policy evaluation that explicitly models heterogeneous household agents.
