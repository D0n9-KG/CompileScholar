**Related Works**

The evolution of Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems has shifted from traditional concatenative methods to neural end-to-end architectures, prioritizing both synthesis speed and naturalness. Early approaches to expressive prosody in unit-selection systems relied on selecting pre-recorded units to match desired prosodic features [1]. The advent of neural networks introduced autoregressive models like Tacotron, which significantly improved quality but suffered from slow inference and limited controllability [2]. To address these limitations, non-autoregressive models such as FastSpeech and FastSpeech 2 were developed to generate mel-spectrograms in parallel, utilizing duration predictors and knowledge distillation to handle the one-to-many mapping problem inherent in TTS [2][3]. While these models offer robust and fast synthesis, they typically require specific architectural modifications or additional training data to achieve fine-grained control over prosodic attributes. In contrast, our method operates as a post-hoc intervention on pre-trained models, eliminating the need for architectural changes or retraining to adjust prosody.

Controlling specific prosodic attributes, such as speaking rate and style, has been a major focus in recent TTS research. Several approaches introduce specialized modules or latent variables to enable this control. For instance, Global Style Tokens (GSTs) were proposed to model acoustic expressiveness through a bank of embeddings trained without explicit labels, allowing for style transfer and speed variation [6]. Similarly, hierarchical generative models have been used to control latent attributes like accent and background noise by employing variational autoencoder frameworks with categorical and Gaussian latent variables [8]. Other methods focus on specific attributes; for example, one study proposed conditioning the duration predictor on speaking rate to achieve implicit speed control in non-autoregressive TTS [5]. Another approach explored temporal control of prosodic variation to manipulate speech dynamics [4]. Furthermore, some works have investigated manipulating internal representations, such as the encoder’s output embeddings, to control speaking rate in end-to-end models [9]. While these methods demonstrate effective control, they generally depend on specialized modules, additional training, or specific architectural designs. Our approach differs by employing Counterfactual Activation Editing, a model-agnostic technique that manipulates internal representations at inference time, thereby avoiding the dependency on specialized modules or additional training phases.

Mispronunciation correction and prosody transfer are critical for enhancing the practicality of TTS systems, particularly in low-resource settings. Traditional mispronunciation correction often relies on grapheme-to-phoneme dictionaries, which can be impractical when such resources are scarce [10]. To address this, some recent works have explored using speech from non-target speakers to correct mispronunciations in grapheme-input TTS systems [10]. In the domain of prosody, researchers have developed methods for end-to-end prosody transfer, where a latent embedding space is learned from a reference acoustic representation to replicate prosody across different speakers or texts [7]. These methods allow for the synthesis of text with the prosodic characteristics of a reference utterance, even when the speakers differ. However, these techniques typically require the model to be trained with specific objectives for transfer or correction. Our work builds on the goal of refining TTS outputs but diverges by offering a unified, inference-time solution for both prosody adjustment and mispronunciation correction. By editing internal activations, we achieve these refinements without retraining, bridging the gap between static pre-trained models and editable speech synthesis.
