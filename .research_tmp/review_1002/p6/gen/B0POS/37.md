Quantization has emerged as a primary technique for accelerating deep neural network inference by reducing the bit-width of model parameters and activations. Early approaches focused on uniform quantization strategies for convolutional networks, such as learned quantization for compactness [1] and whitepaper guidelines for efficient inference [2]. More recent work has addressed the complexity of modern architectures, including attention-based models. GOBO, for instance, compresses BERT models to 3-bit precision without requiring fine-tuning, demonstrating significant reductions in memory traffic and energy consumption on hardware like TPUs and Eyeriss [5]. While these methods effectively reduce storage and computational load for standard layers, they often rely on fixed-point approximations or specific hardware units that may not generalize well to the diverse arithmetic operations found in Vision Transformers. In contrast, this paper introduces the Microscaling Integer (MXInt) format, which provides a unified arithmetic framework that maintains high accuracy across all ViT operations, rather than relying on ad-hoc approximations for specific nonlinear functions.

The challenge of quantizing Vision Transformers (ViTs) is exacerbated by the presence of complex arithmetic operations, such as Softmax and LayerNorm, which are sensitive to precision loss. Existing hardware acceleration methods often exploit parallelism in matrix multiplication but offload these sensitive operations to the CPU, leading to significant communication overhead and suboptimal performance. HeatViT addresses this by proposing a hardware-efficient token pruning framework for FPGAs, which reduces computational load by identifying non-informative tokens and employs 8-bit fixed-point quantization with polynomial approximations for nonlinear functions [6]. Although HeatViT improves efficiency through structural pruning and approximation, it still relies on specific polynomial approximations for nonlinear layers and does not fully integrate all operations into a single low-precision datapath. Our work differs by mapping all ViT operations, including Softmax and LayerNorm, directly onto the FPGA using the MXInt format, thereby eliminating the need for CPU offloading and avoiding the accuracy trade-offs associated with polynomial approximations.

Hardware-aware quantization frameworks have also been developed to optimize the trade-off between accuracy and hardware metrics. HAQ utilizes reinforcement learning to automatically determine mixed-precision quantization policies, leveraging hardware simulator feedback to optimize for latency and energy [4]. This approach highlights the importance of considering hardware constraints during the quantization process. However, HAQ and similar frameworks typically focus on determining bit-widths for standard layers and do not address the specific arithmetic challenges of transformer-based architectures, such as the dynamic range issues in Softmax and LayerNorm. Furthermore, they do not propose new data formats that inherently support these complex operations in low precision. In this paper, we build upon the concept of hardware-aware design by introducing MXInt-specific hardware optimizations that are tailored to the unique arithmetic requirements of ViTs, achieving a superior balance of accuracy, speedup, and area efficiency compared to general-purpose mixed-precision approaches.

The development of new narrow-precision data formats has recently gained traction as a means to improve quantization performance. The Block Data Representations (BDR) framework introduced shared microexponents (MX) to explore a spectrum of narrow-precision formats, demonstrating that MX outperforms traditional floating-point and block floating-point standards in large-scale models [3]. This work established the theoretical and empirical foundation for using shared microexponents to handle dynamic range in deep learning. Our work directly builds on this line of research by applying the MX concept to the specific context of Vision Transformer acceleration on FPGAs. We extend the BDR framework by designing custom hardware datapaths for MXInt, enabling the first ViT accelerator to map all operations onto FPGAs with minimal accuracy loss, thus translating the theoretical benefits of MX formats into practical, high-performance hardware implementations.
