**Related Works**

The rapid evolution of large language models (LLMs) has driven significant advancements in distributed training systems and hardware architectures. The Transformer architecture, introduced in [6], laid the foundation for modern LLMs, enabling parallelizable training that scales effectively. Subsequent works have focused on scaling these models to unprecedented sizes, such as GPT-3 [7] and PaLM [8], which utilized specialized hardware like TPUs to achieve state-of-the-art performance. On the hardware side, systems like TPU v4 [2] and NVIDIA’s architectures [1] have been designed to support the massive computational demands of these models. However, the complexity of deploying these models across diverse hardware clusters introduces significant challenges in achieving optimal efficiency. Our work, Lumos, addresses this by providing a trace-driven performance modeling toolkit that accurately predicts execution behaviors across various LLM configurations, bridging the gap between hardware capabilities and practical deployment efficiency.

To manage the vast search space of distributed training strategies, several systems have proposed automated or semi-automated parallelization techniques. Megatron-LM [9][10] introduced model parallelism for multi-billion parameter models, while DeepSpeed and Megatron were combined to train the 530B parameter MT-NLG model [11]. Other frameworks, such as GSPMD [12] and XLA [13], offer compiler-based automatic parallelization, and ALPA [14] automates inter- and intra-operator parallelism. More recently, Galvatron [15] employs decision trees and dynamic programming to find optimal hybrid parallelism strategies. While these systems focus on generating efficient training plans, they often rely on static assumptions or limited search spaces. Lumos differs by leveraging detailed execution traces to model the actual runtime behavior, allowing for more accurate performance estimation and exploration of new configurations without the need for exhaustive manual tuning or complex search algorithms.

Sequence length has emerged as a critical dimension for LLM training, leading to innovations in sequence parallelism. Early work proposed sequence parallelism to handle long sequences by splitting inputs across devices [16]. DeepSpeed-Ulysses [17] further optimized this by using all-to-all collective communication for attention computation, addressing memory-communication inefficiencies. Ring Attention [18] introduced blockwise computation to overlap communication with computation, enabling near-infinite context lengths. These techniques are essential for modern LLMs but introduce complex communication patterns that are difficult to predict analytically. Lumos builds on this line of work by incorporating these complex execution patterns into its trace-driven model, enabling accurate prediction of performance for long-sequence training scenarios where analytical models may fall short.

Performance modeling and co-design tools have been developed to guide system optimization. Daydream [21] uses low-level traces to simulate DNN execution and predict the impact of optimizations. Astra-Sim [23][24] enables software/hardware co-design exploration for distributed training platforms, with Astra-Sim 2.0 extending this to hierarchical networks. Other tools like Amped [19] and Calculon [20] provide analytical models for distributed training performance. While these tools offer valuable insights, they often rely on simplified analytical models that may not capture the fine-grained complexities of modern LLM training stacks. Lumos distinguishes itself by using high-fidelity traces from production clusters to replay execution with high accuracy, providing a more realistic basis for evaluating optimizations and exploring new model and deployment configurations.
