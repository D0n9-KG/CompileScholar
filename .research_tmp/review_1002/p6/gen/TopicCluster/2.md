## Related Work

### Multimodal Video and Vision-Language Understanding
Recent advancements in vision-language models (VLMs) have significantly expanded the capabilities of artificial intelligence to process and reason over complex visual and textual data. Foundational large language models, such as GPT-4, have established benchmarks for general reasoning and text generation [5], while specialized architectures like Qwen-VL have introduced versatile capabilities for understanding, localization, and text reading within visual contexts [1]. To extend these capabilities to temporal data, several frameworks have emerged that integrate video foundation models with LLMs; for instance, VideoChat focuses on chat-centric video understanding to enhance spatiotemporal reasoning and causal inference [2], whereas Video-LLaMA employs instruction tuning to create an audio-visual language model specifically optimized for video comprehension [3]. Furthermore, Unified-IO 2 represents a shift toward autoregressive multimodality, tokenizing diverse inputs including vision, language, audio, and actions into a shared semantic space to enable unified understanding and generation [4]. In contrast to these general-purpose video understanding systems, our work focuses specifically on the generation of nonverbal cues within conversational contexts, leveraging the architectural insights of these multimodal frameworks to bridge the gap between textual dialogue and nonverbal expression.

### Multimodal Emotion Recognition and Datasets
The field of multimodal emotion recognition has been driven by the development of large-scale, annotated datasets that capture the interplay between verbal and nonverbal signals. Early efforts such as IEMOCAP provided interactive emotional dyadic motion capture data, establishing a foundation for studying emotional dynamics in controlled settings [6]. Subsequent datasets expanded this scope to more naturalistic and complex scenarios; CMU-MOSEI introduced multimodal language analysis in the wild using an interpretable dynamic fusion graph [7], while MELD offered a large-scale multimodal multi-party dataset derived from the TV series *Friends* to facilitate emotion recognition in conversational contexts [8]. Beyond dataset creation, recent research has focused on improving the accessibility and representation of emotional cues. For example, one line of work explores the recognition of nonverbal communication cues as a pathway to more accessible human communication [11], while EmotionCLIP proposes a pre-training paradigm that extracts visual emotion representations from uncurated verbal and nonverbal data to address data scarcity [12]. Unlike these approaches, which primarily focus on recognizing or classifying emotions, our work utilizes the VENUS dataset to train a model that actively generates nonverbal cues, thereby moving from passive recognition to active multimodal generation.

### Embodied Conversational Agents and Social Robots
Embodied conversational agents (ECAs) and social robots aim to create immersive human-machine interactions by integrating physical presence with intelligent dialogue. Recent systems have leveraged large-scale web videos to learn real-world conversation patterns; CHAMPAGNE, for instance, utilizes the YTD-18M corpus of video-based dialogues to achieve state-of-the-art results in vision-language tasks [9]. Similarly, the "Let's Go Real Talk" framework introduces a spoken dialogue model that processes and generates audio-visual speech without intermediate text, supported by the MultiDialog corpus [10]. On the hardware side, FurChat demonstrates an embodied agent on a Furhat robot that combines GPT-3.5 for open and closed-domain dialogue with generated facial expressions for visitor interaction [13]. Additionally, other research has augmented the empathetic capacities of social robots by using LLMs to generate and label four types of empathetic non-verbal cues, including speech, action, facial expression, and emotion [14]. While these systems successfully integrate nonverbal elements into specific robotic or conversational pipelines, they often rely on separate modules for text and nonverbal generation. Our work, MARS, offers a unified framework that jointly generates text and nonverbal cues, aiming to provide a more cohesive and scalable approach to embodied interaction.

### Nonverbal Motion Generation and Dyadic Interaction
Generating realistic nonverbal motion, particularly in response to speech or text, remains a challenging task in computer graphics and human-computer interaction. Several methods have been developed to synthesize facial and body movements from various inputs. EMOCA introduces a monocular face capture and animation method that uses a deep perceptual emotion consistency loss to ensure reconstructed 3D facial expressions match the emotional content of input images [15]. For more holistic motion, frameworks have been developed to generate diverse 3D body motions, including poses, hand gestures, and facial expressions, from speech recordings using compositional VQ-VAEs [16]. In the domain of text-aligned motion, HumanTOMATO generates high-quality whole-body motions using a hierarchical VQ-VAE and a pre-trained text-motion alignment model [18]. More recently, the integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into motion generation has gained traction; MotionLLM learns multimodal motion-language representations [17], and other works have treated quantized motion elements as language tokens within transformer-based LLMs to generate appropriate listener facial responses from speaker transcriptions [19]. Furthermore, modeling dyadic interaction has been approached by autoregressively predicting multiple possible listener responses from multimodal speaker inputs using motion-audio cross-attention transformers [20]. Our work builds upon these advances by employing a next-token prediction objective that combines text with vector-quantized nonverbal representations, enabling the unified generation of both verbal and nonverbal languages within a single multimodal model.
