## Related Work

### Foundations and Core Mechanisms of Generative Retrieval
The paradigm of generative retrieval fundamentally reimagines information retrieval by replacing traditional inverted indexes with neural networks that directly generate document identifiers. Early foundational work introduced the Differentiable Search Index (DSI), which utilizes a single Transformer to encode the entire corpus within its parameters, enabling the direct mapping of string queries to relevant document identifiers [14]. This approach was further analyzed and extended by viewing generative retrieval as a form of dense retrieval, with variants like Tied-Atomic addressing scaling and index updating challenges [15]. To overcome the memory limitations inherent in storing entire corpora in model weights, the Generative Dense Retrieval (GDR) paradigm was proposed, combining the deep interaction of generative retrieval for coarse matching with the scalability of dense retrieval for fine-grained intra-cluster matching [22]. Recent advancements have focused on enhancing the information capacity of these models; for instance, Nonparametric Decoding improves performance by utilizing external nonparametric contextualized vocab embeddings instead of vanilla ones [21]. Furthermore, decoding strategies have been refined to improve both accuracy and speed, such as Planning Ahead in Generative Retrieval (PAG), which guides autoregressive generation through the simultaneous decoding of set-based and sequential identifiers [32]. Broader surveys and conceptual frameworks have also emerged to categorize these advancements, distinguishing between generative document retrieval and reliable response generation [1], and exploring how classical IR concepts can be synthesized with pre-trained language models to mitigate hallucination and provide domain-expert-level advice [2]. Our work builds upon these foundational mechanisms by theoretically examining the inherent limitations of the constrained auto-regressive generation process that underpins these systems.

### Document Identifier Design and Indexing Strategies
A critical component of generative retrieval is the design of document identifiers (docids) and the indexing strategies used to assign them. Initial approaches, such as GENRE, proposed autoregressively generating unique entity names token-by-token to reduce memory footprint and capture fine-grained context-entity interactions [3]. To enable fully end-to-end optimization, methods like GenRet introduced learning to tokenize documents into short discrete identifiers via discrete auto-encoding [4]. The Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI) further unified training and indexing stages, significantly improving recall over traditional paradigms [5]. Subsequent research has explored various identifier structures, including the use of term-sets as strong identifiers for auto-regressive search engines [8] and the generation of distinctive substrings (n-grams) for efficient passage-level retrieval [13]. To address the data distribution mismatch between indexing and retrieval, DSI-QG proposed representing documents with generated and re-ranked queries during the indexing phase [7]. Other strategies include the Auto Search Indexer (ASI), which jointly optimizes identifier learning and retrieval [9], and Semantic-Enhanced DSI (SE-DSI), which uses elaborative descriptions and fine-grained semantic features inspired by cognitive learning strategies [10]. More recently, GR$^2$ extended generative retrieval to handle multi-graded relevance by ensuring distinct identifiers and employing constrained contrastive training [11]. Additionally, RIPOR proposed an optimization framework for large-scale benchmarks using prefix-oriented ranking and relevance-based document ID construction [16]. While these works focus on the structural and semantic quality of identifiers, our paper investigates how the specific constraints imposed by these identifiers affect the theoretical error bounds of the generative model.

### Dynamic Corpora, Scalability, and Robustness
The practical deployment of generative retrieval faces significant challenges regarding scalability to large corpora and robustness to out-of-distribution data. Several studies have highlighted that the generalization capabilities of generative retrieval models require improvement compared to dense retrieval models, particularly when facing query variations, unseen tasks, or corpus expansion [19, 20]. To address dynamic corpora, CLEVER introduced a continual learning model employing incremental product quantization and memory-augmented mechanisms to index new documents without catastrophic forgetting [18]. Similarly, IncDSI formulated the addition of new documents as a constrained optimization problem to enable real-time indexing with minimal parameter changes [23], while DSI++ addressed the challenge of updating transformer memory with new documents [24]. Training-free approaches have also been explored, such as few-shot generative retrieval frameworks that use LLM prompting to create a document identifier bank, thereby reducing training overhead and improving adaptability [17]. Beyond neural-specific solutions, broader theoretical work on tree-based indexes has been applied to improve efficiency and performance in dense retrieval and recommender systems [29, 31]. Crucially, recent theoretical work has developed concepts for Bayes optimality and calibration under beam search, proposing algorithms to learn optimal tree models that eliminate the training-testing discrepancy caused by beam search [30]. Our paper complements this line of work by providing a theoretical lower bound on error in the Bayes-optimal setting and analyzing the specific impact of beam search on marginal distributions, thereby clarifying the theoretical limits of robustness in these dynamic and scalable systems.

### Unified Retrieval-Generation and Constrained Decoding
Recent trends have focused on unifying retrieval and generation tasks and rigorously analyzing the effects of constrained decoding. Frameworks like LTRGR bridge the gap between autoregressive generation objectives and final passage ranking targets by optimizing with a rank loss [6]. Theoretical analyses have demonstrated that generative retrieval can be viewed as a special case of multi-vector dense retrieval, revealing shared relevance computation mechanisms [12]. To tackle knowledge-intensive tasks, unified models such as CorpusLM integrate generative retrieval, closed-book generation, and RAG through a unified greedy decoding process [33], while UniGen integrates generative document retrieval and grounded answer generation into a single model using shared encoders and distinct decoders [34]. The role of constraints in this paradigm has been scrutinized from multiple angles. Generalization analyses for models with concurrent verifiers show that such verifiers can guarantee hypothesis validity without increasing error bounds [25], and specific studies have investigated how introducing constraints at inference time affects generalization error [26]. In the context of large language models, methods like GUARD combine autoregressive proposal distributions with rejection sampling to guarantee strict constraint satisfaction [27], and GeLaTo uses tractable probabilistic models to impose lexical constraints on autoregressive generation [28]. Our work directly engages with this literature by deriving a lower bound of the error in terms of the KL divergence between ground-truth and model-predicted step-wise marginal distributions, and by revealing that the usage of marginal distributions in beam search may not be ideal, thus providing a foundational theoretical understanding of how constraints shape the generalization capabilities of generative retrieval.
