## Related Work

**General Class Imbalance and Loss Re-weighting**
A substantial body of literature addresses the class imbalance problem by modifying the loss function or sampling strategy to mitigate the bias toward head classes. Early systematic studies investigated the impact of imbalance on convolutional neural networks, comparing standard mitigation techniques such as oversampling and undersampling [1]. To address the diminishing returns of additional data points in long-tailed distributions, class-balanced loss functions based on the effective number of samples have been proposed to re-weight the loss accordingly [2]. Similarly, cost-sensitive learning approaches jointly optimize class-dependent costs and network parameters to learn robust feature representations without altering the original data distribution [4]. More recent work has focused on modeling the specific challenges of tail classes [3] and adjusting the logits in standard softmax cross-entropy training to correct for label distribution skew [9]. While these methods effectively rebalance the optimization landscape, they often rely on heuristic adjustments to the loss function rather than fundamentally altering the geometric structure of the feature space, which our Aligned Contrastive Loss (ACL) aims to address through gradient alignment.

**Decoupled Representation and Classifier Learning**
Another prominent line of research decouples the learning of feature representations from the training of the classifier to prevent the degradation of features caused by re-balancing strategies. The foundational work in this area demonstrated that high-quality representations can be learned with natural sampling, with balancing applied only to the classifier [5]. This paradigm was extended by the Bilateral-Branch Network (BBN), which uses cumulative learning to simultaneously optimize representation and classifier learning, thereby mitigating the trade-off where re-balancing damages feature quality [6]. Comprehensive surveys have categorized these deep long-tailed learning methods into class re-balancing, information augmentation, and module improvement, highlighting the importance of relative accuracy metrics [7]. Further advancements include decoupled training with stochastic representations, which utilizes Stochastic Weight Averaging (SWA) and self-distillation to enhance feature generalization and classifier re-training [8]. In contrast to these decoupled approaches, our method integrates contrastive learning directly into the representation learning phase, ensuring that the feature space is inherently balanced and aligned with the classification objective from the outset.

**Contrastive Learning for Long-Tailed Recognition**
Recent efforts have increasingly turned to contrastive learning to improve long-tailed recognition by explicitly shaping the geometry of the embedding space. Parametric Contrastive Learning (PaCo) introduces learnable class-wise centers to rebalance optimization, a concept generalized in GPaCo to handle both imbalanced and balanced data effectively [10, 11]. To correct the optimization bias of supervised contrastive learning on long-tailed data, Balanced Contrastive Learning (BCL) has been developed to form a regular simplex in the feature space [12]. Other approaches reinterpret long-tailed recognition as mutual information maximization between latent features and labels, seamlessly integrating contrastive learning with logit adjustment [13]. Probabilistic methods estimate class data distributions using mixture models to sample contrastive pairs more effectively [18], while subclass-balancing techniques cluster head classes into smaller subclasses to enforce a two-layer hierarchy for instance- and subclass-balance [19]. Additionally, targeted supervised contrastive learning generates uniformly distributed targets on a hypersphere to force minority class features to converge to these targets, improving uniformity [21]. These works highlight the potential of contrastive objectives but often suffer from gradient conflicts or imbalanced attraction and repulsion forces as the number of views increases, issues that our theoretical analysis identifies and our ACL algorithm resolves.

**Foundations of Contrastive and Self-Supervised Learning**
The effectiveness of contrastive methods in long-tailed recognition is grounded in broader advances in self-supervised and contrastive representation learning. Momentum Contrast (MoCo) established a framework for unsupervised visual representation learning by building a dynamic dictionary with a queue and a moving-averaged encoder [14]. SimCLR systematically studied data augmentations, nonlinear transformations, and batch sizes to achieve state-of-the-art self-supervised performance, emphasizing the importance of multiple augmentation samples per image to decrease test error and improve generalization [15, 23]. Bootstrap Your Own Latent (BYOL) introduced a self-supervised approach that predicts representations of a target network without relying on negative pairs, demonstrating the flexibility of contrastive objectives [16]. In the supervised setting, Supervised Contrastive Learning (SCL) extends batch contrastive learning by pulling together same-class clusters and pushing apart different-class clusters, achieving superior accuracy and robustness compared to cross-entropy [17]. Furthermore, SwAV proposes an unsupervised algorithm that enforces consistency between cluster assignments of different augmentations without explicit pairwise comparisons, offering improved memory efficiency [22]. Our work builds upon these foundational principles, specifically analyzing the gradient dynamics of SCL to identify and eliminate the conflicts that hinder generalization in multi-view long-tailed scenarios.
