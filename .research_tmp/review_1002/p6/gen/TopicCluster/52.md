## Related Work

**Dense Passage Retrieval for Question Answering**
The paradigm of dense passage retrieval has fundamentally shifted open-domain question answering from sparse lexical matching to semantic vector space search. Pioneering work by Karpukhin et al. [1] introduced the Dual-Encoder (DPR) framework, demonstrating that bi-encoder architectures significantly outperform traditional sparse methods like BM25 by learning dense representations of questions and passages. Subsequent research has explored architectural variations to enhance retrieval performance; for instance, RePLAMA [5] investigates decoder-based dense retrievers to capture more nuanced contextual dependencies in passage retrieval, while PromptReps [6] leverages prompt-based representations to better align semantic meanings within the retrieval process. These approaches collectively establish the efficacy of dense encoders but often treat the pre-trained backbone as a static foundation. In contrast, our work moves beyond architectural comparisons to dissect the internal mechanisms of knowledge acquisition, specifically examining how pre-training versus fine-tuning contributes to the final retrieval capability in both encoder-only and decoder-based models.

**Sentence Embedding and Similarity Learning**
A parallel line of research focuses on the generalization of sentence embeddings and the optimization of similarity learning objectives. Early efforts, such as Sentence-BERT [3], modified standard BERT architectures using siamese and triplet networks to generate semantically meaningful embeddings, thereby enabling efficient similarity search without the computational overhead of full cross-encoders. More recent advancements have emphasized unsupervised and contrastive learning paradigms to improve representation quality. Contriever [2] demonstrates that fully unsupervised dense retrievers can learn effective representations without labeled data, challenging the necessity of supervised fine-tuning for initial representation learning. Similarly, SimCSE [4] introduces a simple contrastive learning framework that utilizes dropout as noise in unsupervised settings or natural language inference pairs in supervised settings to refine sentence embeddings. While these studies highlight the potential of unsupervised pre-training and contrastive objectives, they do not explicitly quantify the boundary between knowledge acquired during pre-training and that gained during task-specific fine-tuning. Our study addresses this gap by analyzing whether fine-tuning reorganizes existing knowledge or merely adjusts neuron activations, providing a deeper mechanistic understanding of these embedding models.

**Open-Domain Question Answering Benchmarks**
The evaluation of dense retrieval systems relies heavily on large-scale, real-world benchmarks that reflect user intent and query diversity. The Natural Questions benchmark [8] utilizes real user queries from Google Search to evaluate open-domain QA systems, providing a rigorous test of a model's ability to retrieve relevant passages for diverse, natural language questions. Complementing this, MS MARCO [7] offers a large-scale machine reading comprehension dataset derived from real user search queries, specifically designed to benchmark passage ranking and question-answering models on a massive corpus of web documents. These datasets serve as the standard empirical grounds for assessing retrieval performance. In this paper, we utilize both Natural Questions and MS MARCO to ensure our findings on knowledge acquisition are robust across different dataset characteristics and scales, thereby extending previous analyses that were limited to a single benchmark.
