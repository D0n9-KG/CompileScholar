## Related Work

**Foundations of Generative Retrieval**
The paradigm of generative retrieval (GR) has fundamentally shifted information retrieval from index-based lookup to direct sequence generation, aiming to unify indexing and retrieval within a single neural model. Early seminal works established the core architecture by encoding corpus knowledge into model parameters; specifically, the Differentiable Search Index (DSI) introduced the concept of mapping string queries directly to document IDs via a Transformer memory [2], while the Neural Corpus Indexer (NCI) further refined this by unifying training and indexing in a sequence-to-sequence framework to improve recall [4]. Subsequent research expanded these foundations to specific domains and scales, such as GENRE, which applied autoregressive generation to entity retrieval to mitigate the memory constraints of dense vectors [1], and DynamicRetriever, which eliminated the need for sparse or dense indexes by embedding corpus information directly into pre-trained model parameters [3]. More recently, Ultron proposed an "ultimate retriever" approach that utilizes semantically rich document IDs and a multi-stage training workflow to achieve end-to-end retrieval performance [5]. While these foundational models demonstrated the viability of GR on static collections, they largely assumed a fixed corpus, leaving the behavior of these architectures under dynamic conditions underexplored. This paper builds upon these foundational architectures by systematically evaluating their generalization capabilities when the underlying document collection evolves, rather than treating the corpus as a static entity.

**Document Identifier Design and Optimization**
A critical component of generative retrieval is the design of document identifiers (docids), which determines how effectively the model can map queries to relevant documents. Early approaches often relied on arbitrary or numeric IDs, but recent work has focused on optimizing these identifiers for semantic richness and scalability. For instance, RIPOR introduced an optimization framework that addresses prefix-oriented ranking and constructs relevance-based document IDs to enable effective GR on large-scale benchmarks [6]. In parallel, GenRet proposed learning to tokenize documents into semantic discrete identifiers via discrete auto-encoding, enhancing the end-to-end nature of the retrieval process [7]. To further improve representation, multiview identifier approaches have been developed to combine synthetic identifiers, titles, and substrings, thereby enhancing passage ranking [11]. Additionally, methods have been proposed to handle multi-graded relevance by creating distinct identifiers and employing constrained contrastive training [10], while others have explored using all n-grams in a passage as potential identifiers to generate distinctive substrings for efficient retrieval [12]. These studies highlight the importance of docid structure in static settings; however, they do not sufficiently address how different docid granularities and types (e.g., text-based vs. numeric-based) impact performance when new documents are introduced. Our work contrasts these design choices specifically in the context of dynamic corpora, revealing that fine-grained, text-based docids offer superior generalization to unseen documents compared to the high-efficiency but less generalizable numeric-based alternatives.

**Pre-training and Knowledge-Intensive Applications**
Beyond standard retrieval, generative models have been adapted for knowledge-intensive tasks by leveraging pre-training to internalize corpus knowledge. GERE, for example, introduced a system for fact verification that retrieves evidences generatively by producing document titles and sentence identifiers, thereby reducing memory overhead and capturing inter-document dependencies [8]. Similarly, CorpusBrain proposed a pre-trained generative retrieval model that replaces traditional index-retrieve-then-rank pipelines with a single-step model, significantly improving performance on knowledge-intensive language tasks [9]. These approaches demonstrate the potential of GR to serve as a unified interface for knowledge access. However, both GERE and CorpusBrain were primarily evaluated in static or semi-static environments where the knowledge base does not change frequently. In contrast, our study investigates how these pre-trained generative capabilities hold up when the underlying knowledge base is dynamic, providing empirical evidence on the robustness of such models in evolving scenarios.

**Dynamic Corpora and Continual Learning**
The challenge of maintaining retrieval performance as corpora evolve has recently attracted attention, with several works proposing mechanisms for continual learning and index updating. DSI++ addressed the specific challenge of updating transformer memory to incorporate new documents into the corpus without full retraining [13]. Extending this to broader language tasks, CorpusBrain++ introduced a continual generative pre-training framework designed to adapt to evolving corpora for knowledge-intensive applications [14]. More recently, CLEVER proposed a model for continual learning in generative retrieval that utilizes incremental product quantization and memory-augmented learning to index new documents while mitigating catastrophic forgetting [15]. Furthermore, recent evaluations have begun to explore the practicality of GR on dynamic corpora, demonstrating its adaptability and computational efficiency compared to dual encoder systems [16]. While these works provide initial insights into dynamic GR, they often focus on specific update mechanisms or limited experimental settings. Our paper complements this line of work by conducting a comprehensive replication and exploration of various representative GR approaches, systematically comparing text-based and numeric-based docids to identify the specific factors—such as semantic alignment and lexical diversity—that drive superior generalization in dynamic environments.
