## Related Work

**Open-Vocabulary Detection and Segmentation**
Open-vocabulary object detection aims to extend the fixed class sets of traditional detectors to arbitrary user-specified categories, a challenge comprehensively analyzed in recent surveys that categorize methodologies based on their design principles and alignment strategies [1]. Early and prominent approaches in this domain focus on expanding the classifier vocabulary through large-scale image-level supervision or region-language alignment. For instance, Detic demonstrates that detectors can be trained to recognize tens of thousands of classes by leveraging image-level classification data, thereby bypassing the need for complex box-label assignment schemes [6]. In contrast, methods like RegionCLIP and CoDet emphasize the alignment of visual regions with linguistic concepts; RegionCLIP extends the CLIP framework to learn region-level representations by aligning image patches with template captions [7], while CoDet derives region-word alignment by discovering co-occurring objects across images that share common semantic concepts [8]. More recently, SHiNe introduces a training-free classifier that utilizes semantic class hierarchies to improve robustness across varying vocabulary granularities without incurring additional inference overhead [9]. While these methods significantly advance the detection of specific, well-defined classes, they generally assume that the input vocabulary is precise and relevant to the scene. Our work, VocAda, complements these detectors by addressing the orthogonal problem of vocabulary refinement, ensuring that the classes passed to such state-of-the-art detectors are tailored to the specific content of the image at test time.

**Vision-Language Pretraining and Alignment**
The foundation of modern open-vocabulary detection lies in the ability to align visual features with natural language representations. CLIP pioneered this approach by learning transferable visual models from natural language supervision, matching images to captions to enable zero-shot transfer across diverse computer vision tasks [2]. This image-text alignment has become the standard mechanism for bridging the gap between visual perception and linguistic understanding in detection pipelines. However, CLIP’s original formulation operates at the global image level, which can limit its granularity for fine-grained object detection. Subsequent works, such as RegionCLIP [7] and CoDet [8], have built upon this foundation to refine alignment at the region level. Our method leverages the robust zero-shot capabilities of such vision-language models, specifically utilizing an image captioner derived from these pretraining paradigms to generate textual descriptions that inform our vocabulary adaptation process.

**Large-Scale Object Detection Datasets**
The development and evaluation of object detection models rely heavily on large-scale datasets that provide diverse and annotated examples. The Microsoft COCO dataset established a benchmark for scene understanding by providing per-instance segmentations for common objects in complex everyday scenes [3]. To address the limitations of fixed, small-scale vocabularies, the LVIS dataset was introduced to support large-vocabulary instance segmentation, incorporating over 1,000 object categories to mitigate the challenges posed by long-tail category distributions [4]. These datasets are critical for training the base detectors and evaluating the performance of open-vocabulary methods. In our experiments, we utilize COCO and Objects365 (which shares similar large-scale characteristics with LVIS in terms of category diversity) to demonstrate the versatility of VocAda across different data distributions and category granularities.

**General Image Classification Datasets**
Beyond detection-specific benchmarks, general image classification datasets have played a foundational role in training the visual backbones and language encoders used in contemporary detection systems. ImageNet, a large-scale hierarchical image database, was designed to facilitate the development and evaluation of object recognition algorithms, providing the massive volume of labeled data necessary for training deep convolutional neural networks [5]. The representations learned from such hierarchical classification tasks often serve as the initial feature extractors for detection models. While ImageNet itself does not provide instance-level annotations, the robust visual features derived from its training are frequently transferred to downstream detection tasks, including the open-vocabulary settings evaluated in this paper.
