## Related Work

**Hardware Architectures for AI**
Modern AI hardware architectures are designed to address the specific computational and memory demands of large-scale machine learning workloads. NVIDIA’s Blackwell architecture [1] represents the latest evolution in GPU design, focusing on enhanced throughput and efficiency for generative AI tasks. In contrast, Google’s TPU v4 [2] introduces an optically reconfigurable supercomputer that utilizes dynamic interconnect reconfiguration via optical circuit switches and includes specialized SparseCores to accelerate embedding-based models. While both architectures aim to maximize training efficiency, they differ in their interconnect strategies and specialized hardware units. Lumos leverages these underlying hardware capabilities by providing a performance modeling toolkit that accurately captures the execution behaviors of LLMs on such high-performance systems, enabling precise estimation of how software configurations interact with these distinct hardware architectures.

**Large Language Models and Scaling**
The development of large language models (LLMs) has been driven by the Transformer architecture and the scaling laws governing model size and data. The foundational Transformer model [6] established attention mechanisms as the core component for sequence transduction, outperforming recurrent and convolutional networks. Subsequent work demonstrated that scaling these models enables unsupervised multitask learning capabilities [7], with models like PaLM [8] further validating the benefits of scaling to hundreds of billions of parameters on specialized hardware. These advancements have necessitated sophisticated training systems to manage the computational complexity. Lumos builds upon this context by providing a trace-driven performance model that characterizes the execution of these scaled LLMs, facilitating the optimization of training strategies for models of varying sizes and complexities.

**Distributed Training Systems and Parallelism**
Efficiently training LLMs at scale requires advanced distributed training systems and parallelism strategies to overcome memory and compute limitations. Early systems like Megatron-LM introduced model parallelism to train multi-billion parameter models [9, 10], which was later extended to 3D parallelism for training massive models like Megatron-Turing NLG 530B [11]. To automate and generalize these strategies, compiler-based approaches such as GSPMD [12] and XLA [13] utilize tensor distribution annotations and compilation optimizations to achieve high compute utilization. More recent systems, including Alpa [14] and Galvatron [15], automate inter- and intra-operator parallelism using decision trees and dynamic programming to determine optimal hybrid strategies. Furthermore, to handle extremely long sequences, techniques such as sequence parallelism [16], DeepSpeed-Ulysses [17], and Ring Attention [18] partition data along the sequence dimension and optimize communication patterns to enable near-infinite context lengths. Lumos complements these systems by providing a performance estimation toolkit that can predict the runtime behavior of these diverse parallelism configurations, allowing for efficient exploration of deployment strategies without the need for exhaustive empirical testing.

**Performance Profiling, Simulation, and Optimization**
Accurate performance modeling and optimization are critical for guiding system-level studies and improving training efficiency. Profiling tools such as NVProf [3], CUPTI [4], and PyTorch Kineto [5] provide low-level instrumentation to gather detailed performance metrics and execution traces from GPU applications. Building on these data sources, analytical and simulation frameworks have been developed to predict performance and co-design systems. For instance, Amped [19] proposes an analytical model for distributed Transformer training, while Calculon [20] offers a methodology for high-level co-design of systems and LLMs. Daydream [21] uses fine-grained dependency graphs to estimate the impact of optimizations, and Apollo [22] focuses on automatic operator fusion. Simulation frameworks like Astra-sim [23] and its extension Astra-sim 2.0 [24] enable software and hardware co-design exploration by modeling hierarchical networks and disaggregated systems. Additionally, dpro [25] provides a generic toolkit for diagnosing and optimizing distributed DNN training performance. Lumos distinguishes itself by offering a trace-driven approach that replays execution with high accuracy (3.3% average error) and estimates performance for new setups from existing traces, thereby facilitating efficient exploration of model and deployment configurations in production environments.
