## Related Work

**Bias and Fairness in Language Models**
The investigation of bias and fairness in natural language processing has evolved from critical surveys of normative reasoning [2] to comprehensive taxonomic reviews of large language models (LLMs) [3, 6]. Early empirical studies established the prevalence of gender bias in coreference resolution, introducing benchmarks such as WinoBias and Winogender schemas to quantify systematic disparities [11, 12], while subsequent work expanded these evaluations to large-scale datasets for coreference and machine translation [17] and open-ended generation tasks via the BOLD dataset [14]. More recent research has broadened the scope to include question answering [16], occupation stereotypes [15], and compositional evaluation frameworks like CEB [7]. Furthermore, the dynamics of bias have been examined during the training process [5] and across model adaptation stages, confirming that intrinsic biases persist in prompted LLMs [4]. While these works provide robust metrics for discrete accuracy-based fairness, they generally overlook the probabilistic confidence associated with predictions. Our work extends this line of research by introducing an uncertainty-aware perspective, arguing that fairness must account for the model's certainty in its decisions, not just their correctness.

**Uncertainty Quantification in Language Models**
Uncertainty quantification (UQ) in NLP has been extensively reviewed, categorizing sources into input, system, and output types [18, 19]. Specific methods for LLMs include semantic entropy to account for linguistic invariances [26], the Shifting Attention to Relevance (SAR) heuristic for free-form generation [27], and specialized tools like LM-polygraph [21, 30]. Recent exploratory studies have applied UQ to detect hallucinations and assess prediction risks [20], while others have investigated the self-calibration capabilities of larger models [28]. However, a critical limitation in current UQ evaluation is the spurious interaction with response length, which can distort results [31]. Additionally, benchmarking efforts have revealed that higher accuracy does not necessarily correlate with higher certainty [8]. Unlike these studies, which primarily focus on reliability or hallucination detection, our paper leverages UQ specifically as a lens for fairness evaluation, demonstrating that uncertainty disparities between groups reveal biases that accuracy-based metrics miss.

**General Language Model Evaluation and Benchmarks**
Holistic evaluation frameworks have become essential for understanding the multifaceted capabilities of LLMs. The HELM framework assesses models across diverse scenarios using multiple metrics to expose trade-offs beyond simple accuracy [1], while BIG-bench quantifies capabilities against human experts across hundreds of tasks [13]. The history of the Winograd Schema Challenge also highlights the importance of surrogate tasks in evaluating linguistic understanding [10]. These benchmarks have set the standard for comprehensive model assessment. However, they typically treat fairness as a separate, accuracy-centric metric. Our contribution complements these general benchmarks by introducing a specialized fairness benchmark that integrates uncertainty, providing a more granular view of model behavior that is reflective of internal decision-making biases.

**Foundations of Uncertainty and Fairness in Machine Learning**
The theoretical intersection of uncertainty and fairness in machine learning has gained traction, with foundational work exploring the distinction between epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty [22, 24] and methods like dropout as Bayesian approximation [25] or Prior Networks for distributional uncertainty [29]. In the context of fairness, recent studies have proposed using uncertainty as a direct fairness measure [33] and developed predictive modeling techniques that incorporate uncertainty awareness for fair decisions [34]. Other works have examined the trade-offs, such as leveraging aleatoric uncertainty to balance fairness and utility [35] or investigating whether fairness constraints degrade the reliability of uncertainty estimation [32]. While these studies establish the theoretical viability of linking uncertainty and fairness, they are largely confined to traditional classification tasks. Our work bridges this gap by applying these foundational principles to the specific context of LLMs, proposing UCerF as a practical metric that captures the implicit impact of model confidence on fairness.
