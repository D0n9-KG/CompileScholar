## Related Work

**Active Learning Strategies and Uncertainty Metrics**
Active learning (AL) aims to minimize annotation costs by strategically selecting the most informative unlabeled instances for labeling, a paradigm extensively reviewed in comprehensive synthesis lectures [4]. Early foundational work established uncertainty sampling as a core strategy, demonstrating its efficacy in reducing manual labeling requirements for text classification tasks [2]. Subsequent research expanded these principles to complex visual data, applying various uncertainty metrics to CNN-based multi-class image classification to further reduce training example needs [3]. To address the limitations of single-metric approaches, heterogeneous uncertainty sampling methods have been proposed to improve supervised learning performance by leveraging diverse uncertainty signals [1]. Beyond simple uncertainty estimation, structural and distributional approaches have emerged, such as active instance sampling via matrix partitioning [8] and strategies utilizing pre-clustering to identify informative samples [9]. More recent advancements focus on efficiency and robustness in deep learning contexts; for instance, core-set approaches formulate AL for CNNs as a subset selection problem to maintain competitive performance [10], while task-agnostic methods attach loss prediction modules to identify data likely to cause incorrect predictions [11]. Additionally, progressive distribution alignment techniques employ agreement and discrepancy selection to refine model boundaries [12]. While these methods significantly enhance standalone AL efficiency, they typically assume a centralized data setting and do not account for the distributed, privacy-preserving constraints inherent in federated environments, which motivates the development of client heterogeneity-aware selection mechanisms.

**Bayesian Uncertainty in Neural Networks**
Quantifying model uncertainty is critical for identifying epistemic variations in deep learning models. Bayesian approaches offer a principled framework for this task, with "Bayes by Backprop" introducing an algorithm to learn probability distributions over neural network weights, thereby regularizing training and quantifying weight uncertainty [5]. To address the computational prohibitive nature of exact Bayesian inference, dropout has been recast as an approximate Bayesian inference method, enabling the modeling of model uncertainty in deep networks without sacrificing computational efficiency [6]. These Bayesian uncertainty estimation techniques provide the theoretical foundation for identifying samples with high epistemic variation, which is essential for our proposed CHASe framework to track inference inconsistencies and calibrate decision boundaries in a federated setting.

**Federated Learning Fundamentals and Robustness**
Federated Learning (FL) enables collaborative model training across decentralized devices while preserving data privacy. The foundational work on Federated Optimization introduced algorithms for training centralized models from unevenly distributed data with communication efficiency as a primary constraint [13]. This was further advanced by the advocacy for communication-efficient learning of deep networks from decentralized data, which handles non-IID data by aggregating locally computed updates from mobile devices [14]. However, the performance of FL is often compromised by data heterogeneity. To mitigate this, distribution-regularized FL techniques have been developed to address the challenges of non-IID data distributions [18]. Furthermore, experimental analyses have investigated the robustness of FL to data heterogeneity and biased sampling in edge computing, demonstrating that FL remains robust when local training iterations and communication frequency are appropriately tuned [19]. While these works establish the robustness and efficiency of FL, they primarily focus on model aggregation and optimization rather than the strategic selection of data for annotation, a gap that Federated Active Learning seeks to fill.

**Federated Active Learning**
Federated Active Learning (FAL) integrates the benefits of AL and FL to reduce annotation costs in distributed settings. Early applications of FAL have been demonstrated in specific domains, such as waste and natural disaster image classification [15] and emergency response systems like FLARE, which uses naming-assisted federated active learning for rapid model adaptation [16]. Generalized F-AL methods have been proposed where clients collaboratively select informative instances to reduce annotation workload and improve global model performance in image classification [17]. To address the specific challenges of non-IID data and client heterogeneity, Knowledge-Aware Federated Active Learning (KAFAL) employs knowledge-specialized active sampling and compensatory federated updates to efficiently learn global models with limited budgets [20]. Additionally, the impact of inter-class diversity has been analyzed, leading to the LoGo strategy, which integrates global and local query selectors to robustly handle local heterogeneity and global imbalance [21]. On the client selection front, Active Federated Learning has been introduced as a strategy to probabilistically choose clients based on current model and local data, significantly reducing communication costs [22]. Despite these advancements, existing FAL methods often fail to fully account for the fluctuations in global and local model parameters caused by client heterogeneity. Our work, CHASe, addresses this by specifically targeting samples with high epistemic variations that oscillate around decision boundaries, thereby enhancing both the effectiveness and efficiency of data selection in heterogeneous federated settings.
