## Related Works

## Optimal Taxation Theory and Limitations

The theoretical foundation of optimal income taxation is rooted in the seminal work of Mirrlees (1971), which addressed the problem of asymmetric information where individual ability is private but earnings are observed by the government [1]. This framework seeks to maximize the social evaluation of individual utility, which depends on consumption and labor, by providing analytical solutions for second-best efficient tax systems in the presence of adverse selection [1]. While early results from Mirrlees were not closely connected to empirical tax studies, subsequent work by Diamond (1998) and Saez (2001) bridged the gap between the Mirrlees model and practical tax policy and empirical studies [1]. These developments are part of a broader lineage that includes models by Sadka (1976), Seade (1977), Akerlof (1978), Stiglitz (1982), and Piketty, Saez, and Stantcheva (2014) [1].

A significant limitation of these standard models is their treatment of taxpayer heterogeneity, particularly regarding preferences and multidimensional characteristics. Research indicates that preference heterogeneity affects the distribution of consumption relative to leisure, which in turn influences marginal tax rates [2]. Numerical simulations and empirical evidence demonstrate a link between this inter-individual variation and the quantitative measure of consumption, challenging the unproven intuition that preference heterogeneity simply reduces redistribution [2]. Furthermore, multidimensional heterogeneity introduces composition effects that create endogeneity in sufficient statistics used for policy prescriptions [3]. Ignoring these composition effects leads to an underestimation of optimal linear and nonlinear tax rates, with numerical simulations on U.S. data suggesting that optimal tax rates for high-income levels may be underestimated by 6 percentage points [3].

Traditional optimal taxation models also rely on assumptions of rational behavior that may not reflect real-world taxpayer decisions. Behavioral economics has emerged to explain why individual decisions and behaviors may not reflect their own preferences, integrating psychology to analyze human behavior in economic contexts [4]. This field highlights that traditional economic approaches assume decisions are made rationally to reflect best interests, whereas behavioral economics accounts for psychological aspects of decision-making [4]. Quantum Decision Theory (QDT) has been shown to enable modeling of the irrational and subjective aspects of decision making that are not accounted for by classical Cumulative Prospect Theory (CPT) [5]. The implications of behavioral economics for tax policy include a changed understanding of welfare consequences, the desirability of using the tax system for policy implementation, and the role of taxes in policy design [6].

To address these limitations, recent approaches have employed computational and machine learning methods. The AI Economist utilizes a two-level, deep reinforcement learning framework where agents and a social planner co-adapt to design economic policies [7][8]. This framework overcomes limitations of current economic methodology, such as the lack of counterfactual data and simplistic behavioral models, by learning rational agent behaviors that adapt to planner policies [8]. In complex, dynamic economies, the AI Economist improves utilitarian social welfare and the trade-off between equality and productivity over baselines, accounting for emergent tax-gaming strategies and labor specialization [7][8]. Additionally, computational evolutionary game models have been used to study fraud dynamics in consumption tax systems, with parameters calibrated using real-world data from VAT declarations in the Canary Islands region of Spain [9]. These models suggest that policies favoring social rewards for cooperators or alternative penalties for defectors can be effective, though their success depends on the distribution of audit probabilities for low and high transactions [9].

## Agent-Based Modeling in Macroeconomics

Agent-based modeling (ABM) has emerged as a critical methodology for macroeconomic analysis, particularly in addressing the limitations of traditional equilibrium models that assume homogeneous and optimizing agents [1]. Following the Great Financial Crisis, criticism intensified regarding the validity of these dominant assumptions, leading to an interdisciplinary exploration of ABM to capture complexity, heterogeneity, networks, and heuristics in economic dynamics [1]. A systematic comparison of various agent-based macroeconomic models reveals substantial similarities in structure and assumptions, identifying an emerging common core within the field [2]. The full potential of this approach lies in its modular nature, which allows for the creation of new models with heterogeneous interacting agents endowed with partial information and limited computational ability, thereby abandoning the constraints of rational expectations [3].

In simulating household behaviors, ABMs are designed to capture decision-making processes that traditional models often oversimplify. For instance, models have been developed to simulate land use change on household farms by integrating geographic information systems and socio-economic surveys to examine demographic and agricultural interactions [4]. Similarly, ABMs are used to assess the macroeconomic impacts of individual behavioral changes on carbon emissions, addressing the limited representation of household heterogeneity in standard energy-economics models [5]. These models incorporate social dynamics and individual actions to understand how households, as primary agents of change, influence green energy demand and investment in new technologies [5]. Furthermore, ABMs can simulate technology adoption in low-income regions by capturing social networks, individual beliefs, and rational decision-making behaviors, providing insights into how intervention strategies affect adoption patterns [6].

The application of ABM extends to analyzing macroeconomic dynamics, business cycles, and fiscal policy. Micro simulation models based on the stock-flow-consistent (SFC) approach explicitly model the accumulation of surpluses and deficits, allowing for bankruptcies as a mechanism for adjusting long-run balances when agents fail to adapt quickly enough to debt accumulation [7]. Other ABMs focus on the propagation of economic shocks through multi-layered supplier–customer relationships, revealing that household asset accumulation during demand shocks can expand future consumption capacity and suppress sales declines in non-final sectors [8]. In the context of fiscal policy, ABMs have been used to investigate the effects of tax, transfer, and spending configurations, identifying a "balanced budget emerging property" where aggregate activity scales such that a balanced budget emerges spontaneously [9]. Additionally, simulations exploring the expansionary austerity hypothesis suggest that while optimism in a small proportion of firms may lead to negative outcomes, these effects are insufficient to neutralize the negative macroeconomic impacts of cutting government spending [10].

Recent advancements integrate Large Language Models (LLMs) with ABM to enhance the simulation of strategic reasoning and social interactions. Surveys categorize LLM applications in this domain into societal simulation, economic simulation, game theory, and gaming [11]. In larger-scale simulations, such as those modeling virtual societies, the architectural focus shifts from agent-level protocols to organization-level structures [12]. LLMs have demonstrated adaptability and strategic thinking capabilities in economic scenarios, such as engaging effectively in auctions [11]. Moreover, LLM agents equipped with heterogeneous personas and theory of mind can reproduce human behaviors, such as third-party punishment in public-goods games [12]. Studies on AI agents have also provided evidence of behaviors consistent with self-interest and altruism, noting that AI agents shared endowments differently with humans, charities, and other AI agents [13]. These developments indicate that ABM frameworks are evolving to incorporate more nuanced representations of human behavior and decision-making, moving beyond simple heuristics to include complex social and strategic interactions [14][15].

## Large Language Models in Economic Simulation

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into economic and social simulations represents a significant shift from traditional agent-based modeling (ABM) and reinforcement learning approaches. While machine-learning-based economic simulation, such as the AI Economist framework, utilizes two-level deep reinforcement learning to train agents and social planners for optimal policy design, it remains limited by the need for precise reward definitions and environmental modeling [1][2]. In contrast, LLMs are increasingly viewed as implicit computational models of humans, or *homo silicus*, which can be endowed with preferences and information to explore behavior in scenarios via simulation [3]. This approach addresses the limitations of traditional ABM, which often struggles to faithfully capture the full complexity of human-driven behavior due to simplistic agent behaviors and computational constraints [4][5].

Recent surveys categorize LLM applications in strategic reasoning into societal simulation, economic simulation, game theory, and gaming, highlighting their potential to engage in complex interactions [2]. In economic simulations, LLM agents have demonstrated the ability to match human choices in behavioral-economics experiments, such as the Trust Game, and to recover predictions from social exchange theories [6]. Furthermore, LLMs can reproduce human third-party punishment in public-goods games when given heterogeneous personas and theory of mind, and they exhibit behaviors consistent with both self-interest and altruism in incentivized experiments [6][7]. These capabilities allow LLMs to function as effective proxies for human decision-making, enabling researchers to explore human-driven interactions in previously unimaginable ways through prompt engineering and persona configuration [4][2].

To enhance the realism and scalability of these simulations, researchers have developed frameworks that leverage census data to generate virtual populations with statistically truthful demographic characteristics, thereby bypassing privacy issues associated with real-world data [8]. The initialization of LLM-based agents typically involves system messages that configure persona settings, target alignment, and functionality declarations, allowing for the simulation of diverse behavioral traits [2]. For instance, the Generative Agents framework introduced cognitive architectures with memory, reflection, and planning to achieve more realistic social behaviors [6]. In specific domains, such as transportation, LLM-agent-based modeling frameworks aim to replicate the decision-making and interaction processes of human travelers, offering solutions to the behavioral realism limitations of existing microsimulations [9]. Similarly, in the context of electric vehicle usage, LLMs are used to simulate personalized decision-making processes by integrating statistical priors with natural language reasoning to account for psychological and environmental factors [10].

Despite these advancements, challenges remain in the application of LLMs to economic simulations. The logic behind agents’ decisions and behaviors remains challenging to fully comprehend, especially in complex gaming environments, and the absence of explicit explainability in LLMs hinders their application in social sciences [11][12]. Additionally, LLMs may inherit biases from their training data, which poses risks to the validity of simulation outcomes [2]. To address the trade-off between simulation scale and individual agent expressiveness, methodologies such as LLM archetypes have been proposed to efficiently integrate LLMs into ABMs, enabling the simulation of millions of adaptive agents while maintaining nuanced behavior [5]. Benchmarks like GLEE have been developed to standardize research on language-based economic games, evaluating agents' performance in terms of self-gain, efficiency, and fairness [13]. Furthermore, LLMs are being used to simulate field experiments, with prompting strategies allowing for the prediction of main conclusions or the simulation of participant response distributions, although performance varies across topics such as gender differences and social norms [14].

The broader implications of LLMs in economics extend to macroeconomic forecasting and policy analysis. Generative AI is transforming risk assessment and forecasting in the financial sector, while AI-derived scores from corporate transcripts can predict GDP growth and employment trends [15][16]. In the context of international relations, LLM-powered systems are used to simulate historical conflicts and assess escalation risks in wargame scenarios [2][17]. However, the integration of LLM-based agents into real-world data-driven systems raises questions about economic stability and social wellbeing, particularly regarding job displacement and the need for government intervention [18]. Methodological reviews indicate that Agent-Based Simulation, Reinforcement Learning, and other approaches are being employed to predict the economic and social impacts of generative AI adoption, each with distinct strengths and weaknesses in handling uncertainty and resource requirements [19].

## Integration of LLMs and ABM for Policy Design

The integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) with Agent-Based Modeling (ABM) represents an emerging frontier in policy design, aiming to overcome the behavioral realism limitations of traditional simulations. In transportation systems, LLM-based agents are proposed to replicate human decision-making and interaction processes, addressing the constraints of existing ABMs regarding behavioral realism and resource demand [1]. Similarly, in game-theoretic contexts, LLM agents equipped with heterogeneous personas and theory of mind can reproduce human third-party punishment behaviors in public-goods games [2]. The initialization of such LLM-based agents typically requires system messages for persona setting, target alignment, and functionality declaration [3]. However, these methodologies face challenges, as LLMs may inherit biases from training data, while Reinforcement Learning (RL) effectiveness depends on precise reward definitions and environmental modeling [3]. LLMs can also serve as world model simulators to learn complex environmental dynamics, acting as policy interpreters based on their knowledge and reasoning abilities [4]. In specific applications, LLMs have been used to derive rewards for training compact policies in long-horizon tasks, such as in Minecraft [2], and to augment policy update functions in reinforcement learning frameworks [4].

In the domain of economic policy and taxation, prior work has primarily focused on deep reinforcement learning (DRL) and multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) rather than LLMs. The AI Economist framework utilizes a two-level, deep RL approach where agents and a social planner co-adapt to design tax policies [5][6]. This framework uses structured curriculum learning to stabilize the coadaptive learning problem and has been validated in taxation domains, recovering optimal tax policies in one-step economies and improving the equality-productivity trade-off in spatiotemporal economies [5][6]. An earlier iteration of this work demonstrated that AI-driven tax policies could improve the trade-off between equality and productivity by 16% over baselines, including the Saez tax framework, by learning from observational data without explicit economic modeling assumptions [7]. TaxAI extends this by providing a MARL environment for dynamic games involving households, government, firms, and financial intermediaries, benchmarking MARL methods against traditional economic approaches [8]. Other studies have explored multi-agent simulators with RL agents of various types, such as heterogeneous households and governments, to examine the impact of tax credits on myopic decision-making and liquidity [9].

Traditional ABM approaches in taxation have also been developed to address the limitations of utility functions and taxpayer homogeneity. Agent-based models have been used to simulate individual agents in tax evasion scenarios, accommodating a wider variety of intrinsic and extrinsic variables to better characterize taxpayer behaviors [10]. In the context of carbon taxes, ABMs have been employed to simulate electricity markets, determining optimal tax rates to drive investment in renewable energy [11]. Furthermore, agent-based computable models have been used to study tax policy and entrepreneurial entry with information asymmetry, showing how learning affects the optimality of second-best tax and subsidy policies [12]. While deep RL has been applied to solve dynamic stochastic general equilibrium models and model bounded rationality in monetary and fiscal policy interactions [13], and to determine optimal saving strategies for heterogeneous profiles [14], the specific integration of LLMs for adaptive tax policy optimization remains a novel contribution. The use of AI in public sector tax policy optimization has also been critiqued for potentially excluding competing political values and undermining human self-determination [15], highlighting the need for ethical considerations in the design of such systems [16].

## Equity-Efficiency Trade-offs in Taxation

The relationship between equity and efficiency in taxation is a central theme in economic literature, with empirical evidence indicating that a trade-off exists for most countries, although the magnitude and nature of this trade-off vary significantly across different institutional contexts and policy designs. Cross-country analyses of OECD nations refute the notion that income and inequality are negatively associated without cost, demonstrating instead that the best-practice frontier displays a clear trade-off where a larger tax burden is associated with lower efficiency and greater equity [1]. This finding holds true over the sample period from 1980 to 2010, with no evidence that the trade-off has become steeper, though country positions differ, with some consistently operating near the frontier while others remain well inside the opportunity set [1]. In contrast, other studies suggest that specific structural reforms can improve this trade-off without sacrificing equality, such as selective tax relief for elastic secondary earners and low-skilled workers in the Dutch tax-benefit system, which raised employment without worsening equity [2]. Conversely, a flat income tax structure, potentially combined with a negative income tax, was found to worsen the equity-efficiency trade-off in the same Dutch context [2].

Methodological approaches to optimizing this trade-off have evolved from static general equilibrium models to dynamic and evolutionary algorithms. A multi-objective evolutionary algorithm applied to the Italian income tax reform demonstrated that it is possible to determine tax structures that maximize redistributive effects, measured by the Reynolds–Smolensky index, while minimizing inefficiency, defined by the weighted average of effective marginal tax rates [3]. This approach also accounted for revenue guarantees and minimized the share of losing taxpayers, supporting policymaker decisions in complex real-world situations [3]. Similarly, in Brazil, a static behavioral microsimulation study of a personalized value-added tax reform showed that introducing a single general tax rate with increased rates for specific goods and refunds for the poorest families resulted in a tax system superior to the current one in both efficiency and equity [4]. However, not all fiscal policies yield positive results; in Indonesia, panel data regression models revealed that the redistribution value of district/city taxes was negative, indicating that the applicable tax system tended to widen income inequality rather than reduce it [5]. Furthermore, in Morocco, a Computable General Equilibrium Model analysis found that while direct tax reductions significantly increased disposable income for poor households, other policies such as exempting agricultural commodities from indirect taxes did not have a positive effect on their income or consumption [6].

The effectiveness of tax systems is also influenced by administrative structures and taxpayer behavior. The creation of more autonomous tax administrations can have unintended consequences, such as dwarfing the administration's incentive to collect taxes in developing countries, which contributes to low tax revenue collections [7]. Additionally, the design of audit probabilities plays a crucial role in compliance and efficiency; increasing the subjective audit probability for low transactions is more efficient than doing so for high transactions, and policies favoring social rewards for cooperators depend heavily on the distribution of these audit probabilities [8]. These models are often calibrated using real-world data, such as VAT declarations from the Canary Islands region of Spain, to ensure relevance to actual economic conditions [8]. The responsiveness of taxpayers to tax rate changes, measured by the elasticity of taxable income, is a fundamental factor in policy formulation, with estimates varying significantly based on how secular income trends are controlled, such as an elasticity of 0.289 without income control in Indonesian data [9]. Finally, broader institutional factors, including democratic accountability and investment portfolios, also interact with fiscal policy to influence poverty and income inequality, with democratic accountability shown to reduce income inequality in Pakistan [10].
