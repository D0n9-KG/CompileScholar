## Related Works

## Multimodal Large Language Models and Nonverbal Integration

Large language models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong capabilities in language understanding, knowledge generalization, and conversational dialogue by scaling model capacity and training on massive, diverse text corpora [1]. The sudden popularity of LLMs arises from their usefulness in supporting a wide range of applications, including text summarization, text-to-image and text-to-video generation, conversational search, and machine translation [2]. To extend these capabilities, LLMs have been evolved into multi-modal large language models (MM-LLMs) that handle image, video, and audio information in addition to text [2]. For instance, the multimodal Flamingo 80B model achieved new state-of-the-art results on six visual question answering benchmarks [3]. Architectures such as Kosmos-1 utilize Transformer-based causal language modeling to process these diverse inputs [2]. Comprehensive surveys indicate that MLLMs integrate various data types to enable complex AI systems for cross-modal understanding and generation, covering training methods, architectural components, and practical applications [4]. However, for embodied tasks where robots interact with complex environments, text-only LLMs often face challenges due to a lack of compatibility with robotic visual perception [5].

Despite these advancements, existing models struggle to effectively incorporate nonverbal cues such as gestures, facial expressions, and body language, which are integral to human interaction [6]. Interpersonal spoken communication involves not only speech but also non-verbal cues used to express feelings and provide feedback, carrying information about physical and psychological states [6]. A significant barrier to integrating these cues is that collecting large-scale multimodal behavioral video datasets is extremely expensive in terms of time and money, whereas text data is abundantly available via the web [7]. Consequently, pre-trained large language models cannot be directly applied to multimodal behavior understanding tasks, such as video sentiment or humor detection, unless non-verbal features are integrated with language [7]. One approach to mitigate this complexity is TextMI, which converts acoustic and visual information into textual descriptions and concatenates them with spoken text to feed into a pre-trained BERT model [7]. This method significantly reduces model complexity and adds interpretability for downstream tasks like sentiment, humor, and sarcasm detection [7].

Recent efforts have focused on specific nonverbal modalities, such as body language and facial expressions. The BQA dataset was proposed to validate whether Video Large Language Models (VideoLLMs) can correctly interpret emotions from short clips of body language, revealing that understanding body language is challenging and that some models produce biased answers based on the age group and ethnicity of individuals in the video [8]. Similarly, the MERCI dataset addresses the shortage of multimodal information from human-robot interaction dialogues by recording rich embodied interaction data, including facial expression recognition and sentiment analysis, to generate contextually appropriate responses [9]. In the domain of emotion recognition, multi-modal frameworks have been proposed to extract features from facial expressions, speech, and EEG signals, with studies demonstrating that integrating these modalities can improve recognition accuracy [10][11]. Facial emotion recognition (FER) remains a key technology in affective computing, analyzing expressions using a person’s images [12]. Furthermore, research into predicting subjective impressions of interlocutors focuses on the functional aspects of head movement and facial expressions, such as rhythm, back channeling, and emotional expressions, to define functional features for intermodal and intramodal analysis [13].

The integration of facial and bodily expressions is a complex process, as nonverbal emotional signals from the face and body are normally seen together and require specific mechanisms for integration [14]. Studies show that when face and body convey conflicting emotional information, judgment of facial expression is hampered and becomes biased toward the emotion expressed by the body, with rapid neural mechanisms sensitive to the degree of agreement between these signals [15]. In adolescent offenders, research indicates a tendency to overvalue bodily and contextual signals in emotion recognition, leading to poorer facial-emotion categorization [16]. Additionally, emotion words can shape emotion percepts, as perceptual priming of emotional faces is disrupted when the accessibility of a relevant emotion word is temporarily reduced [17]. While LLM-based AI agents are increasingly used in multimodal dialogue, methods for recognizing emotions from the user's facial expressions to adjust interaction have not been extensively studied, though recent work examines whether LLMs can interact with users according to their emotional states by capturing facial expressions via camera [18]. The decoding of social signals from nonverbal cues plays a vital role in social interactions, and understanding the time course of integration of emotional signals from the face and body is crucial for developing immersive conversational experiences [14].

## Nonverbal Communication Datasets and Annotation Standards

Existing research on nonverbal communication datasets highlights significant challenges in scale, annotation granularity, and the integration of multimodal cues. Collecting large-scale multimodal behavioral video datasets is described as extremely expensive in terms of time and money, which contrasts with the availability of web-based text data [1]. To address the complexity of jointly modeling multiple modalities, some approaches investigate converting acoustic and visual information into textual descriptions to integrate non-verbal features with language in pre-trained models [1]. In the domain of facial expression recognition, surveys indicate that having a clear overview of existing datasets is paramount for designing effective solutions, particularly for neural network-based training [2]. These surveys often focus on spontaneous and in-the-wild datasets, reflecting a research trend toward contexts where expressions are shown in a real context [2].

Several large-scale datasets have been developed to address specific aspects of nonverbal behavior. The DFME dataset constructs a dynamic spontaneous micro-expression database with the largest current scale, including 7,526 well-labeled videos induced by 671 participants and annotated by more than 20 annotators over three years [3]. This dataset aims to solve the problem of micro-expression data hunger and provides a new benchmark for automatic micro-expression recognition [3]. Similarly, the FEAFA dataset provides well-annotated data for facial expression analysis, with 99,356 frames manually labeled using a tool that quantifies FACS action units with floating-point numbers between 0 and 1 [4]. This level of detail addresses the limitation of previous datasets that were restricted to rough annotations such as presence or absence of action units [4]. In the context of sign language, the FePh dataset introduces an annotated sequenced facial expression dataset comprising over 3,000 facial images extracted from public television broadcasts [5]. Unlike many existing datasets, FePh provides sequenced semi-blurry facial images with different head poses and orientations, addressing the scarcity of annotated facial expression resources in sign language contexts [5].

Beyond facial expressions, other datasets focus on vocalizations and social interactions. The ReCANVo database contains over 7,000 non-speech vocalizations from minimally speaking individuals, labeled by function in real-world settings [6]. This represents the largest available dataset of nonverbal vocalizations from this specific population [6]. For social dynamics, the AMI meeting corpus has been used to derive time- and hour-based datasets for estimating social dominance by fusing audio and visual cues [7]. Research indicates that the combination of audio and visual cues is necessary to achieve the best performance in such dominance estimation tasks [7]. Additionally, the VRESEE dataset extends engagement analysis to 88 Egyptian students with 3,525 recorded videos, contributing to the understanding of student engagement in educational settings [8]. The UE-HRI dataset documents spontaneous human interactions with the robot Pepper, focusing on facial expressions and postural details related to engagement [8].

Annotation standards and temporal alignment remain critical areas of focus. Traditional sign language processing approaches have been constrained by gloss-based systems that are language-specific and inadequate for capturing the multidimensional nature of sign language [9]. To bridge this gap, systems like SignWiring propose a universal transcription notation to serve as an intermediary between visual-gestural modalities and text-based linguistic representations [9]. Temporal segmentation is also a key challenge, with approaches employing 3D convolutional neural networks to determine temporal boundaries between signs in continuous videos [10]. In facial analysis, quantified facial Temporal-expressiveness Dynamics (TED) leverage multimodal facial features to quantify expressiveness by incorporating static and dynamic information [11]. This quantification is essential for building automated affect modeling systems and has been evaluated on spontaneous pain datasets [11].

Surveys of the field emphasize the need for unified benchmarks and robust evaluation. MultiBench provides a systematic and unified large-scale benchmark spanning 15 datasets, 10 modalities, and 20 prediction tasks to accelerate progress in multimodal representation learning [12]. This benchmark addresses limited resources for studying generalization, complexity, and robustness to noisy modalities [12]. Similarly, surveys on non-verbal social human behavior forecasting highlight the need to unify social signals prediction and human motion forecasting, identifying shared challenges such as future stochasticity and context awareness [13]. These surveys provide overviews of audiovisual datasets featuring non-acted social interactions to promote further research in the field [13]. The integration of these diverse datasets and annotation standards is crucial for developing models that can effectively understand and generate nonverbal cues alongside text.

## Unified Frameworks for Multimodal Generation

Unified frameworks for multimodal generation increasingly rely on the Next Token Prediction (NTP) objective to integrate diverse data types within a single architecture [1]. This paradigm transforms multimodal information into tokens, allowing models to predict the next element given the context, thereby unifying understanding and generation tasks [1]. Recent surveys highlight that NTP has evolved from natural language processing into a versatile training objective for machine learning across various modalities [1]. In the domain of vision, autoregressive models are being developed to unify understanding and generation, with formal definitions of autoregression emphasizing their scalability advantages [2]. These vision foundation models often utilize specific tokenizers and backbones to handle visual data, aiming to overcome limitations in existing models [2].

A critical component of these unified systems is the representation of continuous data, such as images or audio, into discrete or vectorized forms. Vector Quantization (VQ) is essential for discretizing continuous representations in unsupervised learning, though it can suffer from representation collapse and low codebook utilization [3]. To address this, methods like SimVQ reparameterize code vectors through a learnable linear transformation layer, optimizing the entire linear space to prevent collapse and improve codebook usage across modalities [3]. Alternatively, some approaches avoid quantization entirely; for instance, NOVA reformulates video generation as non-quantized autoregressive modeling, maintaining causal properties for flexible in-context capabilities while leveraging bidirectional modeling within frames [4]. This approach demonstrates that high-efficiency video generation is possible without vector quantization, surpassing prior models in data efficiency and visual fidelity [4].

The integration of non-textual modalities into Large Language Models (LLMs) often involves aligning external representations with the model's embedding space. Vector-ICL explores extending in-context learning capabilities to continuous vectors from diverse domains by aligning input data with an LLM's embedding space through lightweight projectors [5]. This method allows LLMs to process and learn from projected vectors, often surpassing few-shot in-context learning and domain-specific models in tasks ranging from text reconstruction to fMRI decoding [5]. Similarly, LMFusion empowers pretrained text-only LLMs with multimodal generative capabilities by introducing parallel transformer modules for processing images with diffusion [6]. In LMFusion, modality-specific layers process data independently, while shared self-attention layers facilitate interactions across text and image features, preserving language capabilities while enhancing visual generation [6].

For specific nonverbal and embodied tasks, unified architectures have been proposed to handle multiple output types. UniTAB unifies text and box outputs for grounded vision-language modeling by representing both with a shared token sequence and using a special token to indicate alignments [7]. This approach provides a simpler solution with a single output head, outperforming state-of-the-art methods in grounding and captioning [7]. In the context of embodied reference, YouRefIt introduces a dataset and benchmarks for understanding how language and gesture cues affect reference understanding in physical scenes [8]. The study provides evidence that gestural cues are as critical as language cues in understanding embodied reference, highlighting the importance of multimodal integration [8]. Furthermore, the EVA-framework investigates the synthesis of visual speech and nonverbal behavior using hierarchical behavioral events and motion templates to simulate reactive communicative behavior [9].

Efficiency in autoregressive generation remains a challenge, with sequential token-by-token prediction leading to slow inference [10]. To mitigate this, ZipAR proposes a training-free parallel decoding framework that exploits spatial locality in images to decode tokens in parallel, reducing the number of forward passes required for generation [11]. Similarly, other methods develop parallel generation strategies that generate distant tokens with weak dependencies in parallel while maintaining sequential generation for strongly dependent local tokens, achieving significant speedups without modifying the architecture [10]. These advancements in parallelization are crucial for deploying unified multimodal models in real-time applications. Additionally, discrete codebooks serve as intermediate features to establish connections between text and other modalities, such as in Text2Avatar, which uses a codebook to enable feature disentanglement for 3D human avatar generation [12].

## Empirical Evaluation of Nonverbal Generation

Evaluating the generation of nonverbal cues requires metrics that assess both the human-likeness of the motion and its appropriateness relative to the accompanying speech or context. The GENEA Challenge 2022 established a benchmark for data-driven co-speech gesture generation, utilizing a standardized visualization pipeline and large-scale crowdsourced user studies to evaluate systems [1]. This evaluation framework specifically decoupled human-likeness from gesture appropriateness, addressing a previous challenge in the field where these factors were often conflated [1][2]. Results from this challenge indicated that some synthetic gesture conditions were rated as significantly more human-like than 3D human motion capture, highlighting the potential of generative models to surpass biological baselines in perceived naturalness [1][2].

Beyond gesture-specific benchmarks, broader evaluation practices for computer-generated nonverbal behavior have compared direct rating methods with questionnaire-based approaches. A 2024 study found that for evaluating gesturing motion, direct ratings of human-likeness and appropriateness are preferred over questionnaires [3]. However, when assessing subtle motion cues, such as those shown during listening, even direct rating methods yield less conclusive results, suggesting that evaluation tools must be tailored to the subtlety of the behavior being assessed [3]. In the context of multi-party interactions, computational models for generating nonverbal behaviors supporting interpersonal attitudes have been evaluated using protocols where subjects recognize the attitudes expressed through nonverbal behavior and turn-taking strategies [4]. This approach validates the effectiveness of nonverbal generation in conveying specific social intentions within group dynamics [4].

The integration of nonverbal cues with verbal content is critical for comprehensive evaluation, as nonverbal impressions often carry higher predictive value than verbal ones in social contexts. Research on job interview analytics demonstrated that nonverbal cues explain a significant portion of variance in impressions, with combined verbal and nonverbal cues providing only a marginal improvement over nonverbal cues alone [5]. Similarly, in social media contexts, automatic nonverbal behavioral cues have been shown to predict personality dimensions such as Extraversion and Openness, though verbal content often outperforms nonverbal cues for other traits [6]. These findings underscore the necessity of evaluating nonverbal generation in conjunction with text to ensure coherent multimodal output.

For conversational agents, evaluation metrics must extend beyond simple text similarity measures like BLEU and perplexity, which should be supplemented with task-specific or objective-specific metrics such as response diversity [7]. Intrinsic automatic metrics, while useful for scaling, often lack interpretability and correlation with human evaluation, necessitating a multifaceted approach [8]. Benchmarks like CharacterEval employ thirteen targeted metrics across four dimensions to evaluate role-playing conversational agents, constructed through rigorous human-led quality control and dialogue extraction [9]. Furthermore, topic-based metrics have been proposed to evaluate the ability of bots to sustain coherent conversations, showing correlation with human judgment in large-scale dialog analyses [10].

The absence of a unified framework for measuring biases and performance in multimodal generation remains a significant challenge, with evaluation datasets and metrics often scattered [11]. To address this, comprehensive benchmarks such as AIGCBench include multiple metrics spanning dimensions like control-video alignment, motion effects, temporal consistency, and video quality [12]. In the domain of affective computing, unified multimodal annotation systems incorporating valence and arousal spaces have been proposed to provide common ground-truth for non-verbal affective analysis [13]. These developments highlight the ongoing effort to standardize evaluation practices for nonverbal generation, ensuring that models like MARS can be validated against robust, multi-dimensional criteria that capture both the technical quality and the social appropriateness of generated cues [12][13].
