## Initial Adsorption and Surface Chemistry

The formation of a biomolecular corona on lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) is a rapid process, typically established within less than one minute of exposure to biological fluids [1]. This initial interaction is driven by the specific binding of plasma proteins to functional chemical groups on the lipid surface, where the affinity of each lipid species is influenced by the co-presence of other components [1]. Consequently, the lipid composition of the nanoparticle serves as a primary determinant of the resulting protein pattern profile [1]. The corona is not static but evolves over time, with its composition shifting as the particle circulates [1]. For instance, studies on AmBisome® indicate that the hard corona can be isolated from excess plasma proteins using techniques such as size exclusion chromatography and membrane ultrafiltration [1]. To accurately assess the composition, methods like the MWNSC correction are employed to account for protein size and evaluate the actual relative abundance of each protein species within the corona [1].

## In Vivo Dynamics and Flow Effects

The biological identity of LNPs, including their size, zeta-potential, and aggregation state, differs significantly between in vitro conditions and the in vivo environment [1]. The variety of protein species present in the in vivo corona is considerably larger than that observed in static in vitro assays [1]. Blood flow dynamics exert a tremendous impact on the composition of the hard corona, resulting in a profile that is largely different from that formed under static conditions [1]. This discrepancy makes direct correlation between in vitro data and in vivo outcomes challenging [1]. Furthermore, the evolution of the biocorona varies between species; for example, the temporal development of the corona differs between rodents and humans, which can significantly affect the biodistribution of the nanoparticles across different animal models [1]. Specific protein components also vary based on the environment, such as a drastic reduction in fibrinogen content observed in coronas formed in vivo compared to other conditions [1].

## Protein-Mediated Clearance and Circulation

The specific proteins adsorbed onto the LNP surface dictate their fate in the bloodstream. A corona enriched with complement and coagulation proteins promotes the uptake of particles by immune cells, leading to their removal from the circulation [1]. Conversely, lower levels of complement proteins are associated with prolonged circulation times for nanoparticles in the blood [1]. Apolipoproteins play a complex role in this process; they can act as dysopsonins, reducing the adsorption of typical opsonins such as complement proteins and immunoglobulins, thereby extending the circulation time of liposomes [1]. However, the adsorption of apolipoprotein E is a key factor in the limitation of traditional LNPs, which often accumulate in the liver due to their physiochemical similarity to very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) [2]. This hepatic accumulation is mediated by endogenous transport mechanisms involving lipoprotein receptors and apolipoproteins [3]. PEGylation is another strategy used to increase blood circulation time, control particle size, and promote accumulation in tumor tissues through passive targeting [2].

## Lipid Design and Tissue Tropism

The chemical structure of the lipids, particularly ionizable cationic lipids, is a dominant factor in predicting the biodistribution of LNPs [3]. Specific electrotopological surface properties, charge- and hydrophobicity-weighted surface areas, molecular topology, and structural motifs such as amides and alkyls are key drivers of extrahepatic accumulation [3]. A higher fraction of ionizable lipid, combined with lower fractions of PEGylated or polymer-conjugated lipids and sterols, tends to favor hepatic accumulation. To alter this default tropism, Selective Organ Targeting (SORT) technology incorporates supplemental lipid molecules into the formulation to achieve tissue-specific delivery [3]. For example, adding a permanently cationic lipid can shift protein expression predominantly to the lungs, while adding an ionizable amino lipid can increase delivery to the liver [2]. All SORT LNPs contain ionizable cationic lipids, which are essential for endosomal escape and efficacy [2]. The addition of different SORT molecules has been shown to alter tissue tropism, as demonstrated by changes in luciferase protein expression [2].

## Novel Lipid Engineering and Stability

Recent engineering efforts have focused on designing novel lipids to target specific cell types without the need for external targeting ligands. For instance, piperazine-containing lipids and adamantyl-containing phospholipids have been developed to preferentially deliver mRNA to immune cells in vivo [3]. Other formulations, such as those incorporating cationic cholesterol with a cationic helper lipid, have enhanced mRNA delivery to the heart and various lung cell types, including stem cell-like populations [3]. Additionally, new ionizable lipids have been synthesized to alter LNP tropism, favoring delivery to the lungs [3]. However, the chemical stability of these ionizable lipids is critical; electrophilic impurities derived from the ionizable cationic lipid component can react with mRNA, rendering it untranslatable and leading to a loss of protein expression [4].
